\section{Robotic platforms as active matter}
\label{sec:platforms}

An active-matter experiment requires more than a collection of moving
objects. We must identify the source of activity, the interactions
between agents, and the observables through which collective behavior
is established. Robotic platforms provide an unusual opportunity to
separate these ingredients. Their propulsion, sensing, communication,
and mechanical properties can often be changed independently. Yet,
this programmability does not automatically make a robot collective
a faithful realization of a physical theory. Control algorithms may
introduce interactions that are absent from the model, while
boundaries, actuator variability, and finite system size can
determine the observed state.

We organize the experimental evidence by the primary platform class
extracted for each study. The classification describes experimental
implementation rather than a hierarchy of physical mechanisms.
In particular, similar phenomena may arise in mechanically driven
particles and communicating mobile robots, although the microscopic
interactions are fundamentally different. Conversely, the same
robotic platform may support several collective states when its
control rules or environment are changed.

The evidence snapshot of October 17, 2026 contains 489 included
studies. The largest class comprises wheeled or legged swarm robots
(203 studies), followed by systems classified as other (87),
robotic micro- and nano-agents (76), vibration-driven robots (63),
modular or self-reconfigurable robots (23), feedback-controlled
active colloids (13), smarticles or granular robots (10), and
robotic metamaterials or active solids (10). Four studies have
no recorded platform class. The ordering used below follows
these frequencies, without implying that publication volume
establishes scientific importance.

Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy} relates the platform taxonomy to the
collective phenomena reported in the included studies. The
classification is multi-label on the phenomenon axis: a study
can contribute to several phenomena. Figure~\ref{fig:interaction}
shows the corresponding distribution of interaction modalities.
Together, these figures provide a map of which experimental
implementations have been used to investigate different physical
questions. They do not measure the strength of agreement with
theory. We assess that distinction explicitly in the text.

We evaluate each class using four connected questions.
Which physical quantities can be measured? Which collective
phenomena have been demonstrated? What aspects of the
platform support controlled comparisons with active-matter
theory? Finally, what do the extracted quality indicators
permit us to conclude? Throughout this discussion, a
quantitative theory-comparison label is distinguished from
a low-risk rating in the corresponding quality domain.
The former describes the reported type of agreement;
the latter evaluates the strength of the evidence for
that comparison. Neither establishes universality by itself.

\subsection{Wheeled or legged swarm robots}
\label{sec:platforms-wheeled}

Wheeled and legged robots provide the most direct connection
between programmable collective motion and classical
swarm-robotics experiments. They are also the largest
platform class in the review, comprising 203 studies.
Their principal advantage is that the microscopic rules
of motion can be specified explicitly. An individual
agent may estimate its neighbors' positions, exchange
messages, respond to local visual information, or follow
a prescribed interaction law. Hence, it is possible to
implement a collective model in hardware and examine
which predicted behavior survives physical realization.

This capability has supported a substantial experimental
literature on alignment, flocking, aggregation, and
collective coordination. Early systems investigated
rule-based interactions and the emergence of coordinated
motion from distributed decisions
\cite{Garnier2005aggregationbehaviou,Turgut2008modelingphasetrans,Turgut2008selforganizedflocka,Navarro2008anapproachtoflock,Miki2010asimplerulebased,Yamada2011circularmotiongene,Ercan2011swarmrobotflocking,Chen2013aflockingalgorithm,Arvin2014comparisonofdiffer,Linevich2016robophysicalstudyo}.
Other experiments examined collective decision-making,
adaptive coordination, distributed interaction rules,
and changes in group configuration
\cite{Cianci2007communicationinas,Christensen2008swarmorphscripta,Parker2010collectiveunarydec,Kernbach2011threecasesofconne,Ferrante2013aselfadaptivecomm,Valentini2015collectivedecision,Li2016turinglearningam,Bonnet2018closedloopinteract,Gaget2020branchedstructuref,Cambier2021culturalevolutiono}.

The dominant reported phenomena are polar order or flocking,
identified in 93 studies, and self-assembly or shape formation,
reported in 83. Clustering appears in 50 studies, while
42 report transport or locomotion. Synchronization, swarming
or vortex behavior, and chirality or rotation also occur.
These categories overlap: an experiment can simultaneously
measure alignment, the shape of an aggregate, and its
collective displacement.

The observable matters more than the visual impression
of the experiment. For flocking, suitable quantities
include polarization, relative orientation, and
velocity correlations. For aggregation, the natural
observables are cluster-size distributions, neighbor
counts, and spatial organization. Shape formation can
be characterized by distances, distortions, and
configuration statistics. These quantities permit
comparison between a prescribed microscopic rule and
the collective behavior actually produced.

Several included studies make this distinction explicit
through experimentally reported order parameters or
collective distributions. Examples include measurements
of polarization and angular momentum, aggregation
statistics, and spatial or temporal collective responses
\cite{Wu2024adversarialimitatio,Zhang2022collectivefissionb,Zhou2024anticipationpromote,Castro2025visualcollectivebe,Barone2024experimentalandnum,MarchPons2024honeybeelikecollec,Mijalkov2016engineeringsensoria,Li2022fieldmediatedlocom,Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc}.
These measurements connect the engineering implementation
to questions from active-matter physics. Yet, not all
observables are equally diagnostic. A final configuration
or convergence time may confirm successful control
without distinguishing between competing mechanisms
of collective organization.

The interaction inventory illustrates why this platform
class is both powerful and difficult to interpret.
Steric contact and communication each appear in
103 studies. Optical or other sensing occurs in 99,
and programmed rules in 98. Environment-mediated
interactions are reported in 37. As these labels
overlap, a robot collective may combine collision
avoidance, explicit information exchange, local
perception, and prescribed dynamics within one
experiment.

The distinction between physical and informational
interactions is essential. Steric contact provides
a direct mechanical coupling. By contrast,
communication implements a transfer of information
that subsequently changes motor commands. The
resulting trajectories may resemble those of
particles interacting through physical forces.
Nevertheless, the microscopic mechanisms are
not equivalent.

For example, a programmed alignment rule may
resemble a Vicsek-type interaction
\cite{Vicsek1995novel}.
However, the hardware implementation also contains
finite sensing ranges, processing delays, limited
turning rates, and potentially asymmetric perception.
A theory that neglects these effects may reproduce
the overall trajectory while missing the mechanisms
that determine stability and fluctuations.
Experiments with programmed interactions are thus
most valuable when the additional physical
constraints are themselves characterized.

One advantage of mobile robots is that these
constraints can often be varied deliberately.
Researchers can modify sensing, communication,
neighbor selection, or feedback while retaining
the same basic hardware. The included literature
contains experiments investigating distributed
cohesion, collective pursuit, indirect control,
and the influence of local information
\cite{Lee2022distributedcohesive,Tran2022frontierledswarmin,Yang2023collectivechaseem,Vega2023indirectswarmcontr,Vega2023simulatelessexpec,Vega2024agentbasedemulatio,Gao2023robustdistributedc,Baumann2024hybridflockforma,Zhou2024anticipationpromote,CetinaDenis2025flockingcontrolfor}.
These systems can therefore function as controlled
tests of interaction design. Yet, changes in
software may modify several effective physical
parameters simultaneously. Identifying the
mechanism responsible for a collective transition
still requires appropriately chosen observables
and parameter sweeps.

Control is predominantly local and rule-based.
Of the 203 studies, 167 use an on-board rules
architecture, 16 use a central controller,
12 use external feedback, six use a learned
policy, and two are classified as having no
control or passive rules. The prevalence of
on-board rules makes this class particularly
relevant to distributed models. It also means
that programmable interactions frequently
coexist with communication protocols or sensor
processing that are not represented explicitly
in simplified physical theories.

Collective size is a further strength, but also
a major limitation. The recorded maximum number
of agents is available for 194 studies.
The median maximum is seven agents, although
the largest recorded experiment reaches 3\,357.
Small ensembles are well suited to investigating
pairwise interactions, collision avoidance,
and the stability of programmed configurations.
They are less decisive for identifying
large-system scaling behavior.

A visually coherent flock containing a few
robots does not establish long-range order in
the thermodynamic sense. Similarly, a cluster
formed under a prescribed attraction rule
need not demonstrate motility-induced phase
separation. To test such an interpretation,
we require measurements of density dependence,
fluctuations, spatial correlations, or
finite-size behavior. The distinction becomes
particularly important when a collective state
is actively stabilized by the control algorithm.

Some studies provide more extensive statistical
or theoretical comparisons
\cite{Spears2004distributedphysics,Guzzi2014bioinspiredobstacle,Cazenille2016automatedcalibratio,Xiao2023perceptionofmotion,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc,Zhang2026emergentnonhermiti}.
Their extracted observables include quantities
such as aggregate distributions, order parameters,
mean-squared displacements, or frequency-dependent
response. The presence of these observables
supports a more informative confrontation with
theory than demonstration of a target formation
alone. Yet, the strength of the comparison
depends on whether the model is tested
quantitatively across conditions rather than
merely fitted to reproduce one experiment.

The extracted theory classifications reveal
this limitation directly. Agent-based simulation
is reported in 127 studies, while 54 have
no theoretical comparison recorded. Only ten
studies are classified as comparing with a
Vicsek-type framework, five with active
Brownian particle models, and 11 with kinetic
or hydrodynamic theory. The theory categories
are multi-label and therefore cannot be
added to obtain a unique study count.

Across the class, 33 studies report quantitative
agreement with a theoretical or computational
model, 117 qualitative agreement, and 54 no
agreement comparison. Thus, the capacity to
implement microscopic rules is much more
widespread than the practice of quantitatively
testing their collective predictions.

The risk-of-bias assessment reinforces this
observation. For quantitative theory comparison
(D6), 18 studies are rated low risk,
52 have some concerns, and 134 are rated
high risk. Hence, the number of studies
reporting quantitative agreement should not
be confused with the number providing
low-risk quantitative tests. This distinction
is especially relevant when the compared
model is an agent-based simulation that
resembles the implemented controller.

Reproducibility indicators are more favorable
but remain incomplete. In the replication
domain (D1), 62 studies are rated low risk,
136 have some concerns, and six are high risk.
A numerical count of independent runs is
recorded for 96 studies. Explicit code
availability is recorded for 25 studies,
and data availability for 14. These values
do not demonstrate that the remaining
experiments were never repeated or their
software was never shared. They identify
the extent to which the extracted records
document the corresponding practice.

Seven studies in this class were assessed
from abstracts only. Their conclusions must
therefore be interpreted with additional
caution where experimental repetition,
tracking accuracy, or theoretical agreement
cannot be established from the available
report.

\subsection{Other robotic platforms}
\label{sec:platforms-other}

The second-largest extracted category is
``other'', containing 87 studies. Its size
is itself informative. Robotic active
matter is not restricted to the familiar
distinction between wheeled swarms and
synthetic active particles. The included
literature also contains aerial systems,
specialized mechanical assemblies,
fluid-mediated platforms, and additional
experimental implementations that do not
fit cleanly into the predefined classes
\cite{Zhou2021synchronizedswimmin,Gravish2023coordinatedcollecti,Li2024memoryinducedspont,Lin2024decodingfrequencies,Zion2024amechanicalorigin,Schilling2019learningvisionbase,Brandsttter2022multiagentspatial,Beaver2022aconstraintdriven,Newbolt2019flowinteractionsbe,Newbolt2024flowinteractionsle,Zhou2022lateralcontactyiel,Fu2023tabletopplatformo}.

We should not interpret this residual class
as a single physical family. Its members
differ in actuation, geometry, interaction
mechanism, and the role of external control.
Consequently, aggregate statements about
the ``other'' category are descriptive
rather than mechanistic. A common label
in the extraction table does not establish
a common universality class.

Nevertheless, the category illustrates
several important extensions of the
experimental concept. Aerial collectives,
for example, permit coordinated motion
in three-dimensional space. Their control
and perception architectures can make
collective alignment measurable outside
a planar arena
\cite{Hauert2011reynoldsflockingin,Vsrhelyi2014outdoorflockingand,Schilling2021visionbaseddronef,Horyna2024fastswarmingofuav,Zhang2022agileformationcont,Amorim2024aminimalistic3dse,Iacone2024decentralizedaccele}.
Yet, their dynamics also involve flight
stabilization, navigation, and actuator
constraints. An observed flocking pattern
may therefore reflect both collective
interaction rules and the individual
vehicle controllers.

Other systems emphasize fluid-mediated,
mechanical, or externally driven
interactions. These experiments provide
routes toward studying collective
behavior without requiring conventional
robot-to-robot communication
\cite{Newbolt2019flowinteractionsbe,Newbolt2024flowinteractionsle,Zhou2021synchronizedswimmin,Zhou2021collectivesynchroni,Li2024memoryinducedspont,Lin2024decodingfrequencies,Zion2024amechanicalorigin,Boudet2025effectsofboundarie}.
In such systems, understanding the
environment is often inseparable from
understanding the interaction law.
This is useful for active-matter
physics, where boundary conditions
and environmental coupling may
qualitatively change collective states.

The extracted phenomenon labels
show a strong representation of
flocking and polar order, with
49 studies. Self-assembly or shape
formation occurs in 23, and transport
or locomotion in 20. Swarming or
vortex behavior, synchronization,
and other collective responses
also appear. These counts overlap,
as expected for multi-label
phenomenon extraction.

In contrast to the more homogeneous
wheeled or legged category, the
relevant observables vary greatly.
Depending on the system, an experiment
may characterize orientation,
collective displacement, synchronized
motion, configuration, or
interaction-mediated response.
This variety is scientifically
productive, but it reduces the
value of pooled descriptive
statistics. A polarization
measurement in an aerial swarm
does not necessarily probe the
same physical assumptions as a
synchronization measurement in
a mechanically coupled assembly.

The interaction modalities reflect
this diversity. Communication and
programmed rules are each recorded
in 32 studies. Optical or other
sensing appears in 23, steric
contact in 17, and
environment-mediated coupling
in 14. The modalities are not
mutually exclusive. They describe
how interactions are implemented,
rather than establishing whether
those interactions correspond to
a particular theoretical potential
or continuum constitutive law.

The extracted agreement ratings
show 14 studies with quantitative
comparison, 49 with qualitative
comparison, and 25 with no
comparison. Risk-of-bias ratings
in D6 are low for eight studies,
indicate some concerns for 30,
and are high for 50. Thus,
a large proportion of this
heterogeneous literature remains
primarily demonstrative or
qualitatively explanatory.

Replication documentation is also
limited. Nineteen studies are
rated low risk for D1, 65 have
some concerns, and four are high
risk. Independent-run counts are
recorded for 36 studies, explicit
code availability for 21, and
data availability for ten.
Nine studies were assessed
from abstracts alone.

The collective-size distribution
further limits generalization.
Agent maxima are reported for
80 studies, with a median of
six and a largest recorded
collective of 52. Hence,
the category contains many
small-ensemble experiments.
Such studies can establish
interactions and local collective
responses, but they provide
limited evidence for system-size
scaling without further
controlled comparisons.

We conclude that the residual
platform category contains
important experimental
innovations but does not support
one unified physical synthesis.
Its most useful role in the
present review is to expose
where the current taxonomy
fails to distinguish mechanisms.
This is not merely a
classification problem.
A useful future taxonomy
should relate platform
implementations to propulsion,
interaction symmetry,
information transfer,
conservation laws, and
environmental coupling.

\subsection{Robotic micro- and nano-agents}
\label{sec:platforms-micro-nano}

Robotic micro- and nano-agents offer a different experimental route
to collective active matter. At small length scales, viscous forces,
surface interactions, and externally imposed fields become central
to the dynamics. This changes both the interactions available to
the experimenter and the meaning of individual autonomy. A
microscopic robot need not carry a processor or communicate with
its neighbors. Instead, its motion may be programmed through
magnetic fields, acoustic excitation, optical actuation, or
particle geometry. The resulting collective behavior can then
be investigated through microscopy and particle tracking.

The included evidence contains 76 studies in this class.
Transport or locomotion is reported in seven studies, while
26 address self-assembly or shape formation. Swarming or
vortex behavior appears in six studies, chirality or rotation
in 13, and clustering in eight. These phenomena frequently
occur together. In particular, a microscopic assembly may
change its configuration while translating, rotating, or
responding to an externally imposed field.

The experimental literature includes programmable
microswimmer collectives, magnetically actuated assemblies,
responsive microstructures, and reconfigurable robotic
micro-agents
\cite{Sun2021swarmingmicrodrople,Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic,Pauer2021programmabledesign,MayorgaMartinez2023multimodaldrivenma,Yigit2019programmablecollect,Das2023optoelectronictraje,Rivas2023spatialpatterningo,McGovern2024multifunctionalchir,Feng2023weakionexchangeba,Sun2024multiplemagnetoopt,Cheng2025selfassemblyandco,Je2024spatiallyselective,Cao2024mofbasedmagneticm,Li2023swarmingresponsive,Liu2022bionicmorphological}.
These systems demonstrate how collective structures can
be manipulated through combinations of agent design,
driving fields, and environmental conditions.

The principal measurable quantities differ from those
most frequently used in macroscopic swarm robotics.
Microscopy enables measurement of individual trajectories,
relative positions, and collective configurations.
Depending on the experimental design, these measurements
can characterize transport, assembly kinetics,
rotational motion, or changes in spatial organization.
For collective active matter, the important question
is whether such observables distinguish interactions
between agents from responses to a common external drive.

This distinction is particularly important for
magnetic actuation. Magnetic interactions are recorded
in 59 studies, hydrodynamic interactions in 49,
and steric contact in 48. Environment-mediated
interactions occur in 31 studies. These categories
overlap because a magnetically actuated particle
may simultaneously interact through magnetic forces,
fluid flows, and physical contact.

The resulting dynamics can be difficult to attribute
to a single mechanism. For example, a rotating
magnetic field may drive individual particle motion
while also changing the forces between neighboring
particles. Collective rotation could then reflect
the imposed drive, interparticle interactions,
or their combination. A convincing mechanistic
interpretation requires observables that distinguish
these possibilities.

Programmable magnetic and optical systems illustrate
the opportunity for such experiments
\cite{Chen2020temporallightmodul,Chen2022programmablespatio,Xu2022collectivebehaviors,Liu2023swarmingmultifuncti,Chen2023nanorobotmediateds,Ussia2024magneticmicrorobot,Tran2025roboticmicrocapsule,Xia2025artificialintellige,Wang2026reconfigurableassem,Kim2026magneticmxenebased}.
Here, externally imposed signals can be changed while
the underlying particles remain physically similar.
This provides experimental control over driving
conditions and, in suitable systems, collective
configuration.

The extracted control architectures reflect this
distinction. External feedback is recorded in
49 studies, central control in 14, passive or
uncontrolled rules in 11, on-board rules in two,
and a learned policy in one. This distribution
contrasts sharply with wheeled or legged swarm
robots, where on-board rules dominate.

Experiments on collective reconfiguration,
self-organization, and controlled assembly
provide examples of the diversity of this class
\cite{Aubret2021metamachinesofplur,Gardi2022microrobotcollectiv,Yang2022emergentmicroroboti,Urso2023reconfigurableself,Goyal2025externallycontrolle,Gao2025softmagneticmicror,Alapan2019shapeencodeddynami,Aubret2018targetedassemblyan,Raghavendra2026programmablepersist,Ahmed2021bioinspiredacousto,Yan2024programmingstructur,Yigit2020cohesiveselforgani,Desgranges2023microswimmersunder}.
The diversity is scientifically useful because
collective phenomena can be studied with different
propulsion and interaction mechanisms. Nevertheless,
visual similarity between resulting assemblies
does not establish equivalence of their microscopic
dynamics.

Collective size is another distinctive property.
The maximum number of agents is recorded in only
32 of the 76 studies. Among these, the median
maximum is 11 agents. The largest recorded system
contains 500\,000 agents
\cite{Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic}.
This broad range reflects the difference between
individually resolved robotic assemblies and
microscopic populations that can contain very
large numbers of active constituents.

Consequently, we must distinguish particle
number from experimentally accessible information.
A large microscopic assembly may be suitable
for investigating spatial organization and
collective transport, but it does not necessarily
provide individually resolved trajectories.
Conversely, experiments with a small number
of microscopic robots may offer precise control
over interaction geometry without supporting
large-system scaling analysis.

The theory comparisons are comparatively diverse.
Agent-based simulation is recorded in 30 studies,
kinetic or hydrodynamic theory in 28,
and active Brownian particle models in three.
Nineteen studies have no theoretical framework
recorded. The presence of hydrodynamic models
reflects the importance of fluid-mediated
interactions in microscopic systems.

Across the class, 26 studies report quantitative
agreement, 33 qualitative agreement, and 18
no theory comparison. In the corresponding
risk-of-bias domain D6, 18 studies are rated
low risk, 23 have some concerns, and 36 are
high risk. Hence, quantitative comparison is
more prominent than in several macroscopic
platform classes, but the evidence is not
uniformly strong.

Replication and accessibility remain important
limitations. In D1, 12 studies are rated
low risk, 63 have some concerns, and two
are high risk. Independent experimental run
counts are recorded for 23 studies. Explicit
code availability is identified in four
studies and data availability in 11.

Two included studies in this class were
assessed from abstracts alone. Their
contribution to claims requiring detailed
experimental characterization is therefore
less secure than that of studies evaluated
from full texts.

\subsection{Vibration-driven robots}
\label{sec:platforms-vibration}

Vibration-driven robots are among the closest
macroscopic analogues of classical dry active
matter. Their propulsion arises from the
conversion of mechanical vibration into
directed or fluctuating motion through
contact with a substrate. Unlike programmable
wheeled robots, they often require no explicit
neighbor sensing or communication.
Collective behavior can emerge directly
from steric interactions, friction, geometry,
and confinement.

The included evidence contains 63 studies.
Transport or locomotion appears in 32,
boundary accumulation in 28,
self-assembly or shape formation in 25,
clustering in 24, and chirality or rotation
in 24. Jamming or crystallization is
reported in seven studies, while four
address active-solid or odd-elastic
phenomena. Several experiments therefore
connect individual motility to spatial
organization and mechanical response.

The literature includes studies of vibrating
robots, mechanically driven active particles,
collective aggregation, and boundary-mediated
dynamics
\cite{Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Welch2019activereorientation,Deblais2019howtoforceanarmy,Xi2024beamsbotsandbey,Balda2022playingwithactive,Xi2024emergentintelligenc,Mohanty2025multimodalmotionan,GuzmnLastra2026emergenceofbeating,Edwards2026robusttopologically,Musacchio2026activeembracemente,Hoek2026pistonlikeinformat,Smith2026fromswarmtoindivi}.
These studies illustrate the value of
mechanical actuation as an experimental
alternative to software-defined interactions.

The most striking characteristic is the
prevalence of physical contact. Steric
interactions are reported in 60 of the
63 studies. Environment-mediated
interactions appear in 28 and magnetic
interactions in nine. Communication and
programmed rules are uncommon.

The resulting collective phenomena can
therefore be studied without assuming
that each agent estimates or responds
explicitly to neighboring states.
A collision changes translational and
rotational motion through physical
coupling. Frictional interactions can
also generate effective alignment
or persistent collective displacement
without an imposed flocking algorithm.

This difference has an important
consequence for theory. In a programmed
robot swarm, agreement with an
agent-based model may partly reflect
the fact that the model has been
implemented in the controller.
For mechanically driven robots,
the collective interaction law
typically emerges from the physical
system itself. A successful theoretical
prediction must therefore account
for measured motion that is not
directly specified by software.

Experiments on rotating robots,
collision-mediated motion,
active clustering, and mechanically
induced collective dynamics
illustrate this physical approach
\cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Antonov2025selfsustainedfrict,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,MuozObreque2026dynamicsofmagnetic,Novkoski2026graspionanopenso,Xi2024emergentbehaviorso,Casiulis2025ageometricconditio,Tian2018flowratebehavioran,Rozenblit2020diffusivedynamicsa,Porvatov2021optimizingselfrota}.
The experimental question is not
merely whether particles move
collectively, but how their
propulsion and collisions generate
the observed state.

The extracted observables span
individual trajectories, transport,
collective order, spatial structure,
and confinement-dependent response.
Boundary accumulation is particularly
prominent. It provides a useful
test because active particles can
respond to boundaries differently
from passive equilibrium systems
\cite{Bechinger2016active}.

Yet, the interpretation requires
care. Wall accumulation can result
from persistent propulsion,
geometric constraints, changes
in substrate contact, or direct
particle-wall interactions.
Without independent characterization
of these effects, agreement with
a simple active-particle model
may not identify a unique
mechanism.

Experiments addressing confinement,
diffusive dynamics, effective
temperature, and transport
provide relevant evidence
\cite{Deblais2018boundariescontrolc,Boudet2022effectivetemperatur,Engbring2023anonlinearfluctuat,Rozenblit2025trafficjamformatio,Musacchio2026fluidizationinduced,Wagner2026geometryinducedtra,Escobar2025effectofparticlec,VidalesHernndez2026onthemotionofsel}.
Such measurements are particularly
useful when they connect changes
in boundary conditions or driving
parameters to quantitative
collective observables.

Control architectures reflect
the mechanically driven character
of the class. Forty-one studies
are classified as using no control
or passive rules, 19 use on-board
rules, and three use external
feedback. This distribution
indicates that many experiments
investigate collective behavior
without programmable neighbor
interactions.

Collective sizes are generally
larger than in the wheeled
or legged category. Maximum
agent counts are available for
57 studies, with a median of
30 and a largest recorded
collective of 1\,141. The
distribution includes experiments
with many tens or hundreds
of interacting agents
\cite{Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Boudet2021fromcollectionsof,Zion2023morphologicalcomput,Giomi2013swarmingswirlinga}.
This scale is advantageous for
investigating spatial distributions,
density dependence, and collective
fluctuations.

Vibration-driven robots exhibit
a relatively substantial connection
to active-matter theory.
Agent-based simulations appear in
32 studies, active Brownian particle
models in 18, and kinetic or
hydrodynamic theory in 14.
Other statistical-mechanical
frameworks also occur.

Twenty-eight studies report
quantitative agreement, 21
qualitative agreement, and 14
no comparison. In D6,
20 studies are rated low risk,
22 have some concerns, and
21 are high risk. This profile
indicates a comparatively
strong emphasis on quantitative
physics, although it remains
far from universal.

The active Brownian particle
framework is a particularly
natural reference because it
separates persistent self-propulsion
from translational and rotational
fluctuations
\cite{Bechinger2016active}.
Yet, real vibration-driven
robots may violate assumptions
about constant speed, isotropic
noise, or memoryless reorientation.
Such deviations should be treated
as measurable physical effects,
not simply experimental imperfections
to be ignored.

Studies investigating dynamics,
fluctuations, and collective
mechanical behavior
\cite{Dmitriev2021statisticalcorrelat,Noirhomme2024brainbotsassmarta,Li2021programmingactivec,Siebers2023exploitingcompositi,Son2024emergentfunctional,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,Mammadli2026physicsinformeddig,Titov2025differentialequatio,Bashkova2026differentialequatio,Lvay2026collectivedynamics}
provide opportunities to compare
simplified stochastic descriptions
with experimentally observed motion.
The strength of such a comparison
depends on whether the relevant
noise statistics, propulsion
properties, and interaction
parameters are measured.

Replication ratings are mixed.
Seventeen studies are rated
low risk for D1, 43 have
some concerns, and three
are high risk. Independent-run
counts are recorded for
23 studies. Explicit code
availability is identified
in 13 studies and data
availability in 11.

Five included records were
assessed from abstracts only.
Their evidence should be
treated cautiously where
the reported collective
phenomenon requires detailed
information about independent
repetitions or the
experimental apparatus.

\subsection{Modular or self-reconfigurable robots}
\label{sec:platforms-modular}

Modular and self-reconfigurable robots introduce
an additional physical degree of freedom:
the collective can change its own connectivity
or geometry. While a conventional robot swarm
consists of individually mobile agents,
a modular assembly can form larger structures
whose mechanical properties and locomotion
emerge from interactions between components.

This makes modular robots particularly relevant
to the physics of active assemblies.
The individual units may contain actuators,
sensors, or programmed responses, while
their collective behavior also depends
on contact, attachment, geometry, and
constraints. Hence, the experiment can
probe how local activity produces
large-scale shape and motion.

The evidence contains 23 studies in this
class. Transport or locomotion appears
in 19 studies, and self-assembly or
shape formation in 18. Synchronization
is reported in seven and chirality
or rotation in five. Two studies
address active-solid or
odd-elastic phenomena.

Experimental realizations include
particle robots, soft modular
systems, shape-changing assemblies,
and magnetically or mechanically
coupled modules
\cite{Li2019particleroboticsba,Yang2024selfamputatingand,Hu2026diatominspired1di,Ma2024smarticle20desig,Yi2025reconfigurablerobot,Rogowski2026coordinatedselfass,Zhao2024snailinspiredrobot,Zhang2026anisotropicshapesh,Bhattacharjee2026collectiveassembly,Oliveri2021continuouslearning,Drew2021acousticcommunicati}.
These experiments shift attention
from the coordination of independent
trajectories toward the collective
mechanics of changing structures.

The distinction matters because
the collective configuration
affects the available motion.
A change in connectivity may
alter how actuator forces are
transmitted through the assembly.
Conversely, the resulting
mechanical response can influence
which configurations remain
stable or accessible.

The primary observables are
therefore often geometric and
kinematic. They include assembly
configuration, collective
displacement, rotational response,
relative module motion, and
changes in shape. Such
measurements connect directly
to active-matter questions
when they reveal collective
effects that cannot be reduced
to the movement of an isolated
module.

Steric contact is recorded
in all 23 studies. Magnetic
interactions occur in 12,
environment-mediated coupling
in six, and communication
and programmed rules in
five each. This interaction
profile differs from that
of conventional mobile swarms.
Mechanical coupling is a
central component rather
than an incidental collision.

Several experiments investigate
how collective structures can
be assembled, transformed,
or coordinated
\cite{Kaeser2025individualandcolle,Smith2025loopymovementseme,Rogowski2020magneticallyprogram,Ding2025transformablemodula,Ceron2021softroboticoscilla,Zhao2022softlatticemodules,Wang2026oscillatingeelrobo,Miyashita2008howmorphologyaffec,Paulos2015automatedselfassem,Bhattacharjee2026closedloopselfass,Ma2026crosslinkcollectiv,ICHIKAWA2004emergentbehaviorso}.
Their relevance to active matter
lies in the interaction between
local actuation and collective
structure, rather than in
the engineering success of
a reconfiguration task alone.

Most experimental collectives
in this class are relatively
small. Maximum agent counts
are recorded for 22 studies,
with a median of eight
and a largest reported
collective of 36.
These systems are well suited
to detailed studies of
connectivity and mechanical
interaction. They provide
less direct access to
large-system scaling behavior.

Control architectures are
also heterogeneous.
Eleven studies use on-board
rules, seven central control,
three external feedback,
and two no control or
passive rules. Thus,
mechanical reconfiguration
may result from local
programmed activity,
externally coordinated
motion, or passive coupling.

Nine studies compare with
agent-based simulation,
while six have no theory
comparison recorded.
Quantitative agreement
is reported in six studies,
qualitative agreement in
ten, and no comparison
in seven. In D6, only
three studies are rated
low risk, nine have
some concerns, and
11 are rated high risk.

Replication ratings
show a similar limitation.
Seven studies are rated
low risk in D1,
15 have some concerns,
and one is high risk.
Independent-run counts
are recorded for ten
studies. Explicit code
availability occurs in
four studies, and data
availability in two.
One included study was
assessed from an abstract
only.

We conclude that modular
robots offer an experimentally
promising route toward
active structures whose
geometry and mechanical
response arise from local
actuation. Their strongest
contribution is the possibility
of directly manipulating
connectivity and observing
its consequences.

\subsection{Feedback-controlled active colloids}
\label{sec:platforms-colloids}

Feedback-controlled active colloids provide a particularly direct
connection between microscopic active-matter theory and programmable
experimentation. Unlike autonomous catalytic particles driven by
fixed chemical conditions, these systems can modify their motion
in response to external measurements or prescribed control rules.
The particles remain microscopic, but their effective interactions
can be changed without modifying their material composition.

The evidence contains 13 studies in this class. Chirality or
rotation is reported in nine studies, self-assembly or shape
formation in seven, and swarming or vortex behavior in seven.
Transport or locomotion appears in five studies, flocking or
polar order in four, and clustering in three. These categories
overlap substantially, since a rotating colloidal assembly
may simultaneously exhibit spatial organization and
collective transport.

The included experiments investigate programmed colloidal
motion, collective rotation, state switching, and
configuration-dependent interactions
\cite{Martinet2022rotationcontrolin,Yan2014colloidalsuperstruc,Chen2024synchronousandfull,Buerle2020formationofstable,Aubret2021metamachinesofplur,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Knippenberg2022roleofcohesionin,Lffler2023collectiveforaging,Han2020programmablechiral,Chen2022collectiveresponse,Guo2026afeedbackcontrolle,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Lffler2021behaviordependentc}.
Together, these studies demonstrate the range of
collective responses accessible through programmable
or feedback-mediated colloidal dynamics.

The main advantage is the availability of physical
observables that connect naturally to statistical
mechanics. Reported measurements include polarization
and rotational order parameters, angular velocity,
orientation distributions, mean-squared displacement,
pair correlations, susceptibility, relaxation times,
and configuration-dependent response. These quantities
allow the experimenter to move beyond visual
classification of collective patterns.

For example, a polarization order parameter measures
the degree of orientational alignment, whereas a
rotational order parameter characterizes collective
circulation. These quantities distinguish forms of
organization that may appear visually similar.
Susceptibility and relaxation measurements add
information about response and stability.

Several studies report observables particularly
relevant to collective-state characterization.
Rotational order, fluctuations, and switching
behavior are extracted for controlled colloidal
assemblies
\cite{Chen2024synchronousandfull,Buerle2020formationofstable,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Lffler2021behaviordependentc}.
Other experiments examine orientation statistics,
collective transport, pair interactions, and
configuration-dependent dynamics
\cite{Martinet2022rotationcontrolin,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Chen2022collectiveresponse,Guo2026afeedbackcontrolle}.
Such measurements provide a basis for testing
effective interaction laws and stochastic
descriptions.

The interaction inventory shows that direct physical
coupling remains important even in programmed systems.
Steric contact occurs in 11 studies, hydrodynamic
coupling in nine, optical or other sensing in eight,
and programmed rules in seven. Magnetic interactions
are reported in two studies, and environment-mediated
interactions in three.

The control architecture is unusually homogeneous.
Twelve studies use external feedback, and one
uses a learned policy. Neither on-board rules
nor central control is recorded as the primary
architecture in this class. This distribution
reflects the experimental importance of observing
particle configurations and using that information
to influence subsequent motion.

External feedback makes it possible to construct
interactions with controlled symmetry and state
dependence. For active-matter physics, the
interesting question is whether the resulting
system obeys the predicted collective dynamics
when the programmed parameters are varied.
The experiment should therefore characterize
the implemented rule as well as the emergent
behavior.

The theoretical evidence is comparatively strong.
Agent-based simulation is recorded in ten studies.
Four compare with other statistical-mechanical
frameworks, three with Vicsek-type models,
two with active Brownian particle descriptions,
and two with kinetic or hydrodynamic theory.
These categories are not mutually exclusive.

Nine studies report quantitative agreement,
two qualitative agreement, and two no
theoretical comparison. The D6 risk-of-bias
assessment rates seven studies as low risk,
three as having some concerns, and three
as high risk.

Thus, a majority of this small platform class
reports quantitative comparison. More importantly,
over half of the studies receive a low-risk
rating for that domain. This distinguishes
feedback-controlled colloids from several
larger robotic categories, where qualitative
comparisons dominate.

The reported collective sizes illustrate
an opportunity for such tests. Maximum
particle counts are available in nine
studies, with a median of 48 and
a largest recorded collective of 400.
These populations are sufficient for
investigating spatial organization
beyond isolated pair interactions,
although the extracted agent counts
alone do not establish finite-size
scaling.

Replication is less consistently documented.
Four studies receive low-risk ratings
in D1, while nine have some concerns.
Independent-run counts are recorded for
three studies. Explicit code availability
and data availability are each identified
in only one study.

These limitations are consequential.
Feedback-controlled experiments depend
not only on physical particle properties
but also on the measurement and control
pipeline. Tracking errors, feedback
latency, calibration, and implementation
details can alter the effective
interaction law. Reproducing the
experiment therefore requires more
information than the nominal controller
equations alone.

All 13 studies were assessed on
a full-text basis. This avoids the
additional uncertainty associated
with abstract-only eligibility
assessment, but it does not remove
the limitations identified by the
risk-of-bias ratings.

We conclude that feedback-controlled
colloids are among the most promising
platforms for quantitative tests of
programmable active-matter interactions.
Their distinctive strength is the
ability to modify effective microscopic
rules while retaining access to
particle-level measurements.

Yet, this strength creates a demanding
standard of interpretation. We must
show not only that the programmed
system behaves as intended, but also
that its collective observables
support the proposed physical model.
The next step is therefore systematic
testing of fluctuations, response,
and finite-size dependence, accompanied
by reproducible descriptions of the
complete feedback implementation.

\subsection{Smarticles and granular robots}
\label{sec:platforms-smarticles}

Smarticles and granular robots occupy an intermediate
position between conventional robotics and driven
granular matter. Their individual components may be
programmable or internally actuated, while collective
motion arises largely through mechanical interactions.
The central physical question is how internal activity
is converted into transport, assembly, or changes in
collective configuration.

Unlike wheeled robots, these systems often cannot
translate freely through independent control of
their direction. Instead, locomotion emerges from
contact with neighboring agents, confinement,
or environmental constraints. The geometry of
an individual robot is therefore part of the
interaction mechanism.

The evidence contains ten studies in this class.
Transport or locomotion is reported in eight,
self-assembly or shape formation in six,
and clustering in two. Flocking or polar
order and chirality or rotation each occur
in one study.

The included experiments investigate internally
actuated robotic assemblies, collective transport,
configuration-dependent motion, and granular
organization
\cite{Li2018supersmarticlealo,Bhamla2021collectivetransport,Vardhan2022glidersinshapecha,Savoie2018phototacticsupersma,OzkanAydin2020collectiveprotectio,Luo2025flockingphasesepar,Warkentin2018locomotingrobotsco,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,Li2020programmingactiveg}.
These studies illustrate how locomotion can
arise from collective constraints rather
than from independently directed propulsion.

Steric contact is recorded in all ten studies.
Environment-mediated interactions occur in four,
while programmed rules, optical or other sensing,
and magnetic interactions each appear in two.
Thus, contact mechanics is the defining interaction
mechanism across the category.

This produces a distinctive experimental regime.
The motion of one component depends on the
configurations and movements of its neighbors.
Mechanical constraints can convert internal
actuation into collective displacement, while
the resulting motion changes the configuration
through which forces are transmitted.

Here, collective transport is not simply the
sum of independently controlled trajectories.
The arrangement of components can determine
whether activity produces translation,
rotation, confinement, or fluctuations.
Hence, the relevant physical state includes
both the individual actuation and the
instantaneous collective geometry.

The reported observables reflect this coupling.
Mean-squared displacement, drift velocity,
collective displacement, binding probability,
configuration lifetime, interparticle distance,
and cluster statistics appear among the
extracted measurements. Some studies also
report fluctuation-based or
statistical-mechanical quantities.

For example, experiments on collective
locomotion and configuration-dependent
transport use displacement and diffusion
measurements
\cite{Li2018supersmarticlealo,Savoie2018phototacticsupersma,Warkentin2018locomotingrobotsco,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo}.
Other studies characterize relative
configuration, binding, or collective
motion under mechanical constraints
\cite{Bhamla2021collectivetransport,Vardhan2022glidersinshapecha,OzkanAydin2020collectiveprotectio}.
These observables permit comparison
with models of mechanically coupled
active systems.

An important methodological advantage
is the possibility of observing the
microscopic configurations responsible
for collective transport. This connects
the experimentally accessible dynamics
to theories involving constraints,
fluctuations, and collective
configurational statistics.

Studies of low-rattling behavior,
programmed aggregation, and
flocking-related collective motion
provide complementary approaches
\cite{Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Li2020programmingactiveg,Luo2025flockingphasesepar}.
They also illustrate that different
collective observables answer
different physical questions.
A displacement measurement establishes
transport, while configuration
statistics provide information
about how the transport arises.

Nine studies employ on-board rules,
and one is classified as having
no control or passive rules.
Thus, the systems are internally
active, but their collective
motion frequently depends on
mechanical coupling rather than
explicit information exchange.

This distinction matters for
active-matter theory. Programmed
actuation may set a driving
cycle, but the resulting
collective trajectory need
not be prescribed. A theoretical
model must therefore account
for the relationship between
actuation, geometry, and
contact-mediated response.

The extracted theoretical comparisons
include agent-based simulations in
six studies, other statistical-mechanical
frameworks in three, and kinetic
or hydrodynamic theory in three.
Three studies have no theoretical
comparison recorded.

Five studies report quantitative
agreement, two qualitative agreement,
and three no comparison.
For D6, four studies are rated
low risk, two have some concerns,
and four are high risk.
This indicates a meaningful,
though limited, body of
quantitative evidence.

Collective sizes remain modest.
Maximum agent counts are recorded
for eight studies, with a
median of 5.5 and a largest
reported collective of 30.
Small ensembles can reveal
how constraints convert
internal activity into
motion. However, they
provide limited evidence
for large-system phase
behavior.

Replication is moderately documented.
Four studies are rated low risk
for D1, and six have some concerns.
Independent-run counts are available
for four studies. Explicit code
availability is recorded in two,
and data availability in three.

Two studies were assessed from
abstracts alone. Their contribution
to claims about quantitative
agreement and experimental
reproducibility must therefore
be treated with additional caution.

The evidence is strongest for
the existence of collective
locomotion and configuration-dependent
transport mediated by mechanical
interactions. It is less
comprehensive for identifying
universal scaling behavior
or macroscopic constitutive laws.

The opportunity is to use
these systems as experimentally
accessible models of driven,
constrained assemblies.
Systematic measurements of
configuration distributions,
contact networks, fluctuations,
and response under controlled
actuation would help distinguish
general collective mechanisms
from platform-specific
locomotion strategies.

As such, smarticles and
granular robots offer
a physically complementary
route to active-matter
experiments. They emphasize
the role of mechanical
constraints, where collective
behavior arises not simply
from communication or
programmed alignment,
but from the interaction
between internal activity
and collective geometry.

\subsection{Robotic metamaterials and active solids}
\label{sec:platforms-active-solids}

Robotic metamaterials and active solids extend the
concept of active matter beyond collections of
independently moving agents. In these systems,
activity may be embedded in a connected mechanical
structure. The collective variables are then
deformations, stresses, waves, mechanical response,
and internally generated motion rather than
particle trajectories alone.

This change of perspective is important.
For a conventional active fluid, we often seek
relations between local motion and coarse-grained
density or orientation fields. For an active
solid, we additionally require a description
of how internal actuation changes mechanical
response. The central question becomes
whether collective forces and deformations
can be represented by an effective
constitutive law.

The evidence contains ten studies in this class.
Seven report active-solid or odd-elastic
phenomena, five report transport or locomotion,
and four contain other collective responses.
Self-assembly or shape formation appears
in two studies. Boundary accumulation,
jamming or crystallization, synchronization,
and chirality or rotation each occur
in one study.

The included experiments investigate
nonreciprocal mechanical interactions,
active elastic response, programmable
metamaterials, and collective structural
dynamics
\cite{Baconnier2022tensioncontrolledc,Zhang2026noiseenhancedtempo,Rezanejad2025programmableentangl,Elshalakani2020simulationofselfc,Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Du2026metamaterialsthatl,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe,Coulais2025activemetamaterials,Chen2020selfsensingmetamat}.
These systems demonstrate the diversity
of mechanical responses accessible
through active or programmable structures.

The most distinctive observables are
mechanical. Reported measurements
include vibrational spectra,
transmission, spatial decay rates,
angular displacement amplitudes,
stiffness matrices, eigenvalues,
deformation fields, shear response,
and elastic moduli.

These observables permit more direct
tests of active-solid theory than
visual inspection of a changing shape.
A measured response matrix can,
for example, characterize how a
system reacts to imposed perturbations.
Likewise, vibrational spectra and
wave propagation reveal dynamical
properties of the coupled structure.

Several studies report measurements
particularly relevant to nonreciprocal
response and active elasticity
\cite{Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe,Coulais2025activemetamaterials}.
Other systems characterize
programmable stiffness,
mechanical response,
and collective dynamics
\cite{Du2026metamaterialsthatl,Chen2020selfsensingmetamat,Baconnier2022tensioncontrolledc,Zhang2026noiseenhancedtempo}.
These observables connect
individual actuation to
collective mechanical behavior.

For an ordinary passive elastic
solid, small deformations are
often characterized by
reciprocal mechanical response
under appropriate equilibrium
assumptions. Active systems
can violate these assumptions
through nonconservative
interactions and internally
supplied energy
\cite{Scheibner2020odd}.

Robotic metamaterials offer a
way to test such behavior
because active elements can
be integrated into a
mechanical network.
The relevant evidence
is not merely the
observation of a moving
structure, but the
measurement of response
that distinguishes
active nonreciprocity
from passive mechanics.

Interaction modalities reflect
the structure of the systems.
Steric contact occurs in seven
studies, programmed rules in six,
communication in three,
environment-mediated interactions
in three, and hydrodynamic
coupling in one.

Seven studies use on-board
rules, two use no control
or passive rules, and one
uses external feedback.
Hence, programmed actuation
often enters through
components embedded in
the structure rather
than through a controller
that explicitly coordinates
the entire assembly.

The extracted theoretical
frameworks include agent-based
simulation in eight studies
and active elasticity in four.
One study reports another
theoretical framework.
These categories overlap.

Six studies report quantitative
agreement, three qualitative
agreement, and one has no
agreement value recorded.
For D6, five studies are
rated low risk, four have
some concerns, and one
is high risk.

This distribution suggests
that the active-solid
literature has a comparatively
strong emphasis on
quantitative mechanics,
despite its small size.
Yet, the evidence remains
heterogeneous in the
specific observables
and physical models tested.

Similarly, agreement between
a mechanical simulation
and an observed deformation
can support the model
of a specific structure.
A stronger test of
a constitutive law
requires quantitative
predictions across
different loadings,
geometries, or
boundary conditions.

The available collective
sizes vary substantially.
Agent maxima are recorded
for seven studies, with
a median of 32 and
a largest value of 800.
However, the meaning
of an agent differs
between a mechanically
connected network and
a collection of
independently moving
robots.

The number of active
elements therefore
provides only one
measure of experimental
scale. For a metamaterial,
network geometry,
connectivity, and
the number of
independently addressable
degrees of freedom
may be equally important.

Replication reporting is
limited. Only one study
is rated low risk for D1,
while nine have some
concerns. A numerical
independent-run count
is recorded for one
study. Explicit code
availability is identified
in one study, and data
availability in two.

Two included studies
were assessed from
abstracts alone.
For these records,
detailed claims about
the experimental
mechanical response
must remain conditional
on the information
available in the
extracted abstracts.

The main strength of
robotic active solids
is that they make
mechanical response
directly programmable
and measurable.
They can realize
effective interactions
and deformation modes
that are difficult
to investigate in
ordinary passive
materials.

We see a particularly
promising direction
in experiments that
measure full response
matrices and their
dependence on actuation,
frequency, and geometry.
These quantities can
connect discrete
robotic implementations
to continuum models
without relying on
visual analogies.

Such experiments would
also help distinguish
intrinsic active
mechanical response
from effects introduced
by controllers,
mechanical constraints,
or finite sample size.
Hence, robotic
metamaterials may
become especially
valuable as
quantitative tests
of nonequilibrium
elasticity.

\subsection{Cross-platform synthesis}
\label{sec:platforms-cross}

The platform taxonomy reveals a central distinction
in robotic active matter. Some experiments use
programmability primarily to implement interaction
rules. Others use active mechanical components
or externally imposed fields to generate motion
whose collective consequences are not directly
specified by software.

Both approaches are valuable, but they test
different aspects of active-matter physics.

Wheeled and legged robots make local sensing,
communication, and programmed interactions
accessible. They are well suited to examining
how microscopic decision rules produce
collective organization. Their limitation
is that successful implementation of
a theoretical interaction rule can
be mistaken for independent confirmation
of its emergent predictions.

Vibration-driven robots and smarticles provide
a complementary regime. Activity is generated
mechanically, and collective motion depends
strongly on contact, friction, geometry,
and confinement. These systems can expose
collective mechanisms that are not explicitly
encoded as neighbor-response algorithms.
Their limitation is the difficulty of
separating propulsion, fluctuations,
and interactions experimentally.

Microscopic robotic agents extend the accessible
range of physical interactions. Magnetic,
hydrodynamic, optical, and other couplings
can produce collective organization under
externally programmable actuation.
Feedback-controlled colloids add more direct
access to the relationship between measured
particle configurations and subsequent
dynamics. Yet, common external forcing
and feedback-mediated interactions must
be distinguished from autonomous
interparticle coupling.

Modular robots and active metamaterials
shift the emphasis from collective
particle motion toward the dynamics
of coupled structures. Their
configurations, response matrices,
and mechanical spectra provide
routes to testing active-material
theories. However, a programmable
structure that moves or changes
shape is not automatically a
quantitative realization of
a proposed constitutive law.

The appropriate comparison
therefore concerns which
physical variables are
independently controlled
and which collective
observables are measured.

\paragraph*{A common experimental standard.}

Across platforms, three requirements are particularly
important for interpreting collective behavior.

First, the isolated-agent dynamics should be
characterized. Without this reference, changes
in collective motion may be incorrectly attributed
to interactions between agents. This is relevant
to mechanically driven robots, magnetic
microswimmers, and mobile systems with complex
feedback controllers.

Second, the interaction mechanism should
be independently constrained wherever
possible. For programmable swarms,
this includes the implemented control
rule and its physical limitations.
For microscopic systems, it includes
the effects of common driving fields
and fluid-mediated coupling.
For active structures, it includes
mechanical connectivity and the
response of individual components.

Third, collective observables should
be compared quantitatively across
controlled experimental conditions.
A visually coherent flock,
rotating cluster, or moving
metamaterial can establish the
existence of an interesting
collective state. It does not
necessarily determine which
theoretical mechanism generated it.

These requirements are closely related
to the review's risk-of-bias
domains. Replication, agent
heterogeneity, finite-size
effects, measurement validity,
and quantitative theory
comparison are not independent
administrative criteria.
They describe the experimental
conditions needed to distinguish
a robust collective phenomenon
from a platform-specific
observation.

A further limitation concerns
the interpretation of
experimental scale.
Agent numbers range from
small groups of macroscopic
robots to very large
microscopic populations.
However, physical size,
independent controllability,
measurement resolution,
and number of interacting
degrees of freedom
change simultaneously
across these regimes.

As a result, the number
of agents cannot serve
as a universal indicator
of experimental quality.
A meaningful comparison
must consider whether
the available system
size is sufficient for
the phenomenon under
investigation and whether
finite-size behavior
has been characterized.

\paragraph*{From platform diversity to physical comparison.}

The diversity of platforms creates opportunities
for experiments that test the same physical
principle through different implementations.
A collective state reproduced with mechanical
contacts, programmable sensing, and
field-mediated interactions may provide
stronger evidence for a general mechanism
than repeated demonstrations on closely
related hardware.

Yet, such comparisons require more than
shared names for collective phenomena.
The relevant order parameters, control
variables, and model predictions
must also be comparable.
Two systems exhibiting rotation
need not share an interaction
law. Similarly, clustering
under imposed attraction
does not automatically imply
motility-induced phase
separation.

We therefore use the platform taxonomy
as the starting point rather than
the endpoint of the synthesis.
The complete study-to-platform
index is provided in
Table~\ref{tab:platform_index}.
This preserves the traceability
of individual classifications
without turning the discussion
into a catalogue of hardware.

The following sections examine
collective phenomena and theoretical
comparisons across these platform
boundaries. There, the decisive
question is whether different
robotic implementations provide
independent evidence for a
shared physical mechanism.

In this sense, the defining
advantage of robotic active
matter is not programmability
alone. It is the possibility
of combining programmed
interactions with measured
physical dynamics. The
strongest experiments use
that combination to expose
the assumptions of
active-matter theory,
test their consequences,
and identify where
simplified models fail.
