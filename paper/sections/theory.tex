\section{Confronting theory with robotic experiments}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{From theoretical analogy to experimental test}

Robotic active matter can play three different roles with respect to theory. A robot may be inspired by a model. It may reproduce a pattern predicted by that model. Or it may test a numerical prediction against measured hardware data. Only the third role directly addresses objective O3.

The distinction is easy to lose. A controller derived from a Vicsek-type rule can generate polar motion. This shows that the rule can be implemented. It does not yet test whether the rule explains the physical system. Likewise, an agent-based simulation that runs the same control code as the robots may reproduce their trajectories. Yet, agreement can follow because the algorithm was copied rather than because the unmodelled mechanics were explained.

A quantitative confrontation requires an observable on both sides. Let the experiment provide \(y_{\mathrm{exp}}(\lambda)\) and the theory provide \(y_{\mathrm{th}}(\lambda;\theta)\), where \(\lambda\) is a controlled variable and \(\theta\) contains model parameters. The evidential question is then not merely whether
\[
y_{\mathrm{exp}} \approx y_{\mathrm{th}}.
\]
We must also ask how \(\theta\) was obtained, how many values of \(\lambda\) were tested, and whether the observable used for evaluation also determined the parameters.

Here, we use the extracted agreement field as a first separation. ``Quantitative'' means that experiment and theory were confronted through numerical observables. It does not mean that the theory agreed well, nor that the comparison was prospective. ``Qualitative'' means that states, trends, or patterns were compared without a numerical agreement test. ``None'' means that no theory--experiment agreement was reported.

In the 2026-10-14 snapshot, the evidence table contains 530 studies. Agreement is coded as quantitative in 134, qualitative in 244, none in 129, and unresolved or missing in 23. Eight of the 134 quantitative rows do not carry a substantive named framework. They concern quantitative validation, control, or reduced descriptions but cannot be assigned to one of the extracted theory classes. Hence, 126 studies contribute to the framework-level quantitative synthesis.

All 126 were assessed from full text. Thus, none of the numerical framework counts below rests only on an abstract. Still, 34 of these rows retain at least one unresolved extraction field. We do not use an unresolved field to support a study-specific claim. Framework counts for the complete evidence base include abstract-only assessments, and Table~\ref{tab:theory-evidence} identifies their number.

The theory labels overlap. One study may compare an agent-based simulation with kinetic theory and an active-Brownian description. Table~\ref{tab:theory-evidence} therefore maps use of each framework rather than partitioning the studies.

Agent-based simulation dominates. It appears in 287 studies and accounts for 103 quantitative comparisons. Kinetic or hydrodynamic theory follows with 68 uses and 32 quantitative comparisons. Active-Brownian-particle and other statistical-mechanical descriptions each appear in 28 studies, with 19 quantitative comparisons apiece. Vicsek-type models appear in 20 studies but are compared quantitatively in only four. Active elasticity and run-and-tumble theory remain small classes.

The quantitative subset is recent. Thirty studies precede 2020, while 96 appeared from 2020 onward. It is also distributed across platforms. Wheeled or legged robots contribute 34 studies, robotic micro- or nano-agents 26, vibration-driven robots 25, residual platforms 15, feedback-controlled colloids nine, robotic metamaterials or active solids six, smarticles five, and modular robots five. One platform class remains unresolved.

This spread is encouraging. A framework tested on several embodiments may capture more than one apparatus. Yet, platform diversity alone does not establish universality. The same theoretical label can be used at different levels, with different parameters and observables. We therefore examine each framework separately before asking what generalizes.

\subsection{What constitutes a strong test?}

\subsubsection{Calibration, prediction, and discrimination}

Let us first define a useful hierarchy. At its base is a visual analogy. The experiment and model form similar clusters, vortices, or trajectories. Such correspondence can motivate a hypothesis, but many mechanisms produce the same pattern.

The next level is numerical reconstruction. Model parameters are chosen so that a measured observable is reproduced. This establishes consistency. It does not establish prediction because the observable helped determine the model.

A stronger test separates calibration from evaluation. Single-agent speed, turning statistics, collision response, or interaction range may determine \(\theta\). The collective prediction is then evaluated without refitting. For example, independently measured persistence and collision statistics may be used to predict clustering or transport.

Stronger still is model discrimination. Two models are calibrated to the same low-level data but predict different collective behaviour. The experiment measures the observable on which they disagree. Robotic systems are particularly suitable for this level because delay, noise, morphology, feedback, and interaction topology can often be changed independently.

Finally, transfer tests whether one calibrated description survives a new population, boundary, or platform. Such evidence is rare. Most robotic studies compare one apparatus with one model under one family of conditions.

Hence, ``quantitative'' covers several levels of inference. The evidence table identifies numerical confrontation, but it does not encode whether parameters were fitted, whether predictions were held out, or whether alternatives were compared. The study-level observables and design must supply that distinction.

\subsubsection{Agreement in one observable and agreement of a mechanism}

A model may reproduce mean speed and fail on fluctuations. It may predict polarization but miss collision rate. It may match a cluster-size distribution while producing the wrong formation pathway. Agreement therefore depends on the chosen observable.

This is especially important in robotic systems. A controller can enforce the mean behaviour while hardware noise determines the distribution around it. Friction can leave an order parameter unchanged but alter relaxation. Communication delay can preserve a stationary state and change its stability.

A persuasive comparison should cover observables at more than one level. Single-agent quantities characterize propulsion. Pair observables characterize interaction. Group observables characterize the collective state. Time-dependent response tests stability and causality.

Several included studies approach this hierarchy. Rotating robot experiments combine displacement, angular motion, domain size, diffusion, and pair structure \cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove}. Visual collective experiments combine polarization, milling, opacity, and collision rate \cite{Castro2025visualcollectivebe}. Active colloid experiments combine spatial order, angular displacement, susceptibility, and radial density \cite{Buerle2020formationofstable}. Agreement across such observables is more informative than agreement in one optimized score.

\subsubsection{Robots as model implementations}

There is one further complication. In conventional experiments, theory describes a system that exists independently of the theory. In programmable robotics, the model may be compiled into the agents. This creates a useful but unusual loop:
\[
\text{model}
\longrightarrow
\text{controller}
\longrightarrow
\text{physical robots}
\longrightarrow
\text{measured collective state}.
\]

The experiment then tests whether the physical implementation preserves the model under latency, noise, finite size, and embodiment. This is a valid question. Yet, it differs from asking whether the model explains a naturally arising interaction.

We therefore distinguish implementation fidelity from explanatory adequacy. Agent-based simulations are often strongest for the first. Kinetic, hydrodynamic, active-particle, and elastic theories more often address the second. The distinction is not absolute, but it helps interpret the evidence.

\subsection{Agent-based simulations}

\subsubsection{The dominant comparator}

Agent-based simulation appears in 287 studies. Of these, 103 report quantitative comparison, 172 qualitative comparison, one no agreement, and 11 an unresolved or missing agreement field. Five were assessed from abstracts only, but all 103 quantitative rows were assessed from full text.

The framework is broad. It includes direct simulations of controller code, particle models with fitted interactions, rigid-body simulations, Brownian dynamics, and numerical models of mechanical modules. Treating all of them as one theory would be misleading. Their common feature is that individual agents or elements are represented explicitly.

This flexibility explains their dominance. Robot identity is observable, control rules are discrete, and collisions occur between finite bodies. An agent-based representation can incorporate these features without deriving a continuum limit.

Wheeled or legged robots account for 31 of the 103 quantitative agent-based comparisons. Vibration-driven robots contribute 19, micro- or nano-agents 18, residual platforms 14, feedback-controlled colloids eight, smarticles five, active solids five, and modular robots two. One platform is unresolved. Hence, agent-based comparison is the only framework used quantitatively across every named platform class.

\subsubsection{When direct simulation is informative}

Direct simulation is most informative when it contains fewer assumptions than the hardware but preserves the hypothesized mechanism. Aggregation experiments provide an early example. Local sensing and joining or leaving rules generate predictions for aggregate size, isolated agents, shelter occupancy, and collective choice \cite{Garnier2005collectivedecision}. The comparison tests whether the proposed local rule is sufficient to account for the measured group statistics.

Mechanically driven transport offers another example. Bristle-bot experiments relate collective payload motion to persistence, contact density, and geometry, while simulations test whether the measured mechanical ingredients reproduce the transport regime \cite{Arbel2025amechanicalroutef}. The value does not come from recreating the payload trajectory point by point. It comes from reproducing how transport changes with controlled physical parameters.

Agent-based models are also useful when body shape creates the interaction. Curvature and contact geometry can be varied in custom vibration-driven robots and compared with predictions for force, cohesion, polar order, cluster size, and structural order \cite{Casiulis2025ageometricconditio}. Here, the model links a geometric intervention to several group-level observables.

Visual interaction rules provide a complementary case. A model can implement the same visual response used by the physical robots and predict polarization, milling, or collision statistics \cite{Castro2025visualcollectivebe}. Agreement establishes that the implemented visual law remains effective after physical delay and motion constraints are added. It does not by itself establish that natural collectives use the same law.

Feedback-controlled colloids give unusually clean access to interaction design. The controller observes physical particles and modifies their propulsion. Agent-based simulation can therefore use the programmed law while retaining stochastic motion and contact. Quantitative comparisons of polar, rotating, and responsive swarm states show how delay and local steering alter collective order \cite{Chen2024persistentandrespo,Chen2024synchronousandfull}.

Smarticles and modular systems use simulations to connect local mechanical degrees of freedom to collective motion. Shape-changing units in confinement generate state probabilities that can be compared with simulated configuration statistics \cite{Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred}. Phase-shifted or connected modules generate locomotion whose direction and speed can be compared with explicit-element models \cite{Li2019particleroboticsba,Rogowski2026coordinatedselfass}.

\subsubsection{The problem of copied algorithms}

A simulation can agree for a trivial reason. If the robot and simulation execute the same rule, then agreement in formation shape or heading may show only that the software was transferred correctly. The physical contribution lies in the residual difference.

A useful comparison should therefore expose quantities absent from the controller. These may include collision rate, slip, force, deformation, latency, energy use, boundary residence, or failure. In other words, the simulation should risk being wrong.

Large programmable assemblies illustrate the issue. A simulation and robot swarm may both reach a target because they share the assembly algorithm. Yet, traffic jams and packing errors arise from physical embodiment \cite{Rubenstein2014programmableselfas}. The informative test concerns whether the model predicts those deviations.

The same applies to learned policies. A policy may reproduce a simulated pattern on hardware. This establishes transfer. It does not validate the simulator as a physical theory unless independent observables also agree. Learned control is addressed in Sec.~\ref{sec:control}; here, the relevant point is that policy success and theory success are different outcomes.

\subsubsection{Parameter identification}

Agent-based models often contain many effective parameters. Speed, angular noise, collision response, sensing range, delay, friction, and attraction may all be adjusted. A model with sufficient freedom can fit one experiment without identifying a mechanism.

The strongest studies reduce this freedom by calibrating at lower levels. Single-agent trajectories determine speed and persistence. Pair experiments determine interaction. Collective measurements then test the model. Rotating-robot experiments follow this logic by linking individual diffusion and rotation to domain and edge dynamics \cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove}. Morphological robot studies relate collision orientation and single-agent persistence to collective transport \cite{Zion2023morphologicalcomput}.

Note that parameter uncertainty should be propagated. A single best-fit parameter set can hide non-identifiability. If several combinations reproduce the same collective order, the mechanism remains underdetermined. Few studies in the evidence table report such uncertainty explicitly.

\subsubsection{What agreement supports}

The 103 quantitative agent-based comparisons support a moderate conclusion. Explicit-agent models often reproduce measured trends and collective observables when the platform's interaction rules are known. This holds across programmed swarms, vibration-driven particles, controlled colloids, microrobots, modular systems, and active structures.

The stronger claim that one minimal model explains several unrelated platforms is less secure. Most models remain specific to one apparatus. Forty-nine of the 287 agent-based studies name code, and 39 name data. Among the quantitative subset, the corresponding evidence remains a minority. Reproduction by an independent group is therefore difficult.

Future work should publish the simulator, controller version, calibration data, and experimental trajectories together. It should also state which observables were fitted and which were predicted. Without this separation, numerical agreement remains descriptive.

\subsection{Active Brownian particles}

\subsubsection{A minimal kinematic description}

The active-Brownian-particle model represents a self-propelled particle whose orientation decorrelates through rotational noise \todocite{canonical active Brownian particle model and review}. A common overdamped form is
\begin{align}
\dot{\mathbf r}_i &=
v_i\mathbf p_i
+\mu_i\mathbf F_i
+\sqrt{2D_{t,i}}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_i,
\\
\dot{\theta}_i &=
\Omega_i
+\sqrt{2D_{r,i}}\,\eta_i,
\end{align}
where \(\mathbf p_i=(\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)\), \(v_i\) is the active speed, \(\mathbf F_i\) contains interactions, and \(D_t\) and \(D_r\) set translational and rotational fluctuations. Chirality enters through \(\Omega_i\).

This model is attractive for robotics because speed, persistence, angular drift, and collision can be measured directly. Yet, a wheeled or vibration-driven robot is not overdamped at every time scale. It may have finite turning speed, coloured noise, static friction, controller delay, and noncircular shape.

Active-Brownian-particle theory appears in 28 studies. Nineteen compare it quantitatively and nine qualitatively. One study was assessed from the abstract only, while every quantitative row was assessed from full text. Twelve of the 19 quantitative comparisons use vibration-driven robots. Three use wheeled robots, two micro- or nano-agents, and two feedback-controlled colloids.

The framework is therefore used primarily as a macroscopic analogue. This is a strength because centimetre-scale robots expose trajectories directly. It is also a limitation because the mapping from hardware noise to Brownian noise must be demonstrated.

\subsubsection{Single-agent calibration}

The decisive step is to characterize isolated motion before interpreting the collective. Suitable observables include mean-squared displacement, velocity autocorrelation, angular velocity, persistence time, and rotational diffusion.

Vibration-driven robot studies measure these quantities and use them to compare regular and chiral active motion, boundary residence, sorting, and induced rotation \cite{Balda2024playingwithactive}. Morphobots similarly connect nominal speed, persistence, diffusion, collision angle, and phototactic transport \cite{Zion2023morphologicalcomput}. These designs make the mapping to an active-particle model explicit.

Geometry-controlled bristlebots add a useful test. Body design changes angular motion, wall residence, radial distribution, and transport \cite{Wagner2026geometryinducedtra}. The model must then account for a controlled change in microscopic kinematics rather than fit one fixed robot.

Still, isolated-agent agreement is not sufficient. Collective contact can change speed or orientation statistics. Battery depletion or substrate wear can also make the parameters time dependent. Hence, calibration should be repeated across density and experiment duration.

\subsubsection{Clustering and phase separation}

The most ambitious use of the model concerns clustering and motility-induced phase separation. A large vibration-driven population can be characterized through local density, instantaneous speed, rotational diffusion, and a clustering index \cite{Hao2022controllingcollisio}. Such measurements address the density--motility feedback more directly than final images.

Magnetically cohesive bristlebots provide a contrasting case. Their clusters reflect both activity and attraction \cite{Li2021programmingactivec}. An active-Brownian comparison can describe motion and aggregation, but the resulting dense phase should not be interpreted as attraction-free MIPS.

Light-controlled active colloids offer finer control of cohesion and clogging. Burst sizes, waiting times, flow, and mean-squared displacement can be compared with active-Brownian simulations \cite{Knippenberg2022roleofcohesionin}. Here, the model tests how activity modifies an explicitly cohesive system.

Light-modulated microscopic swimmers permit comparison between persistent, circular, and clustered motion \cite{Raghavendra2026programmablepersist}. The extracted row includes active-Brownian, run-and-tumble, and statistical-mechanical frameworks, but its control architecture and maximum population remain unresolved. The resolved observables nevertheless include mean-squared displacement, velocity autocorrelation, cluster-size distributions, and growth.

These studies show what the framework does well. It provides a common kinematic language for speed, persistence, chirality, and steric or cohesive interaction. It is weaker when long-lived memory, inertia, hydrodynamic coupling, or programmed sensing dominates.

\subsubsection{Transport and boundaries}

Collective transport is another productive test. Bristle-bot payload transport is compared through displacement, payload speed, persistence, density, and contact geometry \cite{Arbel2025amechanicalroutef}. The active-Brownian model supplies a baseline for collision-driven forcing. It does not encode all details of payload contact, so agreement across geometry is more informative than agreement at one load.

Boundary experiments similarly expose model limitations. Active particles accumulate at walls because persistent propulsion outlasts reorientation. Robotic bodies add collision torques and shape-dependent reflection. Measurements of residence time, density profiles, and angular statistics can determine whether a point-particle description remains adequate \cite{Siebers2023exploitingcompositi,Wagner2026geometryinducedtra}.

Twenty-eight studies use the framework, but only seven report independent run counts. Six name code and seven data. Thus, the model is quantitatively popular but not yet supported by broad reproducibility.

The evidence supports active-Brownian theory as a useful reduced description of robotic persistence and contact-dominated collective motion. It does not support treating every noisy robot as an active Brownian particle. The mapping must be measured, and deviations should be reported as results rather than absorbed into effective parameters.

\subsection{Vicsek-type alignment models}

\subsubsection{A canonical model with few direct tests}

Vicsek-type models couple self-propulsion to local alignment and angular noise \todocite{original Vicsek model and later reviews}. In a simple discrete form,
\[
\theta_i(t+\Delta t)
=
\arg\!\left[
\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}
e^{\mathrm i\theta_j(t)}
\right]
+\eta\,\zeta_i(t),
\]
followed by motion along the updated direction. Extensions add repulsion, attraction, delay, leaders, or spatially varying speed.

The framework appears in 20 studies. Four report quantitative comparison, 14 qualitative comparison, and two unresolved agreement fields. Ten use wheeled robots, five residual platforms, three feedback-controlled colloids, one vibration-driven system, and one unresolved platform.

This is a striking imbalance. Flocking or polar order appears in 153 studies, while only four studies quantitatively confront hardware with a Vicsek-type model. Robotic flocking is therefore much better established as an implementable behaviour than as a quantitative test of the canonical theory.

\subsubsection{The four quantitative confrontations}

Feedback-controlled colloids provide two of the four tests. Optical feedback implements alignment while physical particles retain noise, contact, and delayed response. Polar and rotating order parameters, switching rates, and state probabilities can then be compared with a Vicsek-type model \cite{Chen2024synchronousandfull}. Adaptive persistence and delayed response provide a related test through rotational order, response rate, group velocity, and particle-speed statistics \cite{Chen2024persistentandrespo}.

Wheeled swarms provide the other two. Perception-mediated interaction is evaluated through evacuation, alignment, trajectory curvature, and passage through a constrained environment \cite{Xiao2023perceptionofmotion}. A leader with periodically changing direction permits comparison of group order, angular motion, cross-correlation, and residence in collective states \cite{Dizenhaus2026anonymousleadership}.

These designs go beyond showing a flock. They change a parameter or perturbation and measure the response. Still, each uses an extended model tailored to the apparatus. The evidence does not yet test one fixed Vicsek model across several platforms.

\subsubsection{Why qualitative use dominates}

Many robot controllers borrow alignment, cohesion, and separation rules. A simulation then reproduces ordered motion qualitatively. Several factors make a stricter test difficult.

First, hardware populations are often small. Finite-size switching can dominate a transition. Second, boundaries produce alignment and milling. Third, sensing and communication define anisotropic, delayed neighbourhoods rather than the instantaneous metric neighbourhood of the simplest model. Fourth, collision avoidance adds a rule absent from the theoretical baseline.

These differences are not nuisances to be removed. They are experimental variables. A strong robotic test should measure the actual interaction graph, latency, and angular response. It should then compare the minimal model with extensions added one at a time.

The most useful observables include polarization, susceptibility, Binder cumulant, correlation length, switching time, and finite-size dependence. One hardware study combines order, susceptibility, Binder cumulant, angular displacement, and bulk versus surface density, although its extracted framework labels place the comparison under active-Brownian and other statistical mechanics rather than Vicsek type \cite{Barone2024experimentalandnum}. This example shows that the extracted labels identify explicit comparisons, not every conceptual resemblance.

At present, Vicsek-type theory is a common design language and an uncommon quantitative target. The next step is not another flocking demonstration. It is a preregistered comparison of competing neighbourhood, delay, and noise models on the same physical swarm.

\subsection{Run-and-tumble descriptions}

Run-and-tumble theory alternates persistent runs with discrete reorientation events \todocite{canonical run-and-tumble model}. It is appropriate when angular change occurs through identifiable events rather than continuous diffusion.

Only two studies carry the extracted run-and-tumble label. One is qualitative and one quantitative. Both were assessed from full text, and both contain unresolved extraction fields. The quantitative row concerns programmable microscopic persistence and compares trajectory and cluster observables across active-particle descriptions \cite{Raghavendra2026programmablepersist}.

The count should be interpreted narrowly. Other studies report run duration, tumble duration, turning angle, or tumbling frequency without being coded under the run-and-tumble framework. A linked pair of wheeled robots, for example, exhibits run-and-tumble-like motion and measures these distributions, but its extracted framework labels are agent-based simulation and active Brownian particles \cite{Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc}. Hence, the label count does not measure every conceptual use of run-and-tumble language.

The small evidence base prevents a general conclusion about quantitative agreement. It does reveal an opportunity. Robots can implement a controlled switching rule, while microscopic swimmers can be steered between persistent and reorienting regimes. One may then compare continuous rotational diffusion with discrete tumbles under matched persistence.

A discriminating experiment should calibrate both models to the same single-agent mean-squared displacement. It should then compare turning-angle distributions, velocity autocorrelation, first-passage times, boundary residence, and clustering. Agreement in mean displacement alone would not separate them.

\subsection{Kinetic and hydrodynamic theories}

\subsubsection{From agents to fields}

Kinetic and hydrodynamic theories replace detailed trajectories with distributions or continuum fields \todocite{kinetic and hydrodynamic theories of active matter}. A kinetic description may evolve a density \(f(\mathbf r,\theta,t)\) in position and orientation. Its moments produce density, polarization, and higher-order fields. A generic conservation law has the form
\[
\partial_t \rho + \nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf J = 0,
\]
where the flux \(\mathbf J\) depends on propulsion, polarization, interaction, diffusion, and external forcing.

Such theories are attractive because collective phases, waves, coarsening, and boundary layers are field-level phenomena. Yet, deriving a closed equation requires approximations. Robotic experiments can test whether these approximations survive finite bodies, delay, and friction.

Kinetic or hydrodynamic theory appears in 68 studies. Thirty-two report quantitative comparison, 34 qualitative comparison, and two unresolved agreement fields. One study was assessed from the abstract only, while the 32 quantitative rows were assessed from full text.

Micro- or nano-agents contribute 12 quantitative comparisons and vibration-driven robots 11. Smarticles contribute three, wheeled robots and feedback-controlled colloids two each, and residual and unresolved platforms one each. The concentration in small-scale and vibration-driven systems is physically sensible. Hydrodynamic interaction matters in fluids, while dense vibration-driven systems permit field-level measurements at visible scales.

\subsubsection{Aggregation and continuum density}

One early robotic use connects microscopic joining and leaving rules with macroscopic density evolution. Light-dependent aggregation of wheeled robots is compared with ordinary- and partial-differential-equation descriptions of occupancy and spatial density \cite{Hamann2008spatialmacroscopic}. Such experiments test whether a population-level closure can reproduce the aggregate dynamics without tracking each collision.

The strength of a continuum description is compression. A few fields replace many agents. The risk is that closure parameters absorb finite-population effects. A model fitted to one density may fail when agent number or arena size changes.

Vibration-driven aggregation provides richer spatial observables. Cluster-size distributions, surface fraction, persistence, and arena deformation can be compared with kinetic descriptions \cite{Deblais2018boundariescontrolc}. Geometry-controlled agents add pair forces, collision duration, polar order, and hexatic order \cite{Casiulis2025ageometricconditio}. These measurements can test the bridge between pair interaction and collective field.

\subsubsection{Transport and active baths}

Mechanical payload transport is compared with active-particle and kinetic descriptions through density, persistence, payload speed, and contact geometry \cite{Arbel2025amechanicalroutef}. A confined smarticle assembly is similarly described through mean-squared displacement, granular temperature, and drift \cite{Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo}. In both cases, the collective exerts an effective force on a larger object.

Such experiments probe active-bath theory at an accessible scale. The payload or container acts as a mechanical observer of the collective. Yet, the forcing can be non-Gaussian and history dependent. Mean drift alone cannot establish an effective equilibrium description.

A vibration-driven active bath has also been probed through membrane fluctuations, kinetic energy, spectra, and dissipation \cite{Boudet2022effectivetemperatur}. A later experiment reports autocorrelation, work, dissipation, memory, and effective temperature for a probe coupled to clustering robots \cite{Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami}. These studies directly test when coarse-grained thermodynamic language succeeds and where non-Markovian dynamics appears.

\subsubsection{Hydrodynamics at small scales}

Microrobotic systems make fluid coupling unavoidable. Acousto-magnetic swarms combine rotating magnetic assembly with acoustic and hydrodynamic forces and compare upstream transport with a physical model \cite{Ahmed2021bioinspiredacousto}. Magnetic chains near a wall combine rotation, translation, connectivity, and fluctuation across liquid-like and solid-like states \cite{Yigit2020cohesiveselforgani}. Magnetic nanoparticle swarms moving through flow are compared through velocity, integrity, chain length, and delivery \cite{Law2024navigationofmagnet}.

These comparisons are strongest when the external field is calibrated independently. Otherwise, magnetic, acoustic, and hydrodynamic contributions may be exchanged during fitting. A successful trajectory fit then does not identify the dominant mechanism.

Hydrodynamic coupling also mediates synchronization and collective rotation. Phototactic microgears quantify rotation, phase lag, diffusion, coupling, edge currents, and lifetime \cite{Aubret2018targetedassemblyan}. Feedback-controlled colloids use hydrodynamic interaction to couple spatial motion and phase \cite{Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw}. Such systems test reduced coupling laws, but confinement and wall effects remain integral to the interaction.

\subsubsection{Agreement and scope}

Kinetic or hydrodynamic theory has 32 quantitative comparisons among 68 uses. This proportion is higher than for agent-based simulation and Vicsek-type theory. Twenty-eight of the 68 studies receive a low-risk rating for quantitative theory comparison, 32 some concerns, seven high risk, and one unresolved.

Still, only 20 report independent run counts. Twelve name code and 16 data. Moreover, 48 of the 68 also use agent-based simulation. The continuum theory is therefore commonly supported by a microscopic numerical model rather than tested alone.

This combination can be powerful. The experiment tests the phenomenon, the agent model tests the microscopic mechanism, and the continuum model tests the large-scale reduction. However, the links must be explicit. Parameters should pass from one level to the next rather than being fitted independently at each level.

The evidence supports kinetic and hydrodynamic theories most strongly for trends in density, transport, boundary response, and flow-mediated organization. Evidence for asymptotic scaling or universal continuum coefficients is weaker because system sizes and geometries remain limited.

\subsection{Statistical-mechanical descriptions beyond the named models}

\subsubsection{A heterogeneous but quantitative class}

Twenty-eight studies use a statistical-mechanical framework not captured by the active-Brownian, Vicsek, run-and-tumble, or active-elasticity labels. Nineteen report quantitative comparison and nine qualitative comparison. Two were assessed from abstracts, but all quantitative rows were assessed from full text.

The class includes energy-like descriptions, fluctuation and response, low-rattling principles, effective temperature, hyperuniformity, phase-transition measures, percolation, and synchronization statistics. It is heterogeneous by construction. Still, the studies share an attempt to describe collective behaviour through distributions, fluctuations, or macroscopic state variables.

The 19 quantitative comparisons span wheeled robots, micro-agents, vibration-driven systems, controlled colloids, smarticles, modular robots, and residual platforms. No active-solid platform is coded in this category, although several active-solid studies use related ideas under the active-elasticity or ``other'' labels.

\subsubsection{State selection and low rattling}

A small smarticle ensemble provides a direct test of low rattling as a state-selection principle \cite{Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred}. The experiment measures configuration probabilities, covariance, and an entropy-like rattling quantity. The theory does not merely predict that the units aggregate. It ranks configurations.

This is an instructive use of a small robotic system. The population is too small for a thermodynamic limit, but the state space can be sampled repeatedly. The theory concerns configuration selection rather than bulk scaling, so small size is not automatically a weakness.

The same logic applies to robotic lattices formed through local force rules. Potential, kinetic, and total energy can be related to cluster size and orientation error \cite{Spears2004distributedphysics}. Yet, an engineered potential does not validate equilibrium statistical mechanics unless noise, dissipation, and steady-state sampling are also characterized.

\subsubsection{Transitions, fluctuations, and susceptibility}

Feedback-controlled active colloids allow order and fluctuations to be changed by a programmed interaction. Rotational and orientational order, susceptibility, and state distributions are compared across swarm regimes \cite{Buerle2020formationofstable}. Another feedback study measures autocorrelation, relaxation, susceptibility, bifurcation, and hysteresis in rotating states \cite{Lffler2021behaviordependentc}.

These observables are stronger than a state label. Susceptibility and hysteresis test response and history dependence. Still, finite populations blur transitions. A critical interpretation requires size dependence and a clear relation between controller parameters and effective coupling.

Wheeled robot experiments have measured order, susceptibility, Binder cumulant, angular displacement, and bulk versus surface density \cite{Barone2024experimentalandnum}. Such a combination addresses finite-size and boundary effects directly. However, the hardware population is smaller than the associated numerical systems, so transfer from robot to large-system behaviour remains an extrapolation.

Phase-coherent micromotor arrays provide another statistical-mechanical test. Antiferromagnetic order, synchronization, correlation length, spectral width, and wave propagation are measured together \cite{Braun2026phasecoherenceand}. This row has an unresolved minimum population, but the framework, observables, and quantitative comparison are resolved.

\subsubsection{Effective temperature and nonequilibrium response}

Effective temperature is tempting because it compresses fluctuations into one scalar. Active systems need not satisfy the equilibrium relation between fluctuation and response. Hence, an effective temperature must be tied to a specific observable and time scale.

Vibration-driven robot baths have been probed through velocity distributions, spectra, kinetic energy, and dissipation \cite{Boudet2022effectivetemperatur}. Probe dynamics in clustered robot baths reveal memory through autocorrelation, work, dissipation, and non-Markovian response \cite{Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami}. These experiments use robots as controllable nonequilibrium baths rather than as analogues of biological collectives.

The critical test is consistency. If two probes or observables yield different effective temperatures, the scalar is not a state variable. The present evidence establishes measurable effective descriptions under specific protocols. It does not establish a general thermodynamics of robotic active matter.

\subsubsection{Structural statistics}

Pair correlation, cluster statistics, structure factors, number variance, and hyperuniformity provide theory-sensitive measures of spatial organization. Active magnetic spinners have been characterized through structure factors, number variance, bond change, and pair correlations \cite{Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork}. Their extracted frameworks are agent-based and kinetic or hydrodynamic rather than ``other statistical mechanics,'' which again shows that framework labels overlap conceptually.

Light-activated diffusiophoretic particles have been compared through pair potentials, radial distributions, cluster size, segregation, and growth \cite{Zheng2023photochromismfromw}. These observables distinguish a final morphology from a dynamical process.

The statistical-mechanical class contains some of the most quantitative work in the review. Nineteen of 28 studies report numerical comparison. Yet, only seven state independent run counts, five name code, and four data. The theoretical ambition is therefore ahead of reproducibility.

\subsection{Active elasticity and odd response}

\subsubsection{Theory and observables}

Active elasticity describes mechanically connected systems in which local activity modifies stress, strain, or mode dynamics \todocite{active elasticity and odd elasticity theory}. In a linear description,
\[
\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\epsilon_{kl},
\]
but activity can permit nonreciprocal or odd components of \(C_{ijkl}\). A deformation cycle may then exchange net work with the active elements.

Seven studies carry the active-elasticity label. Two report quantitative comparison and five qualitative comparison. One was assessed from an abstract, while both quantitative studies were assessed from full text. Four use robotic metamaterials or active solids, two residual platforms, and one a vibration-driven system.

The class is smaller than the 15 studies labelled as active-solid or odd-elastic phenomena because not every active solid explicitly compares active-elasticity theory. Conversely, some theory use concerns transport or flocking in connected structures.

\subsubsection{Quantitative tests}

A self-sensing active beam provides one of the two quantitative comparisons \cite{Chen2020selfsensingmetamat}. The experiment measures inverse decay length, wave number, frequency, and wave amplitude. These are direct response observables. The theoretical claim concerns propagation and localization rather than visual resemblance.

A lattice of motorized vertices provides the second \cite{Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac}. Measured quantities include steady motion, angular frequency, shear modes, displacement, torque, and an odd-response ratio. The system connects internal limit cycles with deformation and locomotion.

These studies show why robotic metamaterials are valuable. Local sensing and actuation implement couplings that passive materials cannot easily realize. The response can be measured while the interaction is changed in software.

Yet, the evidence remains finite-system evidence. A beam or lattice can realize a predicted mode without establishing a size-independent constitutive law. Boundary localization may be the phenomenon, but bulk moduli require a region large compared with the decay and correlation lengths.

\subsubsection{Related theories under other labels}

Several active-solid experiments are coded under agent-based simulation or other frameworks rather than active elasticity. A nonreciprocal rotational chain quantifies directional transmission, attenuation, and spatial decay \cite{Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot}. An active lattice of vibration-driven units measures collective frequencies, mode condensation, and the effect of the active fraction \cite{Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec}. Programmable robot networks test non-Hermitian boundary localization through amplitude, energy density, and eigenfrequency \cite{Zhang2026emergentnonhermiti}.

These studies belong to the same conceptual neighbourhood but test different theoretical objects. Nonreciprocal dynamics, non-Hermitian spectra, synchronization, and odd elasticity should not be merged into one claim.

The evidence supports the realizability of active and nonreciprocal mechanical response. It provides only two explicit quantitative confrontations under the active-elasticity label. Systematic size scaling, independent calibration of local response, and closed-cycle work measurements remain priorities.

\subsection{Other theoretical frameworks and reduced models}

Forty-three studies use a framework coded as ``other.'' Nineteen report quantitative comparison, 23 qualitative comparison, and one none. Five are abstract-only, but all 19 quantitative rows were assessed from full text.

The category includes magnetic-force models, non-Hermitian network descriptions, force balances, reduced-order geometric models, nonlinear oscillators, finite-element calculations, and application-specific control models. Its heterogeneity prevents a common success rate. Still, several patterns are informative.

Field-mediated wheeled robots on deformable membranes are compared through orbit geometry, precession, cohesion, speed, and inter-agent distance \cite{Li2022fieldmediatedlocom}. The model concerns an environment-mediated interaction rather than a standard active-particle law.

Contact-synchronized undulatory robots are described through phase difference, force, collision rate, and relaxation \cite{Wei2020densitydependentsy,Zhou2021collectivesynchroni}. The relevant reduced theory concerns coupled mechanical oscillators.

Magnetic microswarms are compared with analytical or finite-element descriptions through force, flow, velocity, shape, and transport \cite{Wang2022reconfigurablemagne}. Selective control of magnetic helical nanobots uses turning time, orientation, speed, and trajectory accuracy \cite{Goyal2025externallycontrolle}. Such models are apparatus-specific but can still test causality when field calibration is independent.

Non-Hermitian robotic networks provide another distinct class. Programmable asymmetric coupling is related to eigenfrequency, amplitude ratio, displacement, and energy localization \cite{Zhang2026emergentnonhermiti}. The comparison concerns spectral response rather than active-particle statistics.

Reduced-order models can also compress a many-particle shape. Magnetic microparticle swarms are represented through principal radii, aspect ratio, anisotropy, and error measures \cite{Boudehane2026physicsbasedreduce}. This row is coded as quantitative agreement but has no resolved theory label, so it belongs to the eight quantitative studies excluded from the framework table. The example illustrates why the extraction taxonomy cannot replace study-level reading.

The ``other'' category contains useful theory but weakens synthesis. Future updates should split it when a coherent framework recurs. Candidate classes include non-Hermitian dynamics, coupled-oscillator theory, magnetic-force models, and reduced-order shape dynamics.

\subsection{Testing more than one framework}

\subsubsection{Overlap is common}

Of the 126 quantitative studies with a substantive framework, 67 use one framework, 46 use two, 12 use three, and one uses four. Multiple labels may represent a genuine comparison between models. They may also represent a hierarchy, e.g. an agent simulation used alongside kinetic theory.

Agent-based simulation overlaps with kinetic or hydrodynamic theory in 48 studies across the full evidence base. It overlaps with active-Brownian theory in 21, other statistical mechanics in 18, Vicsek-type theory in 17, and active elasticity in five. Kinetic or hydrodynamic theory overlaps with active-Brownian theory in eight and other statistical mechanics in seven.

These overlaps create the possibility of cross-scale inference. Yet, listing two models is not the same as discriminating them. A microscopic simulation and continuum theory may both be tuned to one data set and agree with each other because they share parameters.

\subsubsection{A hierarchy of models}

The most useful multi-framework design has a directional flow:
\[
\text{single-agent calibration}
\longrightarrow
\text{interaction model}
\longrightarrow
\text{agent simulation}
\longrightarrow
\text{continuum prediction}
\longrightarrow
\text{collective measurement}.
\]
Each arrow can then be tested.

Rotating-robot experiments connect microscopic diffusion and rotation to domains and edge currents using both agent and kinetic descriptions \cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove}. Cohesive magnetic chains connect pair interaction, agent dynamics, and collective state through simulation and hydrodynamic theory \cite{Yigit2020cohesiveselforgani}. Geometry-controlled bristlebots connect pair forces and collisions to cluster and order statistics through analytical, kinetic, and agent-based descriptions \cite{Casiulis2025ageometricconditio}.

The strength lies in parameter transfer. If each level is refitted independently, the hierarchy loses explanatory force. The evidence table does not record parameter provenance, so we cannot count how often such transfer occurs. We therefore treat it as a study-specific strength rather than an aggregate result.

\subsubsection{Model discrimination}

Direct comparison between competing stochastic descriptions remains rare. Programmable microscopic swimmers offer a route because persistence and chirality can be controlled while the same particles are retained \cite{Raghavendra2026programmablepersist}. A linked robotic pair offers measured run and tumble statistics in a macroscopic system \cite{Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc}. Such experiments could calibrate competing descriptions to one observable and test them on another.

Alignment models provide a second opportunity. Metric, topological, visual, and communication neighbourhoods can produce similar mean polarization. They predict different sensitivity to density, occlusion, and delay. Visual robot systems already report polarization, milling, opacity, and collision rate \cite{Castro2025visualcollectivebe}. A prospective comparison could determine which interaction rule survives changes in visibility.

Active-solid experiments offer a third opportunity. Agent-based models, active elasticity, and non-Hermitian network descriptions may agree on a localized mode but differ in transient response or work. Measuring both deformation cycles and spectral response could distinguish them.

Robotic experiments are most valuable when the intervention is chosen because models disagree. Designing a controller to produce a known state uses theory as engineering. Designing a perturbation to separate explanations uses robots as experimental physics.

\subsection{What does the reported agreement establish?}

\subsubsection{The extracted category is ordinal}

The agreement field records how theory and experiment were compared. It does not provide a common effect size. A quantitative label may refer to trajectory error, order parameters, distributions, phase boundaries, scaling exponents, response spectra, or transport rates.

These quantities cannot be pooled. A small trajectory error and a correct cluster exponent do not measure the same kind of success. Hence, the review does not calculate a mean agreement across studies.

We instead ask what object was compared. Four classes recur.

The first is a state variable, e.g. mean order, speed, cluster size, or angular velocity. Agreement here is necessary but weak because one scalar can be fitted.

The second is a distribution or correlation, e.g. pair correlation, velocity distribution, cluster-size distribution, waiting time, or power spectrum. Such comparisons constrain more of the model.

The third is a response curve across a controlled parameter. Examples include order versus density, transport versus activity, or residence versus geometry. A theory that reproduces the trend across conditions has greater scope.

The fourth is a transition, mode, or constitutive response. Susceptibility, Binder cumulants, hysteresis, decay length, eigenfrequency, and stress--strain response test more specific theoretical structure.

The evidence includes all four. Yet, the extraction table does not encode them as separate agreement levels. A future update should add fields for parameter fitting, held-out prediction, number of tested regimes, and agreement metric.

\subsubsection{Prediction versus reconstruction}

Prediction requires temporal or logical separation. Parameters are fixed before the evaluation data are used. The included studies rarely state this distinction in a form that can be counted reliably.

A simulation matched to observed trajectories is a reconstruction. A model calibrated on isolated agents and evaluated on collective behaviour approaches prediction. A model fitted at one density and tested at another provides out-of-condition validation. A model transferred to another platform provides the strongest test.

Note that prospective prediction is not required for useful science. Reconstruction can identify sufficient mechanisms and expose missing physics. The problem arises only when a fitted reconstruction is described as confirmation.

The correct language is therefore restrained. The evidence shows quantitative consistency for many specific robot--model pairs. It shows fewer prospective predictions and still fewer transfers across platforms.

\subsubsection{Negative agreement}

A failed model is informative when the failure is measured. Non-Markovian probe dynamics, for example, constrain effective-equilibrium descriptions \cite{Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami}. Boundary residence that departs from a point-particle model can reveal body-shape effects \cite{Wagner2026geometryinducedtra}. Traffic jams absent from an ideal controller simulation can reveal finite-body congestion \cite{Rubenstein2014programmableselfas}.

Yet, the agreement coding does not distinguish success from quantitative disagreement. A row labelled quantitative may contain a model that fails in part of the tested regime. Hence, the count of 126 is a count of numerical confrontations, not confirmations.

This distinction matters for RMP synthesis. The value of a theory is not the fraction of plots it fits. It is the set of conditions under which it succeeds, the point at which it fails, and whether that failure identifies a missing mechanism.

\subsection{Risk of bias and reproducibility of the theory tests}

\subsubsection{Measurement is generally stronger than replication}

Among the 126 quantitative framework-level studies, measurement validity is rated low risk in 118 and some concerns in eight. This is encouraging. Robotic experiments usually report observable trajectories, positions, forces, or order parameters suitable for comparison.

Replication is weaker. Thirty-nine studies receive a low-risk rating, while 87 raise some concerns. Only 45 state a number of independent experimental runs. A dense time series from one realization does not replace independent repetition, especially near switching or jamming.

Agent heterogeneity is rated low risk in 103 and some concerns in 23. Still, nominally identical hardware can drift. Low risk often means that heterogeneity was adequately characterized for the reported experiment, not that the agents were physically identical.

Finite size and boundaries are the largest limitation. Only 12 quantitative studies receive a low-risk rating. The remaining 114 raise some concerns. None is rated high in this quantitative subset, but the imbalance is substantial.

Theoretical claims most affected are phase transitions, continuum limits, bulk elastic constants, and scaling laws. A model may agree within one finite arena while its asymptotic prediction remains untested.

\subsubsection{Framework comparison quality}

The pre-specified theory-comparison domain is rated low risk in 82 of the 126 quantitative studies and some concerns in 43. One field remains unresolved. This shows that the quantitative subset is meaningfully stronger than the evidence base as a whole.

Still, the rating does not certify prediction. It assesses whether the comparison is quantitative and appropriate enough for the reported claim. Parameter reuse, model selection, and held-out validation require a more detailed instrument.

Data and code availability remain poor. Ninety-nine of the 126 studies receive a high-risk rating in this domain, 13 some concerns, and 14 low risk. Twenty name code and 22 data. These counts overlap imperfectly because the domain also considers the completeness of available artifacts.

The consequence is practical. A published agreement curve may be impossible to regenerate. Order parameters can depend on tracking thresholds, smoothing, coordinate transforms, and excluded frames. Simulations can depend on undocumented collision or boundary rules.

\subsubsection{What must be released}

A reproducible theory confrontation requires more than source code. At minimum, it should include
\begin{enumerate}
\item raw or minimally processed trajectories with trial identifiers;
\item calibration data for isolated agents and pair interactions;
\item arena geometry and boundary conditions;
\item controller and firmware versions;
\item theory or simulation code with parameter files;
\item scripts that produce the reported observables; and
\item a declaration of which data fitted each parameter.
\end{enumerate}

This package would let another group locate disagreement. If trajectories differ, the hardware or control layer is responsible. If trajectories agree but observables differ, analysis is responsible. If both agree and the theory differs, the model implementation is responsible.

\subsection{Which theories currently work?}

The evidence does not identify one winning framework. Each theory succeeds at a different descriptive level.

Agent-based simulation is the broadest and most frequently quantitative. It works best when local rules, mechanical interactions, and hardware constraints can be represented explicitly. Its main weakness is flexibility. A model can inherit the controller and fit the apparatus without yielding a transferable law.

Active-Brownian-particle theory provides a useful reduced description for persistence, chirality, boundary accumulation, contact-driven transport, and some clustering regimes. It works best when single-agent kinematics are measured and memory is short. It becomes strained by coloured noise, inertia, shape-dependent collision, communication, and hydrodynamics.

Vicsek-type models remain influential as a design language for flocking. Yet, only four quantitative hardware comparisons are present. Their use is better established than their empirical validity across robot platforms.

Run-and-tumble theory is underrepresented. The present evidence contains one quantitative comparison under that label. Robotic control could test it much more directly.

Kinetic and hydrodynamic theories perform well for density evolution, transport, flow coupling, boundary layers, and coarse collective fields. Their quantitative use is substantial, with 32 studies. Their principal limitation is scale. Closures and continuum assumptions are often tested in modest systems.

Other statistical-mechanical frameworks are numerically strong relative to their count. They capture configuration selection, fluctuation and response, susceptibility, hysteresis, structural statistics, and effective temperatures. Their heterogeneity prevents one general agreement claim.

Active elasticity provides the clearest route to constitutive tests, but the explicit evidence base contains only two quantitative comparisons. Robotic active solids demonstrate feasibility more strongly than universality.

The ``other'' frameworks are essential for apparatus-specific physics. Magnetic force, non-Hermitian coupling, oscillator interaction, and reduced-order shape models cannot always be forced into a canonical active-matter class. Their weakness is fragmentation rather than lack of value.

Hence, the question ``Which theory agrees with robotic active matter?'' is too broad. A better question is ``Which level of description predicts which observable under which intervention?''

\subsection{Directions for decisive theory tests}

\subsubsection{Fix parameters before the collective test}

The first priority is parameter provenance. Single-agent speed and persistence should determine propulsion parameters. Pair collisions should determine contact or alignment. Field calibration should determine magnetic, optical, or acoustic forcing. Collective data should then test rather than set these quantities.

Where fitting to collective data is unavoidable, the study should separate training and evaluation conditions. One density, arena, or forcing regime can calibrate the model. Another can test it.

\subsubsection{Compare models, not only model and data}

A successful model is weak evidence when alternatives were not considered. Robotic experiments can change a feature that competing models treat differently.

For clustering, one may compare attraction with density-dependent slowing. For flocking, one may compare metric, topological, visual, and communication neighbourhoods. For persistence, one may compare active-Brownian and run-and-tumble dynamics. For synchronized solids, one may compare oscillator networks with active-elastic continua.

The experiment should be designed around the observable with maximum predicted separation. This is more informative than comparing several models after the data are known.

\subsubsection{Use boundaries as perturbations}

Boundaries affect most robotic experiments. Instead of treating them only as limitations, one can use them to test theory. Curvature, roughness, compliance, and size can be changed systematically.

An active-particle model predicts a relation between persistence and wall residence. A hydrodynamic model predicts how confinement changes flow coupling. An elastic theory predicts how mode localization scales with geometry. A controller-based model predicts how sensing and turning respond to occlusion.

The same collective state under several boundaries constrains the mechanism more strongly than a larger population in one arena.

\subsubsection{Measure transients}

Stationary order is often degenerate. Different models produce the same mean state. Transient response can separate them.

Useful interventions include switching activity, reversing a leader, changing delay, removing a fraction of agents, deforming the boundary, or applying a pulse. Relaxation time, overshoot, switching probability, wave propagation, and recovery then become theory tests.

Several included studies already exploit such interventions. Leader reversal probes collective response \cite{Dizenhaus2026anonymousleadership}. Feedback delay changes rotational persistence \cite{Chen2024persistentandrespo}. Noise changes boundary-state intensity in a robotic network \cite{Zhang2026noiseenhancedtempo}. These designs should become standard rather than exceptional.

\subsubsection{Test transfer}

The strongest result would hold across independent embodiments. One interaction law could be implemented in controlled colloids, wheeled robots, and vibration-driven particles. Parameters would be nondimensionalized, and the same predicted response would be tested.

Such transfer need not imply identical microscopic dynamics. It asks whether a reduced theory captures the shared behaviour after measurable scales are removed. Failure would be equally valuable because it identifies the embodiment-specific term.

Independent reproduction is particularly important. Repeating a model on another apparatus in the same laboratory tests less than reproduction by another group with another tracking and control stack.

\subsubsection{Report disagreement as a result}

Finally, studies should publish where the theory fails. A phase boundary shifted by the arena, a relaxation time missed by the model, or a distribution with a heavy tail can reveal missing physics.

Robotic active matter is well suited to this programme. The interaction can be changed, the agents can be observed, and failures can often be reproduced. The field should therefore move from demonstrating that a model can create a pattern to determining the limits of its explanatory power.

\subsection{Synthesis}

The theoretical evidence is narrower than the experimental literature but no longer sparse. Among 530 included studies, 126 quantitatively confront robotic hardware with at least one substantive theoretical framework. Ninety-six of these appeared from 2020 onward. The quantitative body covers every major platform class and most extracted phenomena.

Agent-based simulation supplies the broadest evidence. Kinetic and hydrodynamic theories form the second-largest quantitative class. Active-Brownian and other statistical-mechanical descriptions contribute smaller but comparatively quantitative bodies of work. Vicsek-type, run-and-tumble, and active-elasticity theories remain under-tested relative to their conceptual importance.

The central limitation is not measurement. Most quantitative studies measure appropriate physical observables. The limitations are calibration, finite size, replication, and availability of data and code. Numerical comparison often establishes consistency with one apparatus. It less often establishes prediction, discrimination, or transfer.

Still, robotic experiments offer a distinctive advantage. They can implement a theory and then perturb the implementation. They can separate sensing from contact, change delay without rebuilding the body, alter morphology while retaining the controller, or vary coupling while tracking every element. These interventions make theories experimentally vulnerable.

Hence, the main direction is clear. Future studies should fix parameters below the collective level, compare competing explanations, measure transient response, vary boundaries and size, and release the complete comparison pipeline. Robotic active matter will contribute most to physics when a robot does not merely behave as the theory predicts, but reveals exactly where and why the prediction ceases to hold.
