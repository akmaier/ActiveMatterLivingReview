\section{Control, sensing, and programmability}\label{sec:control}

Programmability is the main distinction between robotic active matter and most passive many-body experiments. A robot can change how it senses, decides, communicates, and moves without changing its body. External feedback can even replace part of the interaction law during an experiment. Hence, control is not only a means of producing a collective state. It is also an experimental variable.

Here, we address objective O4. We ask how feedback, sensing, communication, and learning tune collective behaviour. The central distinction is between producing a state and explaining it. A controller may reliably create a flock, vortex, assembly, or transport process. Yet, this success does not identify the interaction responsible for that state. Stronger evidence comes from a controlled intervention that changes one part of the perception--action loop and measures the collective response.

The evidence snapshot dated 2026-10-14 contains 530 studies. Control architecture is resolved for 483. On-board rules dominate with 300 studies. External feedback appears in 88, passive or uncontrolled rules in 65, central control in 17, and learned policies in 13. The remaining 47 records lack a resolved control architecture. These categories identify the architecture judged most salient in each study. They do not describe every layer of the apparatus. An on-board rule can still receive positions from an external tracking system. An externally controlled microrobot can interact hydrodynamically and magnetically while a camera closes the loop. A learned policy may run on the agent or on an external computer. As such, the categories should be read together with the interaction modalities.

Those modalities overlap strongly. Steric contact appears in 308 studies. Communication occurs in 174, programmed rules in 169, and optical or other sensing in 163. Environment-mediated interaction is coded in 146 studies, magnetic coupling in 92, and hydrodynamic coupling in 78. Acoustic coupling appears twice. These counts describe coupled channels rather than alternative mechanisms. In particular, a communicated command does not remove contact, and a magnetic field does not remove hydrodynamic or boundary effects.

The resulting picture is not a progression from simple to sophisticated control. Passive mechanical systems often offer the clearest physical mechanism. External feedback offers the cleanest intervention. On-board rules best expose local information processing. Central control can implement otherwise inaccessible couplings. Learned policies offer the broadest functional search but often the least transparent interaction law. Each architecture answers a different scientific question.

\subsection{Passive dynamics as a control baseline}

The label ``passive or uncontrolled rules'' does not mean that the agents are passive in the thermodynamic sense. These systems are actively driven. The label means that no sensing-and-decision loop actively changes the interaction during the experiment. Motion follows from forcing, geometry, friction, contact, fields, fluids, and boundaries.

This architecture occurs in 65 studies, of which 55 appeared from 2020 onward. Steric contact is coded in 58. Environment-mediated interaction appears in 38, magnetic coupling in 18, and hydrodynamic coupling in 12. Hence, passive robotic active matter is primarily a body-mediated and environment-mediated regime.

The scientific advantage is directness. A vibration-driven rotor converts asymmetric contact and substrate forcing into motion. A magnetic element converts orientation and separation into force. A deformable surface converts agent position into a shared field. One can often state the interaction without reconstructing a hidden computation.

Rotating robots provide an instructive example. Their collective organization, domain growth, and edge motion arise from activity, rotation, and contact rather than explicit communication \cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove}. Bristle-bot swarms can transport a payload without sensing or messaging \cite{Arbel2025amechanicalroutef}. Flexible confinement can couple otherwise independent vibration-driven robots into mobile superstructures \cite{Boudet2021fromcollectionsof}. Magnetic interactions can fluidize boundary clusters in a confined population \cite{Musacchio2026fluidizationinduced}. These experiments show that collective function does not require a symbolic controller.

Passive systems also expose morphology as a control parameter. Electronic-free particle robots use geometry and vibration to select attraction, repulsion, assembly, and transport \cite{Yang2025electronicfreepart}. Chains of vibration-driven robots can synchronize through mechanical coupling \cite{Xia2024biomimeticsynchroni}. A magnetic chain of active bots can develop a beating mode through the combination of activity, elasticity, and magnetic attachment \cite{GuzmnLastra2026emergenceofbeating}. In each case, the body performs part of the computation.

The shared environment may provide the effective communication channel. Two wheeled agents on a deformable membrane interact through the surface they deform \cite{Li2022fieldmediatedlocom}. A flexible ring similarly converts local collisions into motion of the complete assembly \cite{Boudet2021fromcollectionsof}. Such systems have no message-passing network. Still, one agent changes a field that another agent encounters.

This distinction matters because environment-mediated coupling can be mistaken for direct coordination. If all agents respond to one vibrating substrate, part of their apparent coordination may come from common forcing. If an arena deforms, an agent can influence another without contact. Hence, the experiment should separate direct pair interaction, common input, and mediated interaction whenever possible.

Passive systems also provide a useful baseline for programmable systems. A controller can be added after the mechanical response is known. The difference between the uncontrolled and controlled ensembles then isolates the contribution of information processing. Without this baseline, the controller may receive credit for order already produced by body shape or confinement.

The evidence is comparatively strong with respect to theory comparison. Thirty-two of the 65 passive or uncontrolled studies report quantitative theory--experiment agreement. This fraction is higher than in the on-board, central, or learned groups. Yet, only 14 report a number of independent runs. Quantitative agreement in one realization does not replace replication.

The main limitation is hidden variability. Vibration amplitude, friction, battery state, body asymmetry, magnetic moment, and substrate condition can drift during an experiment. A nominally fixed rule may therefore change with time. Passive dynamics are interpretable only when single-agent statistics and forcing conditions are measured alongside the collective observable.

\subsection{On-board rules and local closed loops}

On-board rules form the largest group with 300 studies. These systems place sensing, decision, or actuation logic on the agent. They account for 159 of the steric-contact records, 147 of the communication records, 130 of the sensing records, and 124 of the programmed-rule records. Most therefore combine computation with physical interaction.

A local controller typically maps an observation to an action,
\[
a_i(t)=\pi\!\left(o_i(t),s_i(t)\right),
\]
where \(o_i\) denotes the current observation and \(s_i\) an internal state. This notation is deliberately general. The observation may be a distance, bearing, light level, received message, or collision. The action may change velocity, heading, oscillator phase, adhesion, or morphology. Collective behaviour then arises from repeated application of this local map by interacting agents.

The scientific strength is modularity. One may keep the body fixed and change the rule. One may also keep the rule fixed and alter the sensing range, delay, communication topology, or noise. These interventions are difficult in biological collectives and often impossible in uncontrolled active particles.

Early aggregation experiments already used this structure. Cockroach-like robots converted local infrared sensing and aggregation into a collective shelter choice \cite{Garnier2005collectivedecision}. The measured outcome included aggregate and choice statistics rather than only task completion. Directional information transfer was compared through order, cohesion, accuracy, and travelled distance \cite{Ferrante2012explicitandimplici}. Such experiments make the information channel part of the physical question.

Local rules can also realize interactions inspired by physics. Physicomimetic controllers map sensed distances and bearings to effective forces and lattice formation \cite{Spears2004distributedphysics}. Macroscopic models of light-driven robot aggregation connect local sensing to evolving spatial density \cite{Hamann2008spatialmacroscopic}. These examples work because the controller is simple enough to state and the collective observable is defined independently of the desired final shape.

On-board rules become less informative when several layers are changed together. A new controller may change speed, collision avoidance, communication rate, and neighbour selection simultaneously. If the collective state changes, the causal parameter remains unclear. Hence, a useful experiment changes one element of the local map while holding the rest fixed.

The best on-board experiments exploit perturbations. Sensorial delay can switch phototactic robots between clustering and segregation \cite{Mijalkov2016engineeringsensoria}. A periodically reversing leader can probe response, switching, and stochastic resonance in a robot collective \cite{Dizenhaus2026anonymousleadership}. Body morphology can alter collision response while the electronic core remains fixed \cite{Zion2023morphologicalcomput}. These interventions identify a mechanism more directly than another demonstration of a stable formation.

Local control also permits failure experiments. One may disable a sensor, remove messages, reverse a leader, alter a threshold, or introduce actuator faults. The response reveals whether the collective state is maintained by redundancy, mechanical coupling, or a specific information path. A system that continues after communication loss is physically different from one whose state is maintained by continuous consensus.

Yet, the category contains substantial heterogeneity. Some rules approximate minimal active-particle interactions. Others implement elaborate formation algorithms. Both qualify here only because the included studies report physical collective observables. The same architectural label therefore does not imply the same level of emergence.

A useful distinction is between \emph{state controllers} and \emph{interaction controllers}. A state controller uses error relative to a desired formation or trajectory. An interaction controller specifies only local responses and allows the collective state to emerge. Both can generate a pattern. Yet, only the second leaves the macroscopic state unspecified by design.

The distinction is not absolute. A local attraction--repulsion law may encode a preferred separation, while a formation controller may still exhibit collective modes and transitions. The relevant question is how much of the observed state was specified. Studies should therefore report whether the controller defines a target configuration, a target field, or only a local interaction.

On-board rules are well represented but unevenly documented. Among 300 studies, 126 report the number of independent runs. Fifty-six name public code, and 29 name public data. Fifty-one report quantitative theory--experiment agreement. The remaining evidence includes qualitative comparisons and studies without a theory comparison. Hence, the abundance of on-board systems should not be confused with an equally broad body of validated physical laws.

\subsection{Sensing defines the interaction neighbourhood}

Sensing is often presented as a measurement layer. In robotic active matter it also defines who interacts with whom. A range sensor, camera, photodiode, or received signal selects neighbours and weights their influence. The sensing process therefore becomes part of the microscopic dynamics.

Optical or other sensing is coded in 163 studies. It appears in 130 on-board systems and ten learned-policy systems. External-feedback systems contribute 14, while central controllers contribute two. This concentration reflects the role of local perception in programmable collectives.

Let us consider a simple alignment rule. A theoretical agent may use every neighbour within a metric radius. A robot instead sees through a sensor with finite range, field of view, resolution, threshold, and update time. Occlusion can remove a neighbour. Saturation can merge several neighbours. The measured interaction graph may therefore differ from the geometric graph.

Visual collectives make this problem explicit. Blue-light cues allow underwater robots to coordinate aggregation, synchronization, milling, and search without direct state exchange \cite{Berlinger2021implicitcoordinatio}. The robots react to a physically propagated optical signal. Their interaction depends on visibility and orientation, not only on distance.

Externally reconstructed vision creates another regime. Spherical robots can receive actions derived from panoramic information reconstructed by an overhead system \cite{Castro2025visualcollectivebe}. Polarization, milling, opacity, and collisions can then be measured under a controlled visual rule. The experiment offers precise access to the effective visual interaction. Yet, the camera and processing loop introduce their own latency.

Sensorial delay is itself a tuning knob. In phototactic robots, changing the delay changes the effective spatial response and can select aggregation or segregation \cite{Mijalkov2016engineeringsensoria}. The delay acts like memory in the agent dynamics. It should therefore be measured in the same way as persistence or turning rate.

Sensing can also create an apparent force. A robot that turns toward a neighbour behaves as if attracted, even when no mechanical attraction exists. Conversely, collision avoidance can resemble repulsion. Translating such behaviour into a physical model requires the measured perception--action curve rather than the source code alone.

Source code specifies the intended rule. Calibration specifies the realized rule. A threshold in software does not reveal the physical detection probability near that threshold. A commanded turning rate does not reveal the achieved angular response. In order to derive an effective interaction, one must measure stimulus, observation, command, and motion.

This requirement is particularly important near collective transitions. Small changes in detection range or angular noise can shift an ordering threshold. Sensor heterogeneity can broaden that transition. Occlusion can make the neighbourhood density dependent. Without calibration, a change attributed to many-body physics may originate in the measurement layer.

Sensing nevertheless provides one of the clearest routes to controlled active matter. It permits selective modification of information without rebuilding the agent. The strongest designs compare several sensing laws on the same platform and use the same collective observable. Such comparisons separate embodiment from information.

\subsection{Communication as programmable coupling}

Communication appears in 174 studies. It is most common in on-board systems, where 147 records carry the label. Central controllers account for 13, external feedback for six, and learned policies for two. Communication is absent from the passive category by definition of the extracted evidence.

Explicit communication can decouple the interaction graph from physical proximity. Two agents may exchange state while separated, while nearby agents may be ignored. This capability makes communication a direct tuning knob for topology, range, delay, directionality, and information content.

An early many-robot experiment used broadcast coupling to synchronize internal rhythms and motion \cite{Evans1997areactivecoordinat}. Distributed aquatic buoys later combined positioning and a communication network to implement collective spatial behaviours \cite{Zoss2018distributedsystemo}. Kilobot experiments use local infrared exchange to study collective decisions and connectivity \cite{MarchPons2024honeybeelikecollec}. These systems do more than transmit commands. They turn network structure into a physical interaction.

Communication also permits a clean comparison between explicit and implicit information. Explicit transfer sends a state or direction. Implicit transfer lets another agent infer that information from motion or contact. Experiments comparing these routes can reveal whether order depends on message content or on physical response \cite{Ferrante2012explicitandimplici}.

The distinction matters for robustness. Explicit messages can propagate quickly but may suffer delay, loss, interference, and synchronization errors. Implicit cues are tied to embodiment and may be slower, but they remain available when the network fails. A collective that uses both channels may switch mechanisms as density changes.

Communication topology is often treated as a controller setting. From an active-matter perspective it is an interaction geometry. Metric, topological, directed, and time-varying graphs can produce different fluctuations and response times. A broadcast channel can also create a common input that mimics collective synchronization.

The physical communication layer must therefore be reported. Range, update frequency, packet loss, message age, bandwidth, addressing, and retransmission determine the realized coupling. A theoretical adjacency matrix is insufficient when the hardware network is intermittent.

One useful experimental design records the communication graph together with trajectories. The collective observable can then be conditioned on the realized graph rather than the intended graph. This permits direct tests of whether a transition follows density, degree, delay, or connectivity.

Communication can also implement nonreciprocity. Agent \(i\) may respond to \(j\) without an equal reverse response. Robotic chains use programmed local feedback to produce directional transmission \cite{Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot}. Larger robot networks can similarly implement non-Hermitian couplings through communication and programmed rules \cite{Zhang2026emergentnonhermiti}. Here, the communication architecture realizes an interaction that would be difficult to obtain mechanically.

Such implementations are valuable because the coupling can be changed without altering the bodies. Yet, the programmed matrix should be distinguished from the measured mechanical response. Network commands, actuator dynamics, elastic coupling, and contact all contribute to the final propagation.

The strongest communication studies therefore join three descriptions. The first is the intended graph. The second is the realized stream of messages. The third is the observed physical response. Agreement among these layers supports a causal interpretation. Reporting only the first leaves the effective interaction unknown.

\subsection{External feedback as an experimental intervention}

External feedback occurs in 88 studies, of which 64 appeared from 2020 onward. This architecture is especially common in micro- and nano-agent systems and feedback-controlled colloids. Magnetic coupling appears in 50 external-feedback studies, hydrodynamic coupling in 43, steric contact in 51, and environment-mediated interaction in 31.

The loop has three parts. An external instrument observes the agents. A computer estimates their state and computes an action. A field, light pattern, vibration, or command then changes motion. The loop can act on each agent, on selected agents, or on the population as a whole.

This architecture provides unusually direct control of microscopic parameters. Optical feedback can change propulsion direction or speed. Magnetic feedback can select assembly, orientation, and transport. Global vibration can tune activity. The physical agents still collide and couple through the environment, but the experimenter can change one programmed contribution in real time.

Feedback-controlled colloids show the potential clearly. Real-time video feedback can produce stable rotating and polar states in light-driven particles \cite{Buerle2020formationofstable}. Adaptive delay can change persistence and response in rotating groups \cite{Chen2024persistentandrespo}. Related optical systems use programmed alignment to explore switching among collective states \cite{Chen2024synchronousandfull}. Laser control combined with hydrodynamic coupling can tune synchronization and spatial organization together \cite{Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw}.

The main scientific contribution is not the ability to create a striking pattern. It is the controlled relation between a programmed rule and a measured response. Rotational order, polarization, susceptibility, switching, relaxation, and cluster statistics make the feedback law experimentally testable.

Feedback also extends to larger and more heterogeneous platforms. Global vibration changes collision-induced aggregation in a large bristle-robot population \cite{Hao2022controllingcollisio}. External visual processing can implement collective visual interactions in wheeled robots \cite{Castro2025visualcollectivebe}. Predictive control can maintain aerial collective motion under obstacles and safety constraints \cite{Soria2021predictivecontrolo}. These examples share an off-agent observation-and-action loop even though the physical mechanisms differ.

Magnetic systems use feedback in another way. Identical helical nanobots can be selectively steered by combining observation with intermittent randomization \cite{Goyal2025externallycontrolle}. Magnetic cuboid robots can assemble and move under visual feedback and field control \cite{Rogowski2026coordinatedselfass}. Related modular millibots use closed-loop vision for assembly, navigation, and wall climbing \cite{Bhattacharjee2026closedloopselfass}. The controller tunes both configuration and locomotion.

A feedback loop can, however, obscure native interaction. Suppose a camera observes every particle and the controller computes every propulsion direction. The apparent alignment may then reside in software rather than in the particles. Hydrodynamic and steric interactions still act, but their role may be secondary.

This is not a reason to reject the experiment. It changes the claim. The system demonstrates a programmable physical realization of an interaction law. It does not show that the same state would arise from the particles without feedback.

The architecture becomes most informative when the native and programmed contributions are separated. One can first measure motion with the feedback term disabled. The programmed interaction can then be added at several strengths. The resulting response curve reveals whether feedback supplements, opposes, or overwhelms physical coupling.

Latency requires similar treatment. Measurement, computation, communication, and actuation each add delay. Controlled delay can be the experimental variable, as in adaptive-feedback studies \cite{Chen2024persistentandrespo}. Unmeasured delay instead becomes a confounder. A state interpreted as inertial or oscillatory may be generated by the loop.

Feedback gain, sampling interval, state-estimation error, and actuator bandwidth should therefore be reported as physical parameters. The same applies to field calibration. A magnetic command is not a magnetic force until the apparatus response is measured.

External feedback presently provides some of the strongest evidence that programmability can test causality. Thirty-three of 88 studies report quantitative theory--experiment agreement. Yet, only 29 report independent-run counts, five name public code, and eight name public data. The interaction law may be precisely controlled but difficult to reproduce when the complete loop is unavailable.

\subsection{Central control and mixed reality}

Central control is resolved in 17 studies. Thirteen use communication and 11 use programmed rules. Only one reports quantitative theory--experiment agreement, and three report independent-run counts.

A central controller has access to group-level information and computes actions for several agents. This permits global optimization, synchronized actuation, and direct implementation of collective fields. It can also suppress fluctuations that would otherwise reveal the many-body dynamics.

Centralization is not intrinsically incompatible with active matter. The relevant issue is the scientific claim. A centrally generated coupling can serve as a physical simulator. It can realize an interaction graph or continuum field and test the resulting mechanical response. Yet, it does not demonstrate that the same state emerges from local decisions.

Robotic swarmalators illustrate the constructive use of central control. Coupled spatial and phase dynamics can be implemented in a physical robot population and compared through spatial-phase and coherence observables \cite{Beattie2025realizingemergentc}. The controller realizes the coupling, while the hardware contributes motion constraints, contact, delay, and measurement noise.

Mixed reality extends this approach. Physical robots can interact with a larger virtual population through a centrally maintained field \cite{Maffettone2024mixedrealityenviro}. Such systems address scaling without constructing every agent. They can test whether a physical subset follows a predicted density field or collective response.

Yet, a mixed population contains two kinds of agent. Virtual units obey the programmed model exactly. Physical units do not. The experiment should therefore report observables separately for both groups and quantify the influence of virtual agents on physical ones.

The boundary between central control and external feedback is not perfectly represented by one extracted label. For example, predictive control of an aerial swarm is coded as external feedback because an off-board loop observes and commands the agents, although the controller itself is centralized \cite{Soria2021predictivecontrolo}. This case reveals a limitation of a single-valued architecture field.

A more complete description would separate four questions. Where is the state measured? Where is the decision computed? How is information transmitted? Which actuator receives the command? The present taxonomy combines these layers into one principal label.

Central control is most useful as an experimental instrument when it implements a known coupling and leaves the resulting collective state open. It is less informative when it directly prescribes the target trajectory or formation. In the latter case, the main result concerns controller performance unless an independent collective observable is analysed.

\subsection{Learned policies}

Learned policies form the smallest resolved architecture with 13 studies. Twelve appeared from 2020 onward. All 13 were assessed from full text. Ten use optical or other sensing, nine involve steric contact, two use communication, and one carries the programmed-rule label.

The small evidence base already spans several forms of learning. Vision-based policies have been transferred to physical flying robots \cite{Schilling2019learningvisionbase}. Decentralized reinforcement learning has been used for collective motion and formation in aerial systems \cite{Batra2021decentralizedcontro}. Graph-neural-network policies have been deployed on communicating wheeled robots \cite{Blumenkamp2022aframeworkforreal}. Other systems use imitation learning, spiking networks, or learned colloidal control \cite{Kraus2026generativeadversari,Zhu2025amillingswarmofg,Lffler2023collectiveforaging}.

Learning changes the role of programmability. In a hand-written controller, the interaction rule is specified before the experiment. In a learned controller, optimization selects a rule from data, simulation, demonstration, or reward. The collective behaviour is therefore programmed indirectly.

This indirect specification can reveal solutions that a designer did not anticipate. It can also absorb hardware constraints during training. A policy may learn to compensate for actuation error, limited vision, or collision dynamics. Such compensation can improve transfer to hardware.

Yet, the same flexibility weakens interpretation. A learned policy can combine many cues in a nonlinear way. Observing a flock or vortex does not reveal which cue maintains it. The policy may exploit a simulator artifact, a fixed arena feature, or a correlation specific to the training distribution.

The question for active-matter physics is therefore not only whether learning succeeds. We must ask what effective interaction it creates. This requires perturbations after training. One may mask a sensory channel, vary density, change the arena, delay observations, remove communication, or alter body dynamics. The response reveals whether the policy learned a transferable collective law or a narrow solution.

Communication-free learned control offers one useful test. If agents coordinate from local sensing and contact alone, one can compare the resulting effective neighbourhood with an explicitly communicating policy \cite{Choi2026communicationfreec}. Similarly, a graph-based policy can be tested while changing graph density, packet loss, or population size \cite{Blumenkamp2022aframeworkforreal}. Such experiments convert generalization into a physical observable.

Imitation learning raises a different question. A policy derived from demonstrations may reproduce trajectories without reproducing the mechanism that generated them \cite{Kraus2026generativeadversari}. Agreement in path shape is therefore weaker than agreement in response to a new perturbation.

Reinforcement learning can also move the control law outside conventional robotics. In feedback-controlled colloids, learning may choose how particles are driven rather than how a digital robot turns \cite{Lffler2023collectiveforaging}. The physical coupling remains hydrodynamic and steric, while the learned layer selects an intervention.

The extracted evidence does not yet support a broad conclusion about learned collective physics. Only one of the 13 studies reports quantitative theory--experiment agreement. Seven report independent-run counts, five name public code, and two name public data. These figures do not assess engineering performance. They show that learning has rarely been used to confront a physical theory quantitatively.

The coding also hides implementation details. A study labelled ``learned policy'' may run the policy on-board, centrally, or through external feedback. Hence, future extraction and reporting should distinguish the learning method from the execution location.

A convincing learned-policy experiment would include at least four comparisons. It would compare with a simple hand-written rule, test conditions outside training, ablate observations, and measure the induced interaction or response function. Releasing the policy weights alone is insufficient. The training environment, reward, randomization, observation normalization, and hardware interface are also part of the experimental method.

Learning is therefore a promising tuning knob but not yet a mature route to interpretable active matter. Its strongest role may be hypothesis generation. A learned policy can discover a robust collective strategy. A reduced model can then identify the interaction responsible for that strategy. The final physical claim should rest on the reduced and experimentally tested mechanism, not on the optimization score.

\subsection{Delay, noise, morphology, and boundaries as shared control parameters}

The architecture labels organize the evidence, but several tuning knobs cross all architectures. Delay, noise, morphology, and boundaries are particularly important because they alter both information processing and physical response.

Delay appears in sensing, communication, external feedback, and learned inference. It changes the age of the information used for action. At short delay, a controller may approximate an instantaneous interaction. At longer delay, the same rule can produce overshoot, oscillation, switching, or loss of order.

Controlled-delay studies demonstrate that delay can be a parameter rather than a nuisance. Sensorial delay selects spatial organization in phototactic robots \cite{Mijalkov2016engineeringsensoria}. Adaptive feedback delay changes persistence and responsiveness in colloidal swarms \cite{Chen2024persistentandrespo}. These experiments are valuable because they perturb time while retaining the platform.

A dimensionless delay is often more informative than a latency in seconds. One may compare delay with the turning time, persistence time, collision time, oscillator period, or time required to cross an interaction range. The same absolute delay can be negligible in one apparatus and dominant in another.

Noise can also be programmed. It may enter actuation, sensing, communication, or the decision rule. Anonymous-leadership experiments use noise together with periodic reversal to reveal stochastic resonance in a collective response \cite{Dizenhaus2026anonymousleadership}. Such designs turn variability into a controlled intervention.

Yet, injected noise should be distinguished from uncontrolled heterogeneity. A programmed random variable has a known distribution. Battery variation, wheel slip, sensor bias, and body asymmetry do not. A noise-response curve is interpretable only when the background variability is characterized.

Morphology changes the perception--action loop without changing software. A shell can alter collisions, persistence, and alignment. Morphobots exploit this route to combine body design with decentralized adaptation \cite{Zion2023morphologicalcomput}. Electronic-free particle robots go further and encode interaction almost entirely in geometry \cite{Yang2025electronicfreepart}.

Boundaries are another control surface. They alter visibility, collision rate, density, and field propagation. A central or learned policy may use the boundary as an unreported cue. A passive system may accumulate there. External feedback may cancel or enhance that tendency.

Consequently, transfer to a new arena is a powerful test. If a collective state survives changes in size, shape, wall properties, and initial condition, it is less likely to depend on one accidental environmental feature. If it fails, the failure identifies a term missing from the assumed interaction law.

\subsection{From tuning to causal inference}

The control literature becomes most useful when a tuning knob supports a causal comparison. We identify five elements of such a comparison.

First, the intervention must be explicit. A study should state which parameter changed in the sensing, communication, decision, or actuation layer. A new controller version is too broad unless its differences are decomposed.

Second, the local response should be calibrated. For a sensing rule, this means detection probability and angular or range dependence. For a field-driven system, it means field and propulsion response. For communication, it means the realized graph and delay. For morphology, it means single-agent motion and pair collisions.

Third, the collective observable must be chosen before interpreting the state. Polarization, rotational order, cluster distributions, susceptibility, transport, relaxation, and response functions support comparison. A final image does not.

Fourth, the design should include a counterfactual. The relevant comparison may disable feedback, remove communication, change delay, randomize a leader, or substitute a simple controller. This reveals what the programmable layer contributes.

Fifth, the experiment should be repeated and archived. Control code is part of the apparatus. So are policy weights, communication logs, calibration scripts, and camera-to-actuator timing. Without these items, the effective interaction cannot be reconstructed.

The current reporting record leaves substantial room for improvement. Among the resolved architectures, 179 of 483 studies report independent-run counts. Seventy-six name public code, and 50 name public data. These counts overlap, and availability differs strongly by architecture. On-board studies contribute most repositories in absolute terms because they dominate the evidence base. External-feedback studies share little code despite the importance of the control loop to their result.

The 47 unresolved control architectures also matter. Eight were assessed from abstracts, and 41 contain at least one field awaiting author resolution. We therefore treat the architecture counts as resolved evidence rather than complete classification of all 530 studies. The named examples in this section were assessed from full text.

\subsection{What works}

Several conclusions survive the heterogeneity of platforms and phenomena.

Passive embodiment works when the scientific question concerns contact, geometry, shared fields, or mechanical coupling. These systems minimize hidden computation. Their strongest experiments measure single-agent and pair dynamics before interpreting collective order.

On-board rules work when the local perception--action map is simple, calibrated, and perturbed. They are particularly effective for testing information transfer, neighbourhood rules, leader response, delay, and the division between morphology and computation.

Communication works as a physical tuning knob when the intended and realized interaction graphs are both recorded. It can change range, directionality, and topology without rebuilding the agents. Yet, network imperfections must enter the model.

External feedback works especially well for causal intervention. It can change alignment, persistence, delay, activity, or field coupling while retaining the physical particles. Its main weakness is that the controller can dominate the interaction it is meant to study.

Central control works as an experimental implementation of a coupling law or collective field. It is less suitable as evidence for decentralized emergence. Mixed reality can extend scale, provided physical and virtual responses are reported separately.

Learning works for discovering and transferring policies. The present evidence is much weaker for explaining the resulting collective physics. Ablation, out-of-distribution tests, and reduction to an effective interaction are required before a learned controller supports a general physical claim.

These conclusions point to a common principle. Programmability strengthens active-matter experiments when it isolates a mechanism. It weakens them when it changes many layers at once or hides the interaction inside an opaque controller.

\subsection{Directions for programmable active matter}

A more useful architecture taxonomy would separate sensing, computation, communication, and actuation. Each layer should have its own location and update rate. This would resolve the ambiguity between central control, external feedback, and externally executed learning.

Experiments should also report the complete timing budget. Sensor exposure, state estimation, computation, communication, and actuator response each contribute to delay. Their sum is not enough when different stages fluctuate differently.

Interaction kernels should be measured rather than inferred from source code. One agent can be presented with controlled neighbours or stimuli. The resulting observation and action distributions define the realized local rule. Collective experiments can then test how that rule scales.

Communication studies should archive message traces. Sensing studies should archive raw or minimally processed observations. External-feedback studies should archive state estimates and commands. Learned-policy studies should archive training configuration and weights. These records connect software to trajectories.

Ablation should become a standard experimental operation. Communication can be removed while sensing remains. Feedback can be frozen while physical coupling continues. A learned policy can be compared with a reduced rule. Such experiments determine which channel maintains the collective state.

Population and arena transfer are equally important. A controller trained or tuned at one size should be tested at another. Boundaries should change independently of density. A successful state in one arena establishes feasibility. A consistent response across arenas supports a mechanism.

Mixed-reality systems offer a practical route to scale. Yet, their use should not conceal the number of physical agents. Observables from physical, virtual, and combined populations should be separated. The coupling between these populations should be calibrated like any other interaction.

Finally, control should be designed around competing explanations. If two models predict the same steady flock, one can choose a perturbation for which their transient responses differ. Delay, leader reversal, temporary communication loss, boundary deformation, and controlled noise are natural candidates.

The goal is not maximal programmability. It is informative programmability. A complex controller that holds a state perfectly may remove the fluctuations needed to test a theory. A simpler controller that exposes response, failure, and recovery can provide stronger physics.

\subsection{Synthesis}

Control in robotic active matter spans a continuum from body mechanics to learned policies. The evidence does not support one universally superior architecture. Passive systems offer physical transparency. On-board rules offer local programmability. External feedback offers precise intervention. Central control offers access to designed couplings. Learning offers functional search.

Yet, these benefits address different questions. State stabilization asks whether a controller can produce a collective outcome. Active-matter synthesis asks how interaction, activity, noise, and environment produce that outcome. The two questions meet only when the controller is treated as part of the physical system.

The strongest studies expose that part. They measure the realized interaction, vary one control parameter, record a collective response, and compare with a counterfactual. They also report timing, calibration, repetitions, and the code required to reconstruct the loop.

Hence, programmability is most valuable when it makes a mechanism experimentally vulnerable. Robotic active matter can then move beyond reproducing known patterns. It can determine which information channels are necessary, which mechanical couplings are sufficient, and where a collective law stops working.
