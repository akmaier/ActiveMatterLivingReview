\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Living systems are not required for matter to move
collectively. A suspension of driven particles, a
population of self-propelled agents, or an actuated
mechanical network can exhibit organization sustained
by the continuous expenditure of energy. These systems
belong to the broad domain of active matter, whose
central question is how microscopic activity and
interactions give rise to collective behavior far
from thermodynamic equilibrium
\cite{Marchetti2013hydrodynamics}
\cite{Ramaswamy2010mechanics}
\cite{Bechinger2016active}.

The resulting phenomena are remarkably diverse.
Persistent motion can produce coherent flocks,
circulating structures, clustering, anomalous
transport, and density-dependent organization.
Driven constituents may synchronize their motion,
assemble into functional structures, or develop
mechanical responses unavailable to passive
equilibrium materials. Yet, similar patterns
can arise from very different interactions.
Observing a flock, for example, does not
by itself determine whether its coherence
comes from alignment, collisions,
communication, or external control.

This ambiguity lies at the heart of
experimental active-matter physics.
Theories often begin with deliberately
simple ingredients: self-propulsion,
noise, steric exclusion, alignment,
or nonreciprocal forces. Their value
comes from predicting collective
consequences that cannot be read
directly from the microscopic rules.
However, experimental systems
rarely realize these assumptions
in isolation.

Biological collectives contain
adaptive behavior and heterogeneous
individuals. Synthetic active
particles can exhibit coupled
chemical, hydrodynamic, steric,
and boundary-mediated interactions.
Even when their trajectories
are measured accurately, the
effective microscopic interaction
law may remain difficult
to identify.

Robots offer a complementary route.
They are physical active agents
whose sensing, actuation, and
interaction rules can often
be specified or modified
deliberately. At the macroscopic
scale, mobile robots can implement
local alignment, communication,
or density-dependent motion.
At smaller scales, externally
controlled robotic particles
and feedback-mediated colloids
can realize programmable
collective interactions.
Mechanically driven robots
and active structures provide
access to behavior arising
through contact, geometric
constraints, and internal
actuation.

This experimental flexibility
suggests a powerful possibility:
rather than asking only
whether a collective pattern
appears, we can ask
which microscopic ingredient
is necessary to produce it.
Robotic systems may therefore
serve not merely as
demonstrations of active
behavior but as instruments
for testing active-matter
theories.

The opportunity is substantial.
Yet, it also requires
careful interpretation.
A robot executing a
programmed alignment
algorithm does not
independently validate
the theoretical consequences
of alignment. It
demonstrates that the
algorithm can operate
in physical hardware.
The more demanding
question is whether
collective fluctuations,
correlations, stability,
or phase behavior
follow from the
implemented rules
without additional
assumptions or fitting.

We take this distinction
between physical realization
and theoretical validation
as the organizing principle
of the present review.

\subsection{Robots as experimental active matter}
\label{sec:introduction-robots}

Active-matter theory often represents a constituent
through a small number of dynamical variables,
such as its position, orientation, and velocity.
The corresponding interaction rules may involve
alignment, repulsion, attraction, or coupling
to a surrounding medium.

Robotic agents can realize several of these
ingredients directly. A mobile robot
provides physical self-propulsion.
Sensors establish an information-dependent
interaction neighborhood. Communication
permits effective coupling between agents
that need not be in mechanical contact.
Local control rules determine the response
to that information.

Early robotic experiments investigated
whether distributed rules could generate
collective motion and coordination
\cite{Hayes2003selforganizedflock,Spears2004distributedphysics,Kelly2004ascalableonboard,Belta2004controlofateamof,Mizuguchi2004collectivedynamics,Turgut2008modelingphasetrans,Navarro2008anapproachtoflock,Hamann2008spatialmacroscopic,Miki2010asimplerulebased,Yamada2011circularmotiongene}.

These experiments established an important
connection between theoretical interaction
rules and physical multi-agent systems.
However, they also exposed the difference
between an idealized particle and a robot.
Real agents have finite turning rates,
imperfect localization, actuator
variability, and sensing delays.
They experience collisions and
boundary constraints that may not
appear in the mathematical model.

Such imperfections need not be
treated merely as engineering
obstacles. They may be physical
variables that change the
collective dynamics. A finite
response time can affect
the stability of an
aligned state. Directional
perception can make
interactions asymmetric.
Collision geometry can
generate an effective
alignment or rotation
without explicit
neighbor communication.

Consequently, robots
can test not only
the predictions of
idealized theories,
but also the
consequences of
relaxing their
assumptions.

A second class of
experimental systems
uses activity generated
primarily through
mechanical interactions.
Vibration-driven robots,
for example, convert
oscillatory actuation
into persistent or
fluctuating motion.
Their collective behavior
may arise through
steric contact,
friction, confinement,
and propulsion variability
\cite{Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Welch2019activereorientation,Deblais2019howtoforceanarmy,Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Xi2024emergentintelligenc,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami}.

These platforms are
especially useful
because collective
organization need
not be prescribed
by software.
The emergent behavior
instead constrains
models of physically
coupled active particles.

Their limitation
is different from
that of programmable
mobile robots.
The parameters
governing propulsion,
noise, friction,
and collisions
can be difficult
to vary independently.
A simple stochastic
model may therefore
capture selected
features of the
motion while omitting
important mechanical
details.

Robotic micro- and
nano-agents extend
the experimental
range toward
field-mediated
and hydrodynamic
interactions.
Collective transport,
rotation, and
reconfigurable assembly
can be investigated
under magnetic,
optical, acoustic,
or related actuation
\cite{Yigit2019programmablecollect,Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic,Sun2021swarmingmicrodrople,Pauer2021programmabledesign,Das2023optoelectronictraje,Rivas2023spatialpatterningo,McGovern2024multifunctionalchir,Sun2024multiplemagnetoopt,Cheng2025selfassemblyandco}.

In these systems,
the distinction
between externally
driven coordination
and interaction-mediated
collective behavior
is particularly important.
A common field
may move many
particles in
similar ways.
That observation
alone does not
establish that
their motion
arises from
mutual interactions.

Feedback-controlled
colloids make
the distinction
experimentally adjustable.
Measurements of
particle configurations
can determine subsequent
actuation, generating
effective interaction
rules that may be
difficult to realize
through passive
forces alone
\cite{Buerle2020formationofstable,Martinet2022rotationcontrolin,Chen2022collectiveresponse,Chen2024synchronousandfull,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw}.

These systems link
microscopic trajectory
measurements with
programmable interactions.
They also show
why the feedback
loop belongs
to the physical
description:
measurement noise,
latency, and
control updates
can alter the
collective state.

Finally, modular
robots and active
mechanical structures
broaden the meaning
of collective behavior.
Their relevant variables
include connectivity,
deformation, stiffness,
wave propagation,
and nonreciprocal
mechanical response
\cite{Li2019particleroboticsba,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Baconnier2022tensioncontrolledc,Yang2024selfamputatingand,Coulais2025activemetamaterials,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe}.

Here, the active
constituents may
form a connected
structure rather
than a freely
moving population.
The experimental
question shifts
from collective
trajectories toward
the mechanical
properties generated
by internal activity.

Taken together,
these platforms
demonstrate why
robotic active matter
cannot be defined
by one hardware
design or control
architecture.
Its unifying feature
is the use of
physical interacting
artificial agents
to investigate
collective behavior
under active driving.

\subsection{From collective demonstrations to tests of theory}
\label{sec:introduction-tests}

Theoretical active-matter research provides several
candidate descriptions of collective robotic behavior.
Vicsek-type models emphasize local alignment and
noise
\cite{Vicsek1995novel}.
Active Brownian particle models describe
persistent propulsion and stochastic
reorientation
\cite{Bechinger2016active}.
Run-and-tumble models use discrete
reorientation events
\cite{Tailleur2008runtumble}.
Kinetic and hydrodynamic theories
connect microscopic rules
to density, polarization,
flow, and stress fields
\cite{Toner1995longrange}
\cite{Marchetti2013hydrodynamics}.
Active-elastic descriptions
address mechanical response
in driven structures
\cite{Scheibner2020odd}.

Robotic experiments
can provide physical
realizations of these
frameworks. Yet,
a model implemented
as a controller
and a model tested
against independent
measurements represent
different achievements.

Consider flocking.
A programmed interaction
may instruct each robot
to align with its
neighbors. The resulting
group can display
collective motion
\cite{Turgut2008selforganizedflocka,Vsrhelyi2018optimizedflockingo,Zhou2024anticipationpromote}.
This demonstrates
the physical feasibility
of the rule.
It does not
necessarily test
the predicted
dependence of
order on noise,
density, or
system size.

Conversely, a
mechanically driven
assembly can
develop organization
without a programmed
alignment controller
\cite{Welch2019activereorientation,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Luo2025flockingphasesepar}.
Its collective
statistics may
provide a more
independent test
of a physical
interaction model,
provided that
the underlying
propulsion and
collision dynamics
are characterized.

The same issue
appears in
clustering.
A robot swarm
explicitly programmed
to aggregate
does not thereby
demonstrate
motility-induced
phase separation.
That interpretation
requires evidence
connecting persistent
motion, interactions,
and collective
density organization.

Likewise, a rotating
microscopic assembly
may result from
common external
forcing, while
a spontaneously
selected chiral
state would
require a
different explanation.

The appropriate
experimental standard
therefore depends
on the claim
being tested.

We distinguish
three questions.
What behavior
was observed?
Which physical
mechanism generated
it? How strongly
was the proposed
model tested
against measurements?

A single experiment
can answer the
first question
convincingly while
leaving the others
open.

This motivates
a systematic review
that records not
only platforms
and phenomena,
but also measured
observables,
theoretical frameworks,
agreement type,
and methodological
limitations.

\subsection{Position relative to existing reviews}
\label{sec:introduction-prior-reviews}

Several review traditions provide essential context
for robotic active matter.

Reviews of active matter have developed
the theoretical language of
self-propulsion, orientational order,
fluctuations, phase separation,
and continuum dynamics
\cite{Ramaswamy2010mechanics}
\cite{Marchetti2013hydrodynamics}
\cite{Bechinger2016active}.

These works establish
the physical questions,
but robotic experiments
are not generally
their sole organizing
focus.

Reviews of swarm
robotics address
distributed control,
coordination,
collective decision-making,
scalability,
and robotic applications
\cite{Brambilla2013swarm}
\cite{Brambilla2013swarm}
\cite{Brambilla2013swarm}.

These perspectives
are indispensable
for understanding
how interaction
rules are implemented
in physical agents.
Yet, engineering
success does not
always require
the observables
needed to test
active-matter theory.

For example,
a swarm may
perform a
collective task
successfully without
reporting polarization,
density correlations,
cluster statistics,
or a mechanical
response function.

Our focus therefore
differs from a
general review
of multi-robot
coordination.
We ask which
physical collective
quantities have
been measured
and what they
establish about
nonequilibrium
behavior.

A third tradition
concerns microscopic
robotics, active
colloids, and
programmable materials
\cite{Wang2021trends}
\cite{Bechinger2016active}
\cite{Bertoldi2017flexible}.

These literatures
often emphasize
specific actuation
mechanisms or
material families.
Their experimental
detail is essential,
but comparisons
across macroscopic
robots, microscopic
agents, and
mechanical structures
remain difficult
without a common
evidence framework.

The present review
addresses this gap.
It does not seek
to replace
theoretical,
robotics,
or platform-specific
reviews.
Instead, it
connects them
through a
systematic assessment
of physical
robotic experiments
and their
collective observables.

The central organizing
unit is the
included experimental
study, not the
robotic technology
or theoretical
model alone.

This permits
comparisons between
platforms, collective
phenomena,
interaction modalities,
control architectures,
and theoretical
tests.

It also permits
an explicit
assessment of
reporting and
reproducibility,
which is necessary
for distinguishing
a successful
demonstration from
a well-supported
physical conclusion.

\subsection{Scope and objectives of the review}
\label{sec:introduction-objectives}

We address physical collectives containing
at least two interacting, self-propelled
or actively driven artificial agents.
The scope includes macroscopic swarm
robots, vibration-driven systems,
smarticles, modular assemblies,
robotic micro- and nano-agents,
feedback-controlled active colloids,
and robotic active mechanical structures.

Eligibility requires a quantitative
collective or interaction observable
from a hardware experiment.
Examples include an order parameter,
correlation function,
cluster statistic,
transport measurement,
pair-interaction statistic,
or mechanical response.

Two-body interaction studies
qualify when the reported
observable concerns
interacting physical agents.
By contrast,
single-agent demonstrations
do not meet the
collective criterion.

Simulation-only and
theory-only work
without the specified
hardware connection
falls outside the
evidence base.
We also exclude
engineering reports
concerned only
with task performance
when they do not
provide a qualifying
physical collective
observable.

Finally, autonomous
synthetic active
colloids without
the prescribed
programmable or
feedback-control
connection are
outside this
robotics-focused scope.

These boundaries
are methodological,
not judgments
about the scientific
importance of
excluded fields.
They establish
which experiments
can answer the
review questions.

Following the protocol,
we pursue five objectives.

\paragraph*{O1: Experimental platforms.}

We map which robotic platforms have been
used as physical realizations
of active matter.

This requires
identifying hardware
classes, propulsion
mechanisms,
interaction modalities,
experimental scales,
and accessible
collective observables.

The aim is not
to rank platforms
by publication
volume.
It is to
determine which
physical questions
each platform
can address.

\paragraph*{O2: Collective phenomena.}

We identify which collective phenomena
have been observed
in robotic systems.

The taxonomy
includes flocking
and polar order,
clustering and
motility-induced
phase separation,
swarming and vortices,
jamming and
crystallization,
chirality and
rotation,
active solids
and odd elasticity,
transport,
synchronization,
self-assembly,
and boundary
accumulation.

We distinguish
the observation
of a pattern
from evidence
for the
mechanism proposed
to explain it.

\paragraph*{O3: Theoretical frameworks.}

We establish which theoretical frameworks
have been tested quantitatively
against robotic experiments
and what agreement
was reported.

This includes
alignment models,
persistent-particle
descriptions,
kinetic and
hydrodynamic theories,
active elasticity,
other statistical-mechanical
approaches,
and agent-based
simulation.

The main question
is not simply
whether the model
resembles the
experiment.
It is whether
the comparison
constrains or
tests the
physical explanation.

\paragraph*{O4: Control and programmability.}

We characterize how feedback,
sensing, communication,
learning, and
other control
architectures are
used to tune
collective behavior.

Control can create
effective interactions
that are absent
from passive
physical systems.
Yet, it can
also confound
interpretation
when an observed
collective state
is directly
prescribed.

We therefore
treat programmability
as an experimental
variable whose
physical consequences
must be assessed.

\paragraph*{O5: Reproducibility and evidence quality.}

We assess the reproducibility
of the evidence base,
including experimental
replication and
the availability
of data and code.

We additionally
evaluate agent
heterogeneity,
finite-size and
boundary effects,
measurement validity,
and quantitative
theory comparison.

These criteria
support claim-specific
certainty judgments
rather than
a simple
count of
positive studies.

\subsection{A living and reconfigurable evidence synthesis}
\label{sec:introduction-living}

A conventional review provides a synthesis
of the literature at one point in time.
Its conclusions may become incomplete
as new experiments and theoretical
comparisons appear.

Living systematic reviews
address this limitation
through continuing surveillance
of the evidence,
planned updates,
and revision of
the synthesis
when new findings
warrant it
\cite{Elliott2014living,Elliott2017living}.

The living approach
is particularly
appropriate for
robotic active matter.
New robotic
platforms may
change which
interaction mechanisms
can be realized.
New experiments
may strengthen
or challenge
a theoretical
interpretation.
Improved reporting
may also resolve
uncertainty in
previously included
studies.

Yet, updating
the bibliography
is only one
part of
the problem.
A useful
living review
should allow
readers to
explore a
heterogeneous
evidence base
from different
scientific perspectives.

The living-review
concept demonstrated
by Matl and
collaborators
provides a
relevant model:
papers are
tagged by
categories within
an extendable,
interactive
database, and
the categories
can be arranged
as reconfigurable
taxonomy trees
\cite{Matl2017vascular}.

The value
of this arrangement
is that the
hierarchy is
not fixed.
Readers can
choose which
attributes define
successive tree
levels and
reorder them
according to
their question.

For example,
one reader may
begin with
robotic platform,
then examine
interaction modality
and collective
phenomenon.
Another may
begin with
theoretical framework,
then compare
agreement type,
control architecture,
and experimental
implementation.

Neither view
changes the
underlying studies.
They offer
different ways
of organizing
the same
extracted evidence.

Related work
in medical
image analysis
has demonstrated
the usefulness
of an online-updated
survey and
associated
literature resource
\cite{Litjens2017survey}.
Together, these
precedents motivate
an evidence synthesis
that remains
current and
explorable rather
than becoming
a fixed
bibliographic
catalogue.

We follow
that model.
The public
website accompanying
this review
hosts the
included-study
database and
a reconfigurable
taxonomy.

Readers can
search studies,
select the
attributes forming
the taxonomy
tree, and
reorder its
levels to
investigate
different relationships
between experimental
platforms,
phenomena,
interactions,
control architectures,
and theoretical
comparisons.

The website
complements
the critical
narrative synthesis.
It does not
replace it.
An interactive
classification
can reveal
where studies
are concentrated,
but it cannot
by itself
establish the
strength of
a physical
explanation.

\paragraph*{Community contributions and scheduled updates.}

The living infrastructure
also provides
a route for
community participation.

Readers can
propose missing
papers through
reviewed GitHub
pull requests.
A contribution
adds one small
YAML file
containing the
candidate paper's
bibliographic
information.

Automated checks
confirm the
submitted DOI
against Crossref
and flag
records that
may already
appear in
the review.

These checks
validate identifiers
and help
identify duplicates.
They do not
determine eligibility.

Maintainers
review the
contribution
and merge
accepted entries
into the
literature database.
The proposed
paper is
then screened
in the next
update using
the same
panel and
eligibility criteria
as records
identified through
database searches.

This distinction
preserves the
review's selection
standards.
Community submission
creates a
candidate record,
not an
automatic inclusion.

The monthly
search process
and community
contributions
are documented
as separate
routes into
the evidence
base.
Following
PRISMA 2020,
community-supplied
candidates are
accounted for
as records
identified via
other methods
\cite{Page2021prisma}.

The review
uses PRISMA 2020
as its reporting
standard
\cite{Page2021prisma}.
The search,
screening,
extraction,
adjudication,
and synthesis
procedures are
documented
for reproducibility.

Each update
will generate
a dated
evidence snapshot
and preserve
the changes
made since
the preceding
version.
Changes in
study inclusion,
taxonomy assignments,
or certainty
judgments can
therefore be
traced to
the underlying
records.

The living
design cannot
repair missing
experimental
measurements
or produce
independent
replication
where none
has been
reported.
It can,
however,
make such
gaps visible
and revise
the synthesis
when stronger
evidence
becomes available.

\subsection{The present evidence snapshot and organization}
\label{sec:introduction-roadmap}

The present review synthesizes
491 included studies
in the October 17, 2026
evidence snapshot.
All descriptive
statistics refer
to this corrected
evidence base.

The evidence
covers multiple
robotic scales,
actuation mechanisms,
interaction modalities,
and collective
phenomena.
Its breadth
makes a
single physical
narrative inadequate.
We therefore
organize the
review around
successive
questions that
connect experimental
realization
to theoretical
interpretation.

Section~\ref{sec:methods}
describes the
search strategy,
eligibility criteria,
LLM-assisted
screening and
extraction,
adjudication,
validation,
risk-of-bias
assessment,
and living-update
procedure.

The evidence-base
overview summarizes
study selection
and descriptive
characteristics,
with the
PRISMA flow
reported in
Fig.~\ref{fig:prisma}.

Section~\ref{sec:platforms}
examines experimental
platforms as
active-matter
model systems.
It considers
what each
implementation
makes measurable,
its physical
advantages and
limitations,
and the
quality of
the supporting
evidence.

Section~\ref{sec:phenomena}
reorganizes the
literature by
collective phenomenon.
This cross-platform
perspective distinguishes
the observation
of similar
patterns from
evidence of
shared physical
mechanisms.

Section~\ref{sec:theory}
evaluates comparisons
between robotic
experiments and
theoretical models.
It emphasizes
quantitative agreement,
parameter constraints,
and the distinction
between implementation
and independent
prediction.

Section~\ref{sec:control}
examines sensing,
communication,
feedback,
centralized control,
and learned
policies as
ways of
changing
collective behavior.
It also considers
how these
mechanisms complicate
physical interpretation.

The discussion
assesses risk
of bias,
reporting limitations,
and the
certainty of
the principal
conclusions.
The outlook
identifies decisive
experiments,
under-tested
phenomena,
reporting priorities,
and the future
role of the
living infrastructure.

Throughout,
we treat
robotic platforms
not as substitutes
for ideal
theoretical particles,
but as physical
systems with
controllable and
measurable deviations
from idealization.

Our central
premise is
that these
deviations are
scientifically
valuable.
They allow
the assumptions
of active-matter
models to
be varied,
challenged,
and tested.

The most
important contribution
of robotic
active matter
is therefore
not the
ability to
make artificial
agents behave
collectively.
It is the
possibility
of determining
which microscopic
rules generate
collective phenomena,
which theoretical
descriptions predict
them, and
where those
descriptions
cease to
apply.
