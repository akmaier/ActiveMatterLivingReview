\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

Robotic active matter provides an experimental connection
between the microscopic rules of driven artificial agents
and the collective behavior of nonequilibrium systems.
Its distinctive advantage is that propulsion, interactions,
sensing, feedback, and mechanical coupling can often be
modified deliberately. Yet, the ability to generate a
collective pattern is not equivalent to demonstrating
the physical mechanism responsible for it. This distinction
is the central conclusion of our systematic review.

The corrected evidence base contains 491 included studies
in the October 17, 2026 snapshot, following the merging
of one duplicate record. Across the five review objectives,
we identify both substantial experimental achievements
and important limitations in theoretical validation.

For objective O1, robotic active matter encompasses
physically distinct platforms. Wheeled or legged swarms
provide direct access to programmed local interactions.
Vibration-driven robots and smarticles expose the role
of collisions, friction, and geometry. Robotic micro-
and nano-agents enable collective behavior under
field-mediated and hydrodynamic interactions.
Feedback-controlled colloids make effective microscopic
rules adjustable, while modular robots and active
metamaterials connect activity to collective structure
and mechanical response.

These systems are complementary rather than interchangeable.
Their scientific value depends on which variables
they control and which observables they measure.
The platform taxonomy is therefore most useful
as a map of experimental possibilities, not
as a ranking of research approaches.

For O2, the evidence establishes that physical
robotic collectives can exhibit flocking,
clustering, coordinated rotation, transport,
synchronization, self-assembly, boundary
accumulation, and active mechanical responses.
However, similar phenomena can arise from
different mechanisms. Programmed aggregation
does not necessarily demonstrate
motility-induced phase separation, and
externally imposed rotation does not
necessarily establish spontaneous
chiral symmetry breaking.

We therefore assign high certainty to the
broad experimental realizability of
collective behavior, but lower certainty
to claims of universal phase behavior
across robotic implementations.

For O3, quantitative comparisons with
active-matter theory are present across
several frameworks. Agent-based simulation
is the most frequent comparator, while
Vicsek-type, active Brownian particle,
run-and-tumble, kinetic or hydrodynamic,
and active-elastic descriptions address
more specific physical mechanisms.

The principal limitation is the distinction
between agreement and prediction. A
simulation that reproduces an implemented
controller can validate aspects of its
physical realization without independently
testing a collective theory. Stronger
evidence requires parameters constrained
outside the collective measurement and
predictions evaluated under new conditions.

For O4, control and programmability provide
a distinctive experimental resource.
On-board rules, external feedback,
central control, sensing, communication,
and learned policies allow researchers
to modify effective interactions and
collective response. Yet, the controller
forms part of the physical system.
Its delays, noise, information topology,
and imposed objectives may determine
the observed behavior.

The most informative use of programmability
is therefore as a controlled intervention:
change a proposed microscopic mechanism
and test whether the collective response
follows an independently specified prediction.

For O5, the evidence reveals substantial
limitations in reproducibility and
reporting. In the corrected 491-study
extraction, 126 studies received low-risk
ratings for replication, but only 39
for finite-size and boundary effects,
38 for data and code availability,
and 82 for quantitative theory comparison.
One study lacks ratings in all six
risk-of-bias domains.

Independent-run counts and accessible
experimental materials are incompletely
documented. These deficiencies limit
independent verification, although
missing reporting must not be confused
with proof of inadequate experimental
practice.

The review itself has limitations.
Its interdisciplinary search cannot
guarantee complete coverage;
some reports were assessed from
abstracts alone; and LLM-assisted
screening and extraction introduce
classification uncertainty. Blind
validation, subsequent adjudication,
and final-label agreement must be
interpreted as distinct stages
of evidence assessment.

The living design addresses part of
this problem through monthly searches,
versioned snapshots, an interactive
reconfigurable taxonomy, and reviewed
community submissions. It will permit
corrections, new inclusions, and revised
certainty assessments. It cannot
substitute for experiments that
measure fluctuations, finite-size
effects, independent repetitions,
and theory-discriminating responses.

We conclude that robotic active matter
is an established experimental approach
to collective nonequilibrium phenomena,
but its broadest theoretical claims
remain unevenly supported. The next
advance will come from experiments
designed not merely to produce
collective states, but to establish
which microscopic interactions are
necessary, which predictions are
quantitatively correct, and where
simplified physical descriptions fail.
