\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Active matter made programmable}

Active matter converts locally supplied energy into motion and collective stress \todocite{foundational reviews of active matter}. Its constituents operate far from thermal equilibrium. They move, collide, align, transmit forces, and modify their surroundings. Collective order then arises from interactions among many such constituents rather than from an equilibrium free-energy minimum. Flocks, clusters, vortices, synchronized states, transporting assemblies, and active solids are different manifestations of this common principle.

The central experimental question is not whether an active system can form an interesting pattern. It is which microscopic process produces that pattern, which variables control it, and where the proposed explanation fails. Living collectives provide rich examples, but their interactions are difficult to isolate. Synthetic particles offer greater physical control, but their propulsion and coupling can be constrained by chemistry, fabrication, or the surrounding fluid. Robotic systems add another possibility. Their sensing, communication, decision rules, morphology, and actuation can be modified independently or in combination. Hence, robots can serve as experimental realizations of active matter in which the interaction law itself becomes an intervention.

This statement requires care. A robot does not become an active-matter particle merely because it moves. Likewise, several robots do not constitute active matter merely because they share an arena. The relevant case is a physical collective in which active agents interact and produce a measurable collective or interaction-level response. The response may be polarization, a cluster distribution, a transport current, synchronization, force transmission, a deformation mode, or another quantitative observable. Task completion alone is insufficient because the same score can result from physically different collective processes.

Robotic active matter therefore occupies a productive boundary. It connects statistical physics with embodied collective systems. It also connects minimal interaction models with sensing, delay, friction, finite body size, and control. These features are sometimes treated as imperfections relative to an ideal particle model. Yet, they are precisely what makes the comparison informative. A theory that survives them has gained experimental support. A theory that fails under a controlled change reveals which neglected mechanism matters.

Programmability is the distinctive experimental opportunity. A wheeled robot may change its visual neighbourhood without changing its body. A feedback-controlled colloid may change its propulsion or interaction while remaining in the same fluid. A vibration-driven robot may change its collision response through morphology. An active lattice may reverse or break reciprocity through programmed local actuation. In each case, one part of the microscopic dynamics can be changed while much of the apparatus remains fixed.

External feedback extends this idea further. It can observe physical agents, compute an interaction, and return commands in real time. The resulting system is neither a simulation nor an uncontrolled material. It is a physical many-body experiment with a programmable coupling. Stable polar and rotating states have been produced in light-driven colloids through such feedback \cite{Buerle2020formationofstable}. Adaptive delay can then change persistence and response within the same experimental architecture \cite{Chen2024persistentandrespo}. These systems show how a control variable can become a physical tuning parameter.

Mechanical embodiment provides a complementary route. Rotating vibration-driven robots exhibit collective organization, clustering, and chiral dynamics through activity and contact \cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove}. Smarticle ensembles convert internal shape changes and confinement into collective transport \cite{Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo}. Passive bristle-bot collectives transport a payload through persistence and mechanical interaction without explicit navigation \cite{Arbel2025amechanicalroutef}. Here, the interaction is not represented only in software. It is implemented by body shape, collision geometry, and the environment.

Robotic active solids move the question from particle motion to material response. Programmed nonreciprocity can produce directional transmission in a mechanical structure \cite{Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot}. Self-sensing active elements can couple deformation, measurement, and actuation \cite{Chen2020selfsensingmetamat}. The collective observable is then a wave, mode, deformation, or effective response rather than a flocking direction. Hence, robotic active matter includes both mobile collectives and persistently connected active structures.

Scale does not define the field. Macroscopic wheeled swarms, vibration-driven particles, smarticles, modular robots, microrobotic agents, controlled colloids, and active mechanical structures all qualify when they satisfy the same physical criterion. Their differences remain essential. A micrometre-scale magnetic agent and a wheeled robot do not represent the same coarse-graining level. Their inertia, noise, hydrodynamics, sensing, and boundaries differ. The purpose of a common framework is not to erase these differences. It is to determine which conclusions survive them.

\subsection{A gap between neighbouring review traditions}

Active-matter reviews have developed the theoretical language of nonequilibrium collective systems. They organize models and phenomena around self-propulsion, alignment, motility-induced phase separation, active turbulence, topological defects, odd response, and related concepts \todocite{major reviews of active matter}. Biological and synthetic realizations often appear together because they illuminate shared physical principles. Robotic experiments enter this literature as useful examples, but the hardware evidence is rarely assessed systematically as an experimental field in its own right.

Swarm-robotics reviews begin from a different question. They commonly organize methods by coordination strategy, communication architecture, task, scalability, or engineering performance \todocite{reviews of swarm robotics and collective robot control}. Their concerns are indispensable for building reliable collectives. Yet, a successful controller does not by itself identify a collective mechanism. Coverage, formation error, or task success may improve while polarization, density fluctuations, collision dynamics, and force transmission remain unmeasured.

Microrobotics reviews add another neighbouring perspective. They emphasize fabrication, propulsion, navigation, biomedical operation, and the control of micro- and nanoscale agents \todocite{reviews of microrobotic swarms}. These topics determine what an apparatus can do. Still, demonstrations of assembly or transport do not automatically establish an active-matter explanation. A common field-driven motion, a hydrodynamic instability, and a local interaction rule can generate superficially similar outcomes.

Reviews of active mechanical and robotic materials provide a fourth perspective. They connect local actuation, nonreciprocity, adaptation, and programmable response \todocite{reviews of active mechanical metamaterials and robotic materials}. Their natural observables are often material properties rather than trajectories. This literature broadens active matter beyond freely moving agents, but it also introduces a different experimental unit and different standards of comparison.

The present review links these traditions through one question: what has physical robotic hardware established about interacting active systems? We include robotics when it measures collective physics. We include controlled synthetic particles when their behaviour is programmable or feedback-driven. We include active structures when local active elements generate a collective material response. We exclude work that remains solely in simulation, reports only single-agent motion, or evaluates a swarm only through task performance.

This boundary is narrower than robotics and broader than conventional swarm robotics. It is also narrower than synthetic active matter because uncontrolled colloids do not enter merely by being active. The restriction is deliberate. Programmability or feedback is what makes those particles robotic for the purpose of this review. It creates an identifiable intervention on sensing, propulsion, coupling, or state.

Two-agent experiments remain eligible. They can measure collision maps, phase response, hydrodynamic coupling, magnetic forces, or information-mediated interaction. Such measurements may provide stronger evidence for a microscopic law than a many-agent demonstration. The lower boundary is therefore interaction, not an arbitrary swarm size. Single-agent studies remain outside because they cannot establish an agent--agent or agent--environment--agent mechanism.

The distinction between state production and mechanism testing runs through the review. A central controller can place robots in a formation. Local interactions can also produce a similar shape. Both results may be valuable, but they support different claims. The first demonstrates controlled realization. The second may demonstrate emergence. A perturbation that distinguishes competing explanations provides the stronger physical test.

The same caution applies to visual resemblance. Polarization can arise from metric alignment, visual interaction, message exchange, leader following, collision geometry, or common forcing. Clusters can arise from attraction, density-dependent motility, confinement, jamming, or field-induced assembly. Rotation can arise from spontaneous symmetry breaking or from imposed chirality. Hence, a common phenomenon label is the beginning of synthesis rather than its conclusion.

Representative experiments already expose this distinction. Vision-mediated robot collectives permit polarization, milling, and collision outcomes to be changed through the information available to each agent \cite{Castro2025visualcollectivebe}. Controlled colloids implement polar and rotating states through a programmed feedback law \cite{Buerle2020formationofstable}. Vibration-driven rotors reach organized and chiral states through mechanical contact \cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove}. The observable may look related, but the causal routes differ.

A critical review must therefore rotate the evidence several times. Platform classes reveal what interactions can be realized. Phenomenon classes reveal which outcomes recur. Theory comparison reveals whether an explanation predicts hardware data. Control architecture reveals which parts of the interaction were designed, sensed, or learned. Risk-of-bias assessment reveals how much confidence those comparisons deserve. A catalogue of devices would miss these connections.

\subsection{Why a systematic and living review is timely}

The evidence base has grown beyond a small set of illustrative experiments. In the snapshot dated 2026-10-14, the review contains 530 eligible studies. Of these, 358 appeared from 2020 onward, corresponding to 67.5\% of the included literature. The 2026 count is necessarily partial, but it already contains 70 studies. A static narrative assembled around familiar examples would therefore age quickly and would risk following disciplinary visibility rather than prespecified eligibility.

The growth is accompanied by diversification. The current extraction assigns 220 studies to wheeled or legged robots, 85 to robotic micro- or nano-agents, and 58 to vibration-driven robots. Smaller classes comprise modular systems, feedback-controlled colloids, smarticles or granular robots, and active solids or metamaterials. Another 99 studies occupy the residual ``other'' class, while 10 records lack a resolved platform assignment. The taxonomy is useful, but it is not final.

This distribution matters for inference. A phenomenon observed many times on wheeled platforms may still lack cross-platform confirmation. Conversely, a small class may provide unusually clean control of an interaction. Feedback-controlled colloids are few in number, but they permit the coupling law to be changed during the experiment. Active structures are also a small class, but they expose force transmission and nonreciprocal response directly. Study counts measure the extent of evidence, not the value of an apparatus.

The field is also heterogeneous in its explanatory depth. Quantitative theory--experiment comparison is coded in 134 studies. Qualitative agreement is coded in 244, while 129 report no comparison and 23 lack a resolved agreement classification. A model name in a paper therefore cannot be counted as a test. It may motivate the design, reproduce a pattern, fit selected data, or make a numerical prediction. These roles must be separated.

Reporting creates a second constraint. A number of independent experimental runs is stated in 188 studies. Public code is named in 78 and public data in 56. These counts do not prove that the remaining studies are irreproducible. They show that important information needed to judge replication or reuse is often absent from the extracted report. A field built around programmable apparatus should make the complete path from interaction rule to collective observable more accessible.

Full-text availability also affects certainty. We assessed 496 included studies from full reports and 34 from abstracts only. At least one extracted field remains unresolved in 140 rows. We retain these studies in the descriptive evidence map, but statements that depend on abstract-only or unresolved fields require explicit qualification. The living design allows later retrieval and adjudication to correct these limitations without rewriting the review history.

A systematic method is necessary because terminology does not define eligibility reliably. The phrase ``active matter'' can describe theory, living collectives, colloids, or robots. The phrase ``swarm robotics'' can describe physical interaction, distributed computation, or task allocation. Some relevant active-solid experiments may use neither phrase. The search must therefore span physics, robotics, materials science, and microengineering, followed by study-level eligibility decisions.

PRISMA 2020 supplies the reporting structure for this process \todocite{PRISMA 2020 statement}. It makes identification, screening, exclusion, extraction, and synthesis visible. Yet, a checklist alone does not resolve the conceptual boundary. The operational criteria must still distinguish physical collective evidence from task performance, hardware comparison from simulation alone, and quantitative confrontation from qualitative resemblance.

The living format addresses the temporal problem \todocite{living systematic review guidance}. Searches are rerun monthly. New records are screened with the same stored criteria and added to dated evidence snapshots. Each update receives an updated PRISMA flow and a change log. Earlier snapshots remain available, so corrected extraction, new evidence, and changes to the taxonomy can be distinguished.

Not every monthly search requires a new scientific conclusion. The manuscript is re-versioned when the synthesis changes materially. Candidate triggers include a new platform class, a newly observed phenomenon, or reversal of a certainty judgement. In practice, a strong independent replication or a quantitative contradiction may matter more than several additional demonstrations of a known pattern. The living review is therefore designed to track changes in evidence, not only publication volume.

\subsection{Objectives}

We address five prespecified objectives. They follow the path from apparatus to phenomenon, from phenomenon to explanation, and from explanation to confidence.

Objective O1 is to map which robotic platforms have been used as experimental realizations of active matter. We examine embodiment, propulsion, interaction modality, control architecture, environment, agent number, and scale. The aim is not to rank devices. It is to identify what each platform makes measurable and which conclusions depend on one realization.

Objective O2 is to identify the collective phenomena observed in robotic systems. The taxonomy includes flocking or polar order, clustering or motility-induced phase separation, swarming or vortex behaviour, chirality or rotation, synchronization, self-assembly or shape formation, jamming or crystallization, boundary accumulation, transport or locomotion, and active-solid or odd-elastic response. Since studies may report several phenomena, these categories are multi-label.

Objective O3 is to establish which theoretical frameworks have been tested quantitatively against robotic experiments and with what agreement. We distinguish agent-based simulation, active-Brownian-particle descriptions, Vicsek-type models, run-and-tumble models, kinetic or hydrodynamic theories, statistical-mechanical descriptions, active elasticity, and other apparatus-specific models. The key question is not whether theory appears, but whether measured observables confront a prediction.

Objective O4 is to characterize how control and programmability tune collective behaviour. We examine feedback, sensing, communication, learning, controller location, and passive physical rules. These variables can produce a collective state, but they can also test causality when one component is changed systematically. The synthesis keeps these roles separate.

Objective O5 is to assess reproducibility across the evidence base. We examine independent runs, finite-size and boundary treatment, measurement validity, data and code availability, and the strength of theory comparison. Reproducibility is treated as part of the physical claim. A collective state that depends on one boundary, one realization, or one undocumented controller supports a narrower conclusion than a response reproduced across conditions.

Together, the objectives ask a single question. Which aspects of robotic active matter are established as transferable physical knowledge, and which remain platform-specific demonstrations? Answering it requires more than counting papers. We must connect each conclusion to the apparatus, observable, intervention, comparator, and reporting quality that support it.

\subsection{Organization and logic of the review}

Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the eligibility criteria, information sources, screening, extraction, risk-of-bias assessment, synthesis rules, and living-update procedure. It also explains how artificial-intelligence tools assisted extraction and drafting under human review. The goal is an auditable chain from retrieved record to synthesis statement.

Section~\ref{sec:results_overview} reports study selection and maps the evidence base. It establishes the temporal growth, platform composition, control architectures, phenomena, theoretical comparisons, and reporting practices. These descriptive results define where detailed synthesis is possible and where missing or unresolved fields constrain it.

Section~\ref{sec:platforms} addresses O1. It begins with wheeled and legged robots, then considers vibration-driven systems, micro- and nano-agents, feedback-controlled colloids, smarticles, modular robots, active solids, and residual platforms. Each class is interpreted through its physical affordances and limitations. Population size alone does not make one platform more representative than another.

Section~\ref{sec:phenomena} reorganizes the same studies by collective behaviour and addresses O2. This second view asks whether flocking, clustering, synchronization, chirality, assembly, transport, boundary accumulation, jamming, and active-solid response recur through related mechanisms. It also separates imposed states from spontaneously selected ones and visual resemblance from shared observables.

Section~\ref{sec:theory} addresses O3. We distinguish inspiration, qualitative reproduction, and quantitative confrontation. Agreement is assessed with respect to the observable actually compared. A model may explain one response while failing at another scale, boundary, or transient. The relevant unit is therefore a model--observable--regime relation rather than a simple count of supported theories.

Section~\ref{sec:control} addresses O4. It treats sensing, communication, feedback, central control, passive rules, and learned policies as physical parts of the interaction. Delay, update rate, field of view, packet loss, and controller location can change the realized dynamics. Programmability becomes scientifically useful when these quantities are reported and perturbed.

Section~\ref{sec:discussion} integrates O1--O5. It evaluates risk of bias, reporting bias, certainty, and the limitations of both the evidence and the review. Strong evidence requires more than repeated observation. It requires independent platforms or groups, quantitative comparison, controlled variation, and sufficient reporting to understand the result.

Section~\ref{sec:outlook} converts the synthesis into an experimental programme. It identifies opportunities for cross-platform tests, prospective model discrimination, controlled finite-size studies, analysis of transient response, interpretable learning, and reproducible release of trajectories and controllers. It also defines what future living updates will monitor.

This organization is intentionally non-chronological. A chronological review would show growth but obscure the relation between apparatus and inference. A platform catalogue would show variety but obscure recurring phenomena. A phenomenon catalogue would show patterns but obscure how they were produced. By examining the same evidence through platform, phenomenon, theory, control, and certainty, we can identify where conclusions converge.

The central proposition is simple. Robotic active matter contributes most when programmability makes a physical explanation easier to falsify. A robot that displays a predicted pattern is an illustration. A robot that changes one interaction, measures the response, and exposes the limit of a theory is an experiment. The purpose of this living review is to distinguish these cases and to track how the balance changes.
