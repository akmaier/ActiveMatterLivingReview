\section{Confronting theory with robotic experiments}
\label{sec:theory}

Robotic active matter offers an unusual opportunity to test
theories of collective motion. In conventional experiments,
microscopic interactions often depend on material properties
that cannot be varied independently. Robots permit us to
prescribe alignment, alter feedback, modify interaction
networks, and change mechanical coupling while measuring
the resulting collective dynamics.

Yet, implementing a theory as a control algorithm does
not necessarily validate it experimentally. Hardware may
reproduce an intended interaction without testing its
collective predictions. Conversely, a model may reproduce
an observed pattern without identifying its mechanism.

We distinguish implementation, qualitative consistency,
quantitative agreement, and predictive validation.
Implementation establishes that a microscopic rule can
operate in hardware. Qualitative agreement concerns
patterns or trends; quantitative agreement requires
comparison of numerical observables. Predictive validation
additionally requires independently constrained parameters
and successful predictions for outcomes not used in
calibration.

The evidence base contains 489 studies in the
October 17, 2026 snapshot. The framework counts
reported below are calculated from the corrected
extraction table, using its supplied citation keys.

Theoretical frameworks were coded using a multi-label
vocabulary. A study could compare several models,
including a continuum theory and an agent-based
simulation. Framework totals therefore cannot
be added to obtain the number of theory-tested studies.

Figure~\ref{fig:theory} summarizes the extracted
frameworks and reported levels of agreement.
It identifies what was compared, not whether
the comparison constituted predictive validation.

test of collective theory.

\subsection{Vicsek-type models and alignment theories}
\label{sec:theory-vicsek}

Vicsek-type models provide a natural starting point
because their ingredients resemble programmable
robotic capabilities. Self-propelled agents adjust
their orientations in response to neighbors and
fluctuations, potentially generating polarization
and collective motion \cite{Vicsek1995novel}.

A representative discrete-time formulation is
\begin{equation}
\theta_i(t+\Delta t)=
\operatorname{Arg}
\left[
\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}
e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_j(t)}
\right]
+\eta\xi_i(t),
\label{eq:theory-vicsek}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal N_i$ is the interaction neighborhood
and $\eta\xi_i$ represents angular noise.
Position evolves through self-propulsion
along the selected direction.

Local alignment may generate global order,
but the theory also concerns fluctuations,
density-dependent organization, collective
modes, and large-system behavior
\cite{Toner1995longrange,Vicsek1995novel}.

A robot swarm can implement alignment rules,
although sensor noise, communication delays,
finite turning rates, collision avoidance,
and boundaries can modify their consequences.
The relevant test is whether measured
collective responses agree with a specified
model after the implementation has been
characterized.

\paragraph*{Experimental realizations and observables.}

The extraction identifies 20 studies comparing
with a Vicsek-type framework. Ten involve
wheeled or legged swarm robots, five belong
to the residual platform category, three
use feedback-controlled colloids, and one
uses vibration-driven robots. One additional
record has no platform classification.

Experiments address programmed alignment,
collective trajectories, orientation-dependent
interactions, and ordered states
\cite{Turgut2008selforganizedflocka,Tarcai2011patternstransition,Vsrhelyi2014outdoorflockingand,Balzs2020adaptiveleadership,Fernando2021onlineflockingcont,Wang2022optiswarmopticals,Xiao2023perceptionofmotion,Zheng2024bodyorientationcha,Jianchao2024emergentchiralitya,Verdoucq2025flockingphasetrans,Zheng2026nestedinteractionn,Dizenhaus2026anonymousleadership,Zhang2026noiseresilientimpl,Yasuda2014selforganizedflockb}.

Extracted observables include polarization,
group orientation, collective trajectories,
spatial organization, and responses to
changing interactions. These constrain
different aspects of alignment theory.
Polarization measures global coherence
but cannot distinguish homogeneous order
from spatially heterogeneous organization.
Correlations and density-dependent
measurements provide further constraints.

Likewise, successful collective turning
demonstrates a coordinated response
without necessarily establishing
the predicted fluctuations or relaxation.

Feedback-controlled colloids provide
particle-scale comparisons
\cite{Chen2024synchronousandfull,Chen2022collectiveresponse,Chen2024persistentandrespo}.
They allow effective orientation-dependent
interactions to be examined alongside
collective order parameters, provided
the imposed feedback law is distinguished
from its physical consequences.

Other comparisons involve nonstandard
or asymmetric interactions
\cite{Fruchart2021nonreciprocalphase,Giomi2013swarmingswirlinga,Liu2025collectivemotionmo}.
Such departures are not necessarily
experimental imperfections. Nonreciprocity,
delayed information, and persistent
turning may require models different
from basic Vicsek dynamics.

\paragraph*{Quantitative agreement and its limits.}

Only four of the 20 Vicsek-type studies
have an extracted quantitative agreement
classification
\cite{Chen2024synchronousandfull,Xiao2023perceptionofmotion,Dizenhaus2026anonymousleadership,Chen2024persistentandrespo}.
Fourteen are classified as qualitative
comparisons, while two have no
agreement comparison recorded.

For D6, four studies receive low-risk
ratings, eleven have some concerns,
and five are high risk. The subset
providing stronger quantitative tests
is therefore substantially smaller
than the literature implementing
Vicsek-type dynamics.

For replication, eight studies receive
low-risk D1 ratings and twelve have
some concerns. Independent-run counts
are recorded for eleven studies.
Explicit code availability occurs
in two and data availability in three.
All 20 studies were assessed
on a full-text basis.

These results establish experimental
realizations and comparisons of
Vicsek-type models across several
platforms, but most comparisons
remain qualitative.

The distinction matters because
implementing the alignment update
can reproduce the mathematical
rule without independently testing
its predictions for collective
behavior.

Stronger validation would measure
the implemented interaction,
polarization, correlations, and
parameter dependence, then compare
them with predictions specified
before observing those outcomes.

Finite-size analysis is especially
important. Alignment in a small
group is compatible with several
mechanisms; fluctuations and
scaling can distinguish
their collective predictions.

We therefore find stronger evidence
for the feasibility of robotic
Vicsek-type realizations than for
universal conclusions about their
phase behavior. Programmable
alignment offers a particularly
promising route to controlled tests
of fluctuations, response, and
scaling laws.

\subsection{Active Brownian particle models}
\label{sec:theory-abp}

Active Brownian particle models provide
a different theoretical description.
Instead of prescribing explicit
alignment, they treat individual
agents as persistently self-propelled
particles whose orientations fluctuate.
Interactions may arise through
steric contact, external potentials,
or additional physical coupling
\cite{Bechinger2016active}.

In a simple two-dimensional formulation,
\begin{align}
\dot{\mathbf r}_i &=
v_0\mathbf e(\theta_i)
+\mu\mathbf F_i
+\sqrt{2D_t}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_i(t),
\\
\dot{\theta}_i &=
\sqrt{2D_r}\,\eta_i(t),
\label{eq:theory-abp}
\end{align}
where $v_0$ is the self-propulsion speed,
$D_t$ and $D_r$ are translational and
rotational diffusivities, and
$\mathbf F_i$ denotes interactions
or external forces.

Even without programmed alignment,
persistent propulsion and interactions
can generate nonequilibrium collective
dynamics. This makes active Brownian
particle models suitable for
mechanically driven robotic systems.

However, their assumptions require
experimental characterization.
Vibration-driven robots may exhibit
intermittent propulsion, correlated
rotation, asymmetric geometry,
and substrate-dependent friction.
These effects can determine
collective behavior rather than
constitute negligible deviations
from the ideal model.

\paragraph*{Experimental implementations.}

The extraction identifies 28 studies
with active Brownian particle comparisons.
Eighteen use vibration-driven robots,
five wheeled or legged robots,
three robotic micro- or nano-agents,
and two feedback-controlled colloids.

The predominance of vibration-driven
systems distinguishes this framework
from Vicsek-type models.
These experiments investigate
persistent motion, aggregation,
transport, confinement, and
collective reorganization
\cite{Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Balda2022playingwithactive,Fersula2026aggregatingswarmst,Edwards2026robusttopologically,Musacchio2026activeembracemente,Hoek2026pistonlikeinformat,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Fersula2026leveragingdesignfo,MuozObreque2026dynamicsofmagnetic,Musacchio2026fluidizationinduced,Callegari2023playingwithactive,Balda2024playingwithactive,Li2021programmingactivec,Siebers2023exploitingcompositi,Son2024emergentfunctional,Zion2023morphologicalcomput,Wagner2026geometryinducedtra,Zion2021distributedonline}.

They connect microscopic propulsion
statistics with collective observables
without necessarily prescribing
mechanical interactions through
individual controllers.

Relevant measurements include
isolated-agent trajectories,
mean-squared displacement,
rotational dynamics,
density distributions,
clustering statistics,
and confinement-dependent
transport.

For example, isolated-agent
mean-squared displacement can
constrain persistence before
collective measurements reveal
the effects of interactions.
Parameters inferred independently
provide a stronger prediction
than those fitted directly
to collective transport.

Wheeled or legged experiments
provide complementary realizations
through controlled persistent motion
\cite{Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Mijalkov2016engineeringsensoria,Mayya2019nonuniformrobotde,Hao2022controllingcollisio}.
Such systems can vary effective
motility and interaction rules,
although feedback may introduce
dynamics absent from the standard
active Brownian equations.

Microscopic and colloidal systems
extend these comparisons toward
field-mediated and feedback-controlled
interactions
\cite{Rivas2023spatialpatterningo,Raghavendra2026programmablepersist,Desgranges2023microswimmersunder,Knippenberg2022roleofcohesionin,Guo2026afeedbackcontrolle}.

Persistent motion and effective
interactions can be characterized
at smaller length scales.
Nevertheless, common external
forcing and hydrodynamic coupling
may produce correlations that
simple active Brownian models
do not represent.

\paragraph*{Quantitative evidence.}

Nineteen of the 28 studies report
quantitative theory--experiment
agreement, while nine report
qualitative agreement. The
quantitative fraction is markedly
higher than in the Vicsek-type
category.

The quantitative subset includes
mechanically driven systems,
programmable colloids, and
mobile robotic experiments
\cite{Balda2022playingwithactive,Musacchio2026activeembracemente,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Knippenberg2022roleofcohesionin,Raghavendra2026programmablepersist,Desgranges2023microswimmersunder,Guo2026afeedbackcontrolle,Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc,Musacchio2026fluidizationinduced,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Mijalkov2016engineeringsensoria,Hao2022controllingcollisio,Callegari2023playingwithactive,Balda2024playingwithactive,Li2021programmingactivec,Siebers2023exploitingcompositi,Zion2023morphologicalcomput,Wagner2026geometryinducedtra,Zion2021distributedonline}.

For D6, twelve studies receive
low-risk ratings, fourteen have
some concerns, and two are high
risk. Thus, quantitative comparisons
are comparatively well represented,
although their methodological
strength varies.

Replication evidence is weaker.
Only six studies receive low-risk
D1 ratings, twenty have some
concerns, and two are high risk.
Independent-run counts are
recorded for eight studies.

Explicit code availability
occurs in six studies,
and data availability in seven.
One study was assessed from
its abstract alone, limiting
assessment of detailed
experimental parameters.

The evidence supports the usefulness
of active Brownian particle models
for describing several robotic
experiments. However, the extraction
does not systematically distinguish
fitted parameters from independently
measured ones. The nineteen
quantitative comparisons therefore
cannot be equated with nineteen
independent predictive validations.

\paragraph*{What would distinguish competing descriptions?}

The most informative tests begin
with isolated-agent measurements
of propulsion speed,
orientation persistence,
and fluctuations.

Particle density and confinement
can then be varied while retaining
the independently determined
parameters. For clustering,
this distinguishes motility-induced
organization from direct attraction.
For transport, it tests whether
mobility changes follow from
persistence and interactions.
For boundary accumulation,
it connects measured density
profiles to single-agent dynamics.

A demanding comparison would
predict several observables
with one parameterization.
Agreement with mean-squared
displacement, density correlations,
and confinement response without
separate fitting would provide
stronger evidence than agreement
with one trajectory distribution.

Effective noise assumptions
also require testing.
Correlated or non-Gaussian
fluctuations may allow
a standard active Brownian
model to describe long-time
transport while failing
at shorter times.
Such discrepancies identify
the model's domain of
applicability and indicate
when additional physical
degrees of freedom matter.

We therefore regard active
Brownian particle models
as one of the better-supported
quantitative frameworks in
robotic active matter.
Their connection to measurable
single-particle dynamics
makes them suitable for
discriminating experiments.

The outstanding challenge is
to move beyond agreement
with selected observables
toward parameter-constrained
predictions of collective
behavior.

\subsection{Run-and-tumble descriptions}
\label{sec:theory-run-tumble}

Run-and-tumble models describe active motion as alternating
episodes of persistent displacement and reorientation.
They provide an alternative to continuous rotational
diffusion. In the simplest formulation, an agent moves
at approximately constant speed during a run and changes
direction through discrete reorientation events
\cite{Tailleur2008runtumble}.

A characteristic observable is the distribution
of run durations. If reorientations occur as
a Poisson process with rate $\alpha$,
the probability that a run persists
longer than time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
S(t)=\exp(-\alpha t).
\label{eq:theory-run-tumble}
\end{equation}
This is a model prediction, not a general
property of robotic run-and-tumble systems.
Nonexponential waiting times, correlated
turning angles, and state-dependent
reorientation require other descriptions.

The distinction from active Brownian motion
lies in the statistics of orientation changes.
Both descriptions can generate persistent
trajectories and enhanced long-time spreading.
Agreement with mean-squared displacement alone
may therefore not distinguish them.
Run-duration distributions, turning events,
and orientation correlations provide
more discriminating observables,
especially when measured independently
of collective behavior.

Only three studies explicitly compare
with a run-and-tumble framework.
Two involve vibration-driven robots
and one a robotic micro- or nano-agent
system
\cite{Noirhomme2024brainbotsassmarta,Raghavendra2026programmablepersist,Balda2024playingwithactive}.

This evidence base is small relative
to Vicsek-type and active Brownian
comparisons, but it connects robotic
control directly to stochastic motility.
Discrete reorientation can arise
through programmed motor commands,
changes in actuation, or
interaction-mediated turning.

Implementing such sequences does not,
however, independently validate
run-and-tumble theory. A stronger
test asks whether measured run and
turn statistics predict collective
transport, response, or spatial
organization without fitting those
outcomes separately.

The three studies include two
quantitative comparisons and
one qualitative comparison.
Two receive low-risk D6 ratings,
while one is rated high risk.

For replication, all three have
some concerns in D1.
An independent-run count is
recorded for one study.
One explicitly reports code
availability, and one reports
data availability.
All three were assessed
on full text.

Thus, run-and-tumble descriptions
have received quantitative tests
in a limited set of robotic systems,
but the evidence does not establish
cross-platform robustness.

The principal opportunity is a
controlled comparison between
continuous orientational diffusion
and discrete reorientation.
Robots permit different turning
statistics to be implemented
on otherwise similar hardware.

A decisive experiment would
characterize single-agent orientation
dynamics, then test whether those
parameters predict collective
observables across density,
interaction, and confinement.
Measurements sensitive to the
full distribution of reorientation
events, rather than only the
average turning rate, could
identify when run-and-tumble
and active Brownian descriptions
are experimentally distinguishable.

We therefore regard run-and-tumble
models as promising candidates
for discriminating experiments,
rather than broadly validated
explanations of robotic
collective behavior.

\subsection{Kinetic and hydrodynamic theories}
\label{sec:theory-kinetic}

Kinetic and hydrodynamic theories connect microscopic
interactions to collective fields such as density,
polarization, velocity, and stress. They permit
predictions of collective transport, fluctuations,
stability, and spatial organization beyond the
motion of individual agents
\cite{Toner1995longrange,Marchetti2013hydrodynamics}.

For a conserved population, the density
$\rho(\mathbf r,t)$ obeys
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho+
\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0,
\label{eq:theory-continuity}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf J$ is the particle current.
A common active contribution is
$\mathbf J_{\mathrm a}=v_0\rho\mathbf p$,
with $\mathbf p$ denoting local polarization.
Additional equations specify orientation,
velocity, or stress dynamics.

The central challenge is to test the constitutive
relations closing these equations. Different
microscopic interactions can produce similar
macroscopic flows, while small changes in an
interaction rule may alter collective stability.
Agreement with a measured velocity field
therefore need not validate the proposed closure.

Robotic experiments can provide both microscopic
trajectories and coarse-grained measurements.
In principle, independently characterized
interactions can constrain a continuum model
whose predictions are then tested against
density and velocity fields.

This requires sufficient scale separation
between the microscopic interaction length
and collective spatial variations.
Many robotic systems contain relatively few
agents, making the continuum approximation
itself an important experimental question.

\paragraph*{Experimental coverage.}

The extraction identifies 65 studies comparing
with kinetic or hydrodynamic theory.
Robotic micro- and nano-agents contribute 28,
vibration-driven robots 14,
and wheeled or legged robots eleven.
Five belong to the residual platform category,
three involve smarticles or granular robots,
two feedback-controlled colloids,
and one a modular system.
One study has no recorded platform class.

Microscopic robotic assemblies form the largest
group, reflecting the importance of fluid-mediated
interactions and collective transport at small scales.

The included experiments investigate collective
propulsion, field-driven motion, hydrodynamic
coupling, and reconfigurable assemblies
\cite{Sun2021swarmingmicrodrople,Calabr2026interactiondrivena,Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic,Pauer2021programmabledesign,Yigit2019programmablecollect,Das2023optoelectronictraje,McGovern2024multifunctionalchir,Sun2024multiplemagnetoopt,Liu2022bionicmorphological,Xu2023multiplecilialike,Yue2022wheellikemagnetic,Wang2018collectivebehavior,Chen2022programmablespatio,Xu2022collectivebehaviors,Baker2019shapeprogrammed3d,Goyal2025externallycontrolle,Aubret2018targetedassemblyan,Ahmed2021bioinspiredacousto,Yan2024programmingstructur,Yigit2020cohesiveselforgani,Hanson2025electrokineticpropu,Xu2023bioinspiredciliali,Ji2021ondemandassemblya,Xie2019programmablegenerat,Law2024navigationofmagnet,Yu2022adaptivepatternand,Ceron2026fluidictorqueenabl,Li2022vectorcontrolledwh}.

Their observables include particle velocities,
relative displacement, angular motion,
fluid flows, cluster geometry, and
collective organization.

A measured flow field can test a hydrodynamic
calculation; trajectories can constrain mobility;
and density correlations can test predictions
of collective structure. Yet, reproducing
motion under an imposed field differs from
predicting spontaneous organization.

\paragraph*{Mechanical and macroscopic realizations.}

Vibration-driven robots provide a complementary
regime in which continuum behavior arises
through mechanical contact rather than
fluid-mediated coupling
\cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Casiulis2025ageometricconditio,Deblais2018boundariescontrolc,Boudet2022effectivetemperatur,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Miyashita2007selfassembleofwat,Mayya2019nonuniformrobotde,Li2021programmingactivec,Zion2023morphologicalcomput,Wagner2026geometryinducedtra,Lvay2026collectivedynamics,Zion2021distributedonline}.

Measurements include spatial correlations,
mean-squared displacement, velocity distributions,
collective oscillations, and structural order.
These experiments test effective field descriptions
against mechanically generated collective dynamics.

However, friction and contact forces may violate
assumptions appropriate to conventional fluid
hydrodynamics. The continuum model must retain
the physical mechanisms relevant to the platform.

Wheeled or legged robots offer controlled
microscopic rules and measurements of population
density, collective motion, and spatial organization
\cite{Giardina2022collectivephototact,Fersula2026aggregatingswarmst,Valentini2015collectivedecision,Zhou2024anticipationpromote,Hamann2008spatialmacroscopic,Maffettone2024mixedrealityenviro,Aguilar2018collectiveclogcont,Kernbach2013adaptivecollective,Arvin2011imitationofhoneybe,Prasath2022dynamicsofcooperat,Fruchart2020phasetransitionsin}.

Their advantage is control of the underlying
interaction law. A stringent test derives
collective predictions from that law and
compares spatial or temporal dependence
without fitting each observable independently.

Other implementations address environment-mediated
flows, mechanical response, and hydrodynamic
interactions
\cite{Bo2026threephasesofodd,Newbolt2024flowinteractionsle,Boudet2025effectsofboundarie,Ren2025chlamydomonasinspir,Leech2026spincoupledhydrody,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Guo2026afeedbackcontrolle,Luo2025flockingphasesepar,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,Li2020programmingactiveg,Miyashita2008howmorphologyaffec,Fruchart2021nonreciprocalphase}.

Together, these studies demonstrate that kinetic
and continuum theories have been confronted with
physically different robotic systems.

\paragraph*{Agreement and experimental limitations.}

Of the 65 studies, 29 report quantitative
theory--experiment agreement and 36 qualitative
agreement. None has an agreement classification
of ``none'' in the extracted field.

For D6, 24 studies are rated low risk,
33 have some concerns, and eight are high risk.
Thus, a substantial subset provides
relatively strong quantitative comparisons.

Replication reporting remains less complete.
Twenty studies are rated low risk in D1,
and 45 have some concerns.
Independent-run counts are recorded in 24 studies.

Explicit code availability is recorded
for twelve studies and data availability
for sixteen. One study was assessed from
an abstract alone; the remaining 64
were assessed on full text.

These findings support the experimental relevance
of kinetic and hydrodynamic descriptions,
but not the conclusion that the same continuum
equations apply across all robotic platforms.

Microscopic suspensions interact through
surrounding fluids, whereas dry robots
may interact through collisions,
sensing, or mechanical transmission.
Similar density and velocity fields
need not imply identical constitutive laws.

\paragraph*{What constitutes a decisive continuum test?}

A decisive comparison connects independently
characterized microscopic parameters to
coarse-grained observables.

First, the interaction law should be measured
where possible. For microscopic swimmers,
this may involve pair trajectories or
flow-mediated coupling; for macroscopic robots,
the implemented sensing and control rules
provide a starting point.

Second, continuum fields should be measured
over sufficient spatial ranges. Density
or velocity fields inferred from a handful
of robots may be dominated by finite-size
effects.

Third, predictions should be tested under
controlled parameter changes. Strong evidence
would use one parameterization to predict
several observables, such as density
fluctuations, transport, and confinement
response.

Reproducing one steady-state density profile
is useful; predicting the evolution of
density and polarization after a perturbation
without additional fitting is more decisive.

We conclude that kinetic and hydrodynamic
theories form an important quantitative
component of robotic active-matter research.
Their central opportunity is to connect
programmable microscopic interactions with
measured collective fields.

Progress will depend on systematic continuum
measurements and direct tests of where
coarse-graining and constitutive
approximations remain valid.

\subsection{Active elasticity}
\label{sec:theory-active-elasticity}

Active elasticity addresses collective systems in which
internally generated forces modify mechanical response.
Unlike models focused primarily on particle trajectories,
elastic theories describe the relation between deformation,
stress, and coupled mechanical degrees of freedom.

Active systems can exhibit nonreciprocal response
because their forces need not derive from an
equilibrium elastic energy
\cite{Scheibner2020odd}.

The experimental challenge is to distinguish
an active constitutive property from
the response of a particular controller
or mechanical assembly.

For a linearized mechanical system,
the relation between generalized
displacements and forces may be written
\begin{equation}
F_a=\sum_b K_{ab}u_b,
\label{eq:theory-active-stiffness}
\end{equation}
where $K_{ab}$ is an effective
response matrix. In a reciprocal
passive system, equilibrium
assumptions constrain its symmetry;
active mechanical coupling can
produce nonreciprocal contributions.

The relevant test measures response
coefficients and their dependence
on actuation, geometry, or frequency.
Motion alone does not establish
an active elastic constitutive law.

\paragraph*{Experimental comparisons.}

The extraction identifies seven studies
comparing robotic experiments with
active elasticity. Four use robotic
metamaterials or active solids, two
belong to the residual platform category,
and one uses vibration-driven robots.

Mechanically focused comparisons
investigate nonreciprocal coupling,
active elastic response, and
collective structural dynamics
\cite{Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe,Coulais2025activemetamaterials,Chen2020selfsensingmetamat}.

Extracted observables include
shear response, odd elastic
coefficients, vibrational properties,
wave amplitude, frequency,
and spatial decay. Frequency-dependent
propagation can constrain the dynamical
response, while a measured stiffness
matrix can directly test
nonreciprocal coupling.

Other comparisons involve mechanically
driven or programmed collectives
\cite{Mohanty2025multimodalmotionan,nr2022mindthegappredic,Karagzel2024selforganizedflock}.
Their observables include velocity,
collective order, and spatial response.
These measurements may be consistent
with elastic descriptions without
independently determining their
constitutive coefficients.

We therefore distinguish direct
mechanical response measurements
from indirect comparisons between
collective dynamics and elastic models.

\paragraph*{Quantitative evidence.}

Two of the seven studies report
quantitative theory--experiment agreement
\cite{Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Chen2020selfsensingmetamat}.
The remaining five report
qualitative agreement.

For D6, two studies are rated
low risk, three have some concerns,
and two are high risk.
The quantitative testing subset
is therefore small despite the
physical specificity of the framework.

Replication documentation is also
limited. One study receives a
low-risk D1 rating, while six
have some concerns. Independent-run
counts are reported in two studies.
Explicit code availability occurs
in one study and data availability
in two. One study was assessed
on an abstract-only basis.

These limitations do not diminish
the conceptual importance of
active robotic materials, but
they restrict conclusions about
general constitutive behavior
across independent platforms.

\paragraph*{What should be tested next?}

A decisive experiment should measure
mechanical response under several
independent perturbations, including
reciprocity tests that compare
loading and response at different
degrees of freedom.

Frequency-dependent measurements
can establish the regime in which
an effective elastic description
remains appropriate. Boundary
conditions also require explicit
characterization: localized modes
and apparent nonreciprocity
may depend on finite geometry.

The strongest comparisons would
use independently characterized
actuation and mechanical parameters
to predict deformation fields,
response spectra, and work
over cyclic loading.
Variation in system size and
network geometry would test
whether the inferred constitutive
description is general.

We conclude that robotic active
solids provide direct access
to mechanical predictions
of nonequilibrium theory,
but quantitative evidence
remains concentrated in
a small number of experiments.
Independent reproduction of
response coefficients and
controlled tests across
different mechanical structures
are immediate priorities.

\subsection{Other statistical-mechanical frameworks}
\label{sec:theory-statistical}

The theoretical diversity of robotic active matter
extends beyond alignment, persistent-particle,
hydrodynamic, and elastic descriptions.
Some experiments use statistical-mechanical
concepts to characterize fluctuations,
configuration probabilities, transport,
collective response, or nonequilibrium
state selection.

The extraction groups these approaches
under ``other statistical mechanics''.
This is a residual theory category,
not a single mathematical framework.
Its contents must therefore be interpreted
through the observables and mechanisms
reported by each study.

The category contains 25 studies:
six wheeled or legged robot systems,
five vibration-driven systems,
four feedback-controlled colloids,
three microscopic robotic systems,
three smarticles or granular systems,
three residual platforms,
and one modular system.

These systems involve informational,
mechanical, field-mediated, and
feedback-controlled interactions.
Their common classification does not
imply shared assumptions about
conservation laws, effective temperature,
detailed balance, or interaction symmetry.

\paragraph*{Configuration statistics and collective dynamics.}

Smarticle and granular-robot experiments
compare collective transport, configuration
probabilities, and fluctuation-related
quantities
\cite{Li2018supersmarticlealo,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Li2020programmingactiveg}.

Their internal actuation and mechanical
constraints jointly determine accessible
collective configurations. Statistical
descriptions can relate the observed
configuration distributions to the dynamics,
but a stationary distribution need not
have the interpretation of an equilibrium
Boltzmann distribution.

Active driving can maintain probability
currents even when the distribution appears
stationary. Configuration probabilities
alone may therefore be insufficient
to characterize the nonequilibrium process.

Feedback-controlled colloids provide
complementary tests involving interaction
potentials, rotational response, and
configuration statistics
\cite{Yan2014colloidalsuperstruc,Buerle2020formationofstable,Knippenberg2022roleofcohesionin,Lffler2021behaviordependentc}.

Their observables include pair potentials,
rotational order, susceptibility,
relaxation time, and flow statistics.
Programmable feedback permits comparisons
between different effective interactions,
although state-dependent control may
invalidate an equilibrium-potential
description.

\paragraph*{Other robotic realizations.}

Microscopic robotic systems compare
collective assembly, fluctuations,
and effective stochastic descriptions
\cite{Yang2022emergentmicroroboti,Aubret2018targetedassemblyan,Raghavendra2026programmablepersist}.

Vibration-driven experiments address
collective statistics, fluctuation
spectra, and configuration-dependent
dynamics
\cite{Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,Mayya2019nonuniformrobotde,Zhang2024proteinfoldinginsp,Li2021programmingactivec}.

Mechanical active systems are valuable
because their statistical behavior is
not necessarily specified by a programmed
interaction rule. Comparisons can test
whether stochastic models capture
collective measurements arising
from physical coupling.

Wheeled or legged systems investigate
programmed interactions and collective
order
\cite{Sankar2026trendformationwith,Turgut2008selforganizedflocka,Nishinari2006modellingofselfdr,Spears2004distributedphysics,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Rezeck2021flockingsegregative}.

Other specialized platforms address
chiral response, active ordering,
and nonequilibrium transport
\cite{Li2024memoryinducedspont,Bo2026threephasesofodd,Das2025roleofkinematicco,Kaeser2025individualandcolle}.

These studies illustrate the reach
of statistical-mechanical approaches
in robotic experiments, but their
different assumptions prevent us
from treating them as tests
of one common theory.

\paragraph*{Evidence and limitations.}

Sixteen of the 25 studies report
quantitative agreement and nine
qualitative agreement.

For D6, thirteen studies receive
low-risk ratings, eleven have
some concerns, and one is high risk.
This category therefore contains
a comparatively high proportion
of quantitative and lower-risk
theoretical comparisons.

Replication evidence is less complete:
seven studies receive low-risk
D1 ratings, seventeen have
some concerns, and one is high risk.
Independent-run counts are
recorded in eight studies.

Explicit code availability occurs
in four studies and data availability
in three. Two studies were assessed
from abstracts alone.

Statistical-mechanical methods can
describe several robotic collective
observables quantitatively. However,
the residual category does not
establish the general validity
of a single theoretical framework.

The strongest tests specify a
particular model, constrain its
parameters independently, and
evaluate predictions under
new experimental conditions.

For nonequilibrium systems,
dynamical observables are especially
important. Different processes can
produce similar stationary
configuration statistics while
maintaining different probability
currents and temporal correlations.

Future experiments should therefore
combine configuration measurements
with fluctuation, relaxation,
and response statistics.
Such comparisons can distinguish
a predictive statistical description
from a compact fit to observed data.

\subsection{Agent-based simulation as a comparator}
\label{sec:theory-agent-based}

Agent-based simulation is the most frequently
recorded theoretical comparator in the evidence base.
A simulation can represent the individual rules
of a robotic system, including sensing,
communication, actuation, and interactions.
It can also explore conditions that are
difficult to realize in hardware.

Yet, an agent-based simulation occupies a
different scientific position from a
coarse-grained physical theory.
It may reproduce the implemented controller
almost directly. Agreement with experiment
then demonstrates that the simulation
captures relevant aspects of the hardware,
but it does not necessarily identify
a universal collective mechanism.
The important question is what the model
predicts beyond the rules already implemented.

The extraction identifies 275 studies
using agent-based simulation.
Wheeled or legged robots contribute 127,
other platforms 52,
vibration-driven robots 32,
and robotic micro- and nano-agents 30.
Feedback-controlled colloids contribute ten,
modular robots nine,
robotic active solids eight,
and smarticles or granular robots six.
One study has no platform classification.

Thus, agent-based simulation spans nearly
the entire robotic taxonomy, although
the category is highly heterogeneous.

\paragraph*{What simulations can establish.}\hfill\break % layout only

Agent-based simulations can connect microscopic
implementation to collective observables.
A model incorporating measured interactions
can predict group-level behavior under
changes in agent number, noise, geometry,
or driving.

The robotic literature includes comparisons
in flocking, aggregation, transport,
and programmable collective organization
\cite{Turgut2008modelingphasetrans,Navarro2008anapproachtoflock,Garnier2005aggregationbehaviou,Guzzi2014bioinspiredobstacle,Cazenille2016automatedcalibratio,Vsrhelyi2018optimizedflockingo,Ferrante2012selforganizedflock,Mijalkov2016engineeringsensoria,Berlinger2021selforganizedevasi,Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,Zhou2024anticipationpromote,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc,Zhang2026emergentnonhermiti}.

Other comparisons investigate collective
motion, configuration, and interactions
under programmable control
\cite{Yamada2011circularmotiongene,Yang2023collectivechaseem,Tran2022frontierledswarmin,Giardina2022collectivephototact,Vega2023simulatelessexpec,Vega2023indirectswarmcontr,Vega2024agentbasedemulatio,Gao2023robustdistributedc,Lee2022distributedcohesive,Baumann2024hybridflockforma,Wu2024adversarialimitatio,Varadharajan2026programmableflocks,Pan2026exploringthephysic,Sankar2026trendformationwith,Verdoucq2025flockingphasetrans,Jond2026positionbasedflock}.

Agreement with hardware is nontrivial:
actuator limitations, sensing errors,
latency, and physical contacts may
be simplified or omitted in simulation.
Comparisons can identify which constraints
must be represented to reproduce
collective dynamics.

Nevertheless, reproducing the trajectory
of a programmed swarm is a weaker
test of active-matter theory than
predicting collective fluctuations
or transitions from independently
characterized rules.

\paragraph*{Simulations of physical interactions.}

For mechanically driven and microscopic
systems, agent-based simulation can
represent interactions not directly
programmed by a controller.

The included studies cover collision-mediated
dynamics, mechanical aggregation,
fluctuations, and collective transport
\cite{Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Welch2019activereorientation,Xi2024emergentintelligenc,GuzmnLastra2026emergenceofbeating,Edwards2026robusttopologically,Musacchio2026activeembracemente,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Casiulis2025ageometricconditio,Deblais2018boundariescontrolc,Boudet2022effectivetemperatur,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Musacchio2026fluidizationinduced,Zion2023morphologicalcomput,Wagner2026geometryinducedtra,Zion2021distributedonline}.

Microscopic robotic experiments provide
comparisons with models of field-driven
and interaction-mediated collective dynamics
\cite{Sun2021swarmingmicrodrople,Calabr2026interactiondrivena,Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic,Pauer2021programmabledesign,Chen2024synchronousandfull,Yigit2019programmablecollect,McGovern2024multifunctionalchir,Feng2023weakionexchangeba,Sun2024multiplemagnetoopt,Liu2022bionicmorphological,Wang2018collectivebehavior,Baker2019shapeprogrammed3d,Goyal2025externallycontrolle,Aubret2018targetedassemblyan,Yan2024programmingstructur,Yigit2020cohesiveselforgani,Hanson2025electrokineticpropu,Law2024navigationofmagnet,Ceron2026fluidictorqueenabl,Li2022vectorcontrolledwh}.

Here, the microscopic interaction model
may itself be a scientific hypothesis.
A simulation based on measured forces
or hydrodynamic coupling can therefore
test physical mechanisms. If its
parameters are adjusted separately
to reproduce every experimental
configuration, however, it remains
primarily descriptive.

A stronger comparison uses independently
measured parameters to predict collective
responses to changes in density,
confinement, or driving.

\paragraph*{Mechanical assemblies and active materials.}

Agent-based simulations also describe
systems with changing connectivity
or mechanically coupled components,
including modular robots, smarticles,
and active solids
\cite{Vardhan2022glidersinshapecha,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,Li2020programmingactiveg,Hu2026diatominspired1di,Rogowski2026coordinatedselfass,Kaeser2025individualandcolle,Bhattacharjee2026collectiveassembly,Li2019particleroboticsba,Baconnier2022tensioncontrolledc,Rezanejad2025programmableentangl,Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe,Coulais2025activemetamaterials,Chen2020selfsensingmetamat}.

Measured observables include displacement,
assembly geometry, mechanical response,
configuration statistics, and collective
deformation.

A discrete model may accurately reproduce
the response of a finite mechanical network
without validating a continuum constitutive
law. The latter requires additional
assumptions about scale separation
and effective material properties.
Agent-based simulation is therefore
a useful intermediate description,
not necessarily the final physical
explanation.

\paragraph*{Quantitative evidence and reproducibility.}

Among the 275 studies,
96 report quantitative agreement
and 171 qualitative agreement.
Seven are classified as having
no agreement comparison, and one
has a missing agreement value.

The D6 ratings are low risk
for 62 studies, some concerns
for 110, and high risk for 103.
Thus, fewer than one quarter
receive low-risk ratings for
quantitative theory comparison.

For replication, 78 studies
are rated low risk in D1,
190 have some concerns,
and seven are high risk.
Independent-run counts are
recorded for 107 studies.
Explicit code availability
occurs in 45, and data
availability in 37.
Eight studies were assessed
from abstracts alone.

Simulation code is particularly
important because model descriptions
may omit consequential details:
update order, numerical integration,
collision handling, and stochastic
sampling. Hardware-controller code
is equally relevant when differences
between the implemented rule and
the simulated rule affect collective
behavior.

Reproducible comparisons should
therefore document both the
computational model and the
experimental implementation.

\paragraph*{From simulation agreement to physical explanation.}

We distinguish three uses of
agent-based simulation.

A hardware-oriented simulation
reproduces control and actuation,
testing whether the implementation
behaves as expected.

A phenomenological simulation
reproduces selected collective
patterns but may not distinguish
between microscopic mechanisms.

A mechanistic simulation predicts
collective observables from
independently characterized
interactions. This provides
the strongest test of a
physical explanation.

The extracted comparison labels
do not systematically distinguish
these uses. Hence, the 96
quantitative agreements cannot
be interpreted as 96 independent
mechanistic validations.

An agent-based simulation may also
implement an active Brownian
particle model, a Vicsek-type
rule, or a discrete elastic network.
Because the framework field
is multi-label, the same
comparison can appear in
several theoretical categories.

Interpretation must therefore
rest on the mathematical model,
its independently constrained
parameters, and the observable
being tested.

We conclude that agent-based
simulation is indispensable
for connecting robotic
implementation to collective
dynamics. Its greatest
scientific value lies in
predictions that could fail
experimentally, rather than
merely reproducing the
behavior of a controller.

\subsection{What constitutes a decisive theoretical test?}
\label{sec:theory-decisive}

The comparison across theoretical frameworks
reveals a consistent limitation.
Robotic active matter is highly programmable,
but quantitative predictivity is less
widespread than implementation and
qualitative agreement.

Qualitative experiments remain valuable.
A new platform can establish that an
interaction or collective state is
physically realizable and reveal
unexpected behavior that motivates theory.
Testing an established model, however,
requires determining which observations
it explains, which parameters were
adjusted, and which predictions
were evaluated independently.

\paragraph*{Fitted parameters versus predicted observables.}

Suppose a theoretical model predicts
an observable $O$ as a function of
control parameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$
and model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$:
\begin{equation}
O_{\mathrm{th}}
=
F(\boldsymbol{\lambda},
  \boldsymbol{\theta}).
\label{eq:theory-prediction}
\end{equation}

A comparison becomes more informative
when $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is constrained
independently rather than adjusted
for every experimental condition.

Fitting a model to one measured curve
establishes descriptive adequacy,
not independent predictive validation.
Testing the same parameterization
under new conditions establishes
transferability. Stronger still
is determining microscopic parameters
independently and predicting the
collective response without fitting
to that response.

For Vicsek-type models, the implemented
alignment rule may be known while
collective fluctuations remain to
be predicted. For active Brownian
particles, isolated-agent motility
statistics can constrain parameters
before density-dependent behavior
is measured. Hydrodynamic theories
can use microscopic interactions
to constrain constitutive coefficients,
while active-elastic models can
predict deformation or wave propagation
under new loading conditions.

These comparisons turn programmability
into independent experimental constraint.

\paragraph*{Parameter sweeps and competing predictions.}

Several theories may reproduce the same
trajectory, density profile, or steady-state
configuration. A stronger experiment varies
an independently controlled parameter
and compares competing predictions.

Density variation can distinguish clustering
mechanisms; interaction range can test
alignment theories; persistence can
differentiate stochastic motility models;
and controlled loading can test
active-solid constitutive relations.

The most informative parameter changes
are those for which competing models
predict different outcomes.

Calibration is essential. Changing robot
speed may also alter collision rates,
motor noise, and turning dynamics.
Increasing a magnetic field may modify
both propulsion and interparticle forces.
Where parameters are physically coupled,
the model must account for that coupling;
otherwise, agreement may arise through
compensating errors.

\paragraph*{Several observables from one parameterization.}

Simultaneous agreement with different
collective observables provides another
demanding test.

A model may reproduce mean-squared
displacement but not orientation
correlations, or a steady-state
density profile but not relaxation
after a perturbation. A mechanical
model may predict deformation
without reproducing frequency response.

Such discrepancies identify where
approximations are adequate and
which mechanisms may be missing.
A well-characterized failure of a
prediction can be more informative
than agreement obtained through
extensive fitting.

Particularly useful comparisons
combine microscopic and collective
measurements: isolated-agent velocity
correlations with collective transport,
or pairwise interaction statistics
with clustering and alignment.
These directly connect proposed
mechanisms to emergent behavior.

\paragraph*{Experimental scale and finite-size effects.}

Many collective predictions become
sharply defined only at large system size.
Robotic experiments are finite and
sometimes contain few agents.
Small systems can measure interactions
precisely, but cannot establish
thermodynamic-limit behavior
without additional analysis.

Persistent polarization in a small
collective does not determine
fluctuation scaling. A confined
cluster does not necessarily
demonstrate phase separation.
A localized mode in a short
active network need not establish
a bulk constitutive property.

Where theories predict scaling,
experiments should vary system size
and characterize boundary conditions.
This directly concerns the review's
D3 finite-size and boundary
risk-of-bias domain.

A quantitatively accurate comparison
may still be valid only for one
geometry or system size. Its
reported conclusion should preserve
that domain of applicability.

\paragraph*{Independent replication and research groups.}

General physical claims become
stronger when predictions are
reproduced independently.

Independence may concern hardware,
research groups, analysis methods,
or the physical implementation
of interactions. Cross-platform
replication is especially valuable
when comparable dimensionless
parameters and observables
can be established.

A theory successfully tested in
mechanically driven particles,
programmable mobile robots,
and microscopic assemblies
may apply more broadly than
one demonstrated using
a single hardware design.
But the relevant physical
assumptions must genuinely
be shared.

The extracted dataset includes
publication metadata, platforms,
and theoretical comparisons,
but not a normalized mapping
of independent experimental groups.
Different publications therefore
cannot automatically be counted
as independent replications.

A future living-review update
could document experimental
lineage, hardware reuse,
and independent replication
to improve certainty assessments.

\paragraph*{Reporting requirements for reproducibility.}

Across theoretical frameworks,
independent-run counts, code,
and data availability are
often incompletely reported.
Finite-size and boundary
effects are also insufficiently
characterized in many studies.

These omissions limit independent
evaluation of measurement uncertainty,
order parameters, correlation functions,
and fitted scaling laws.
Unreported controller or simulation
details can change the effective
interaction and numerical comparison.

Requirements differ by platform.
Mechanical active solids need
appropriate response calibration;
feedback-controlled colloids need
documentation of measurement and
feedback processes.

The shared principle is that
the experimental conditions
necessary to reproduce a
theoretical prediction must
be documented.

\paragraph*{From agreement to explanatory power.}

Agent-based simulation is the
dominant comparator in the
extracted literature. Its breadth
reflects flexibility, but many
comparisons remain qualitative
or closely tied to implemented
robotic controllers.

Vicsek-type models offer directly
programmable alignment, yet relatively
few low-risk quantitative tests.
Active Brownian descriptions have
a stronger quantitative presence,
especially in mechanically driven
systems, and can be constrained
by single-agent motility.

Run-and-tumble models remain
represented by few comparisons
but offer opportunities to
discriminate stochastic
motility mechanisms.

Kinetic and hydrodynamic theories
connect interactions to collective
fields; their quantitative evidence
spans physically different platforms.
Active elasticity provides
specific mechanical predictions,
although independent quantitative
replication remains limited.
Other statistical-mechanical
frameworks have supported several
comparisons without constituting
one unified theoretical model.

Across these frameworks, the
central opportunity is the same:
use programmability to distinguish
physical explanations rather
than merely reproduce patterns.

A decisive robotic active-matter
experiment characterizes its
microscopic implementation,
measures collective observables
quantitatively, and evaluates
theoretical predictions under
conditions not used to determine
their parameters. Independent
reproduction strengthens the
claim of a general mechanism.

Robots are therefore more than
visible realizations of theoretical
particles. They allow experimentalists
to modify model assumptions
individually and test their
collective consequences.

Used this way, robotic active
matter can move from demonstrating
nonequilibrium phenomena toward
discriminating between the
theories that explain them.
