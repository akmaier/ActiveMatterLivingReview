\section{Overview of the evidence base}\label{sec:results_overview}

\subsection{Study selection}

Figure~\ref{fig:prisma} summarizes identification, deduplication, screening, retrieval, and inclusion. The matched PRISMA count file was absent from the supplied snapshot. Hence, the flow boxes remain [records identified], [duplicates removed], [records screened], [records excluded], [reports sought], [reports not retrieved], [reports assessed], and [reports excluded by reason]. These values must be regenerated from the screening audit trail before submission.

The final evidence table contains 528 included studies in the snapshot dated 2026-10-14. Full reports were available for 495 studies, i.e. 93.8\% of the evidence base. The remaining 33 studies, or 6.3\%, were assessed from their abstracts. They include conference abstracts with reported results and reports whose full text could not be retrieved. We retain them in the descriptive overview but exclude them in the planned sensitivity analysis. Consequently, a phenomenon supported only by these records will be identified as abstract-only evidence.

Extraction is not yet fully resolved for every record. At least one field awaits author checking in 135 studies, corresponding to 25.6\% of the included set. This does not mean that all information in those rows is uncertain. It means that at least one single-valued field produced three different extractor values or otherwise required adjudication. We therefore give the denominator for fields with missing values and avoid interpreting missing entries as negative findings.

The selection total is broad by design. Eligibility required hardware, interaction, and a quantitative collective or interaction observable. Yet, it did not require a particular platform, length scale, control method, or theoretical model. The resulting evidence base spans early multi-robot coordination, programmable particle systems, vibration-driven collectives, microrobotic swarms, and active mechanical solids. This breadth is useful only when the differences remain visible. We therefore treat the 528 studies as a common evidence base, not as one homogeneous experimental population.

\subsection{Growth and diversification of the field}

The earliest included study appeared in 1995. Only 46 studies were published from 1995 through 2009. The following decade contributed 126 studies. Growth accelerated after 2020. We identified 127 studies from 2020 through 2022 and 229 from 2023 through 2026. Thus, 356 of 528 studies, or 67.4\%, appeared from 2020 onward. The count for 2026 is necessarily incomplete because the snapshot precedes the end of the year.

Annual output remained modest until the late 2010s. The table contains 26 studies from 2018 and 20 from 2019. It then contains 30 from 2020, 43 from 2021, and 54 from 2022. The later counts are 41 in 2023, 61 in 2024, 57 in 2025, and 70 in the partial 2026 record. Hence, the evidence base has expanded faster than a conventional static review cycle can follow. This is the main reason for the living-review design.

Growth also changed the composition of the literature. Before 2020, wheeled or legged swarm robots accounted for 111 of 172 studies, or 64.5\%. From 2020 onward, they accounted for 104 of 356, or 29.2\%. Their absolute contribution remained large, but the field diversified around them. Robotic micro- and nano-agents increased from 15 studies before 2020 to 68 afterward. Vibration-driven robots increased from 9 to 49, modular or self-reconfigurable robots from 5 to 18, and feedback-controlled active colloids from 1 to 13. Robotic metamaterials or active solids increased from 1 to 9.

Representative studies illustrate this change in scope. Vibration-driven robots have been used to quantify collective rotation and clustering \cite{Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove}. Smarticle ensembles connect internal shape change to emergent transport \cite{Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo}. Robotic chains and lattices make nonreciprocal response and active-solid behaviour directly measurable \cite{Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec}. Feedback-controlled colloids permit interaction rules to be changed during an experiment \cite{Buerle2020formationofstable}. Microrobotic systems extend the same questions to fluid-mediated transport and assembly \cite{Ahmed2021bioinspiredacousto}. These examples do not define separate fields. They show that common active-matter questions are now accessible through physically different realizations.

\subsection{Platforms and propulsion}

Wheeled or legged swarm robots remain the largest platform class with 215 studies, corresponding to 40.7\% of the evidence base. The next largest coded class is robotic micro- and nano-agents with 83 studies, or 15.7\%. Vibration-driven robots contribute 58 studies, or 11.0\%. Modular or self-reconfigurable robots contribute 23, feedback-controlled active colloids 14, smarticles or granular robots 11, and robotic metamaterials or active solids 10.

A further 95 studies were coded as ``other.'' This is the second-largest label, but it is not a coherent platform. It includes systems that did not fit the prespecified classes. Treating this residual group as one physical category would therefore conceal rather than simplify the evidence. Platform class remains missing in 19 records. These records include unresolved and abstract-only assessments and are not redistributed among the other classes.

Propulsion reflects the dominance of macroscopic mobile robots. Wheels appear in 196 studies. Another 127 use propulsion coded as ``other,'' while 72 use vibration and 66 magnetic fields. Light drives 20 systems, chemical mechanisms 13, legs 3, and acoustic actuation 1. Propulsion is unresolved or unreported in 30 studies.

Note that platform and propulsion answer different questions. A magnetic field may actuate a microrobot, a colloid, or a modular macroscopic system. Likewise, vibration can drive an isolated mobile unit or an element constrained in an active lattice. We therefore use platform to describe embodiment and propulsion to describe energy injection. The later synthesis keeps both variables because similar collective patterns can arise from different combinations.

\subsection{Interaction and control}

Steric contact is the most frequently coded interaction modality, appearing in 304 studies. Communication appears in 169, programmed rules in 166, and optical or other sensing in 160. Shared-environment interactions occur in 143 studies. Magnetic coupling appears in 91 and hydrodynamic coupling in 74. These categories are multi-label. A study may combine contact, sensing, communication, and environmental mediation, so their counts do not sum to 528.

The distribution shows that embodiment cannot be separated from information flow. Contact-dominated systems can generate collective states without explicit messages. At the other extreme, communication and sensing create interaction graphs that need not follow physical distance. Environment-mediated systems lie between these cases because agents alter a field, object, boundary, or substrate that then affects other agents.

On-board rules are the most common control architecture, with 293 studies. External feedback appears in 86, while 65 studies use passive or uncontrolled rules. Central controllers appear in 17 and learned policies in 13. Control architecture is missing in 54 records.

The small learned-policy count is important. Learning attracts considerable attention, but it is not yet the dominant experimental route in this evidence base. In contrast, simple on-board rules and direct feedback support most hardware studies. Even recent learning experiments often depend on morphology, collisions, or a controlled environment. For example, decentralized learning has been combined with sterically interacting vibration-driven robots \cite{Zion2023morphologicalcomput}, while graph-based policies have been deployed on physical wheeled robots \cite{Blumenkamp2022aframeworkforreal}. The synthesis must therefore distinguish learning as an interaction rule from learning as the cause of collective behaviour.

\subsection{Collective phenomena}

Self-assembly or shape formation is the most frequent phenomenon, coded in 256 studies. Transport or locomotion follows with 215. Flocking or polar order appears in 153 studies, clustering or motility-induced phase separation in 109, and chirality or rotation in 93. Swarming or vortex states occur in 81 studies, synchronization in 66, and boundary accumulation in 65. Jamming or crystallization appears in 30, while active-solid or odd-elastic behaviour appears in 15. Another 53 studies contain phenomena coded as ``other.''

Again, these counts are not mutually exclusive. A system may assemble, rotate, and transport an object in the same experiment. As such, frequency indicates research attention rather than independent confirmation. The later phenomenon synthesis asks a stricter question. We examine whether the same observable and mechanism recur across independent platforms and groups.

The distribution also reveals a difference between mature and emerging themes. Flocking, self-assembly, and transport have large and diverse evidence bases. Active-solid and odd-elastic phenomena have fewer studies and are concentrated in specialized platforms. Still, smaller counts do not imply lower physical importance. They imply that conclusions about generality require greater care.

Some labels also combine mechanisms that must be separated in the detailed synthesis. Clustering caused by steric crowding is not automatically motility-induced phase separation. A rotating aggregate produced by magnetic forcing is not equivalent to a spontaneously selected chiral state. Likewise, shape formation under a central controller is different from self-assembly through local interactions. We retain the broad categories for mapping, then return to observables, interventions, and theory in the corresponding sections.

\subsection{Theory comparison}

Agent-based simulation is the most common theoretical comparator and appears in 284 studies. No theory is recorded for 135 studies. Kinetic or hydrodynamic theory appears in 67, while 43 studies use another theoretical framework. Statistical-mechanical models other than the named classes occur in 28 studies, as do active-Brownian-particle models. Vicsek-type models appear in 20, active elasticity in 7, and run-and-tumble theory in 2. Theory labels are multi-valued because one experiment may be compared with several models.

The agreement field gives a more restrictive view. Quantitative theory--experiment comparison is coded in 133 studies. Qualitative agreement is coded in 238, and 123 report no comparison. The field is unresolved or missing in 34 records. Thus, a theoretical label does not by itself establish a quantitative test. A simulation can be used to illustrate a mechanism without testing a numerical prediction.

The temporal trend is encouraging but incomplete. Quantitative agreement rose from 32 of 172 studies before 2020, or 18.6\%, to 101 of 356 studies from 2020 onward, or 28.4\%. Yet, qualitative comparison remains the largest category in both periods. The evidence base is therefore moving toward quantitative confrontation, but visual or descriptive agreement still dominates.

This distinction determines the later theory synthesis. We will not infer support from the presence of a model name. Instead, we ask which observables were compared, whether parameters were measured or fitted, and whether agreement persisted across regimes. That step turns a bibliometric map into a test of physical explanation.

\subsection{Venues and disciplinary structure}

The studies are distributed across physics, robotics, materials science, and engineering venues. The largest single venue label is arXiv with 37 records. The IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation, \textit{Nature Communications}, and \textit{IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters} each contribute 20. The IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems contributes 16, while APS March Meeting abstracts contribute 13. Fourteen records have no resolved venue.

No venue dominates the evidence base. This dispersion explains why the field can appear fragmented even when different communities study related phenomena. Robotics venues often organize work around platforms and control. Physics venues organize it around phases, interactions, and models. Materials venues emphasize function and fabrication. The review connects these literatures through shared observables rather than through publication venue.

Preprint records also form a visible part of the evidence base. However, the venue field alone does not establish that every arXiv record remained unpublished. We therefore reserve the planned preprint-versus-peer-reviewed comparison for records whose publication status can be resolved. Counting venue strings as publication status would overstate reporting-bias evidence.

\subsection{Completeness and reproducibility of reporting}

Independent experimental runs are explicitly stated in 186 studies, or 35.2\% of the evidence base. The remaining records either omit the number or do not permit it to be extracted. Named code repositories occur in 78 studies, corresponding to 14.8\%. Named data repositories occur in 56, or 10.6\%. Availability is unresolved in 13 records for each item.

There has been improvement. Before 2020, code was available in 13 of 172 studies and data in 4. From 2020 onward, these counts rose to 65 of 356 and 52 of 356. In proportional terms, code availability increased from 7.6\% to 18.3\%, while data availability increased from 2.3\% to 14.6\%. These gains are substantial. Still, most experiments cannot be reproduced from an archived trajectory set and documented control implementation.

The domain-level quality ratings show where interpretation is most vulnerable. Replication was rated as some concerns in 363 studies and high risk in 21. Finite-size and boundary treatment raised some concerns in 468 studies and high risk in 7. In contrast, measurement validity was rated low risk in 402 studies. Agent heterogeneity was also usually characterized adequately, with 409 low-risk ratings. Data and code availability received a high-risk rating in 413 studies. Quantitative theory comparison received a high-risk rating in 268.

Hence, the typical weakness is not that experiments lack measurable behaviour. It is that the conditions needed to generalize the measurement are incompletely reported. Boundaries, population size, repetition, and theory comparison determine whether an observed state is a robust collective phenomenon or a property of one apparatus.

This overview provides the map for the following sections. The platform section examines embodiment. The phenomenon section asks what recurs across embodiments. The theory section tests explanations rather than labels. Finally, the control section asks when programmability becomes a causal experimental tool. Together, these steps move from the size of the literature to the strength of its physical claims.
