\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:outlook}

Robotic active matter has reached a point at which the central
challenge is no longer simply to produce collective behavior.
Experiments have demonstrated flocking, clustering, transport,
synchronization, collective assembly, and mechanically
nonreciprocal response across a wide range of artificial
systems. The next challenge is to establish which physical
principles these observations test.

The distinctive advantage of robotics is experimental
intervention. Propulsion, perception, communication,
interaction rules, and mechanical coupling can often
be modified without replacing the entire apparatus.
This flexibility offers a way to distinguish competing
theoretical explanations rather than merely reproduce
their predicted patterns.

Yet, an adjustable controller does not guarantee an
independently adjustable physical parameter. Changing
a sensing rule may also alter response delay.
Increasing activity may change collision frequency
and effective noise. Applying a stronger magnetic
field may simultaneously modify propulsion and
interparticle coupling. Future experiments must
characterize these dependencies explicitly.

We identify three connected priorities. First, robotic
experiments should be designed around predictions
that can fail. Second, under-tested collective
phenomena and platform combinations should receive
more systematic attention. Third, the field needs
reporting practices that permit quantitative results
to be reproduced and compared.

The living review provides an infrastructure for
tracking progress on these priorities. It cannot
replace the experiments themselves, but it can
make changes in the evidence base transparent
and help direct attention toward unresolved
physical questions.

\subsection{Experiments designed to distinguish mechanisms}
\label{sec:outlook-decisive}

A decisive active-matter experiment should compare
at least two physically distinguishable explanations
of a collective observable. The experimental
intervention must change a relevant control
parameter, while the theoretical prediction
must concern an outcome not directly imposed
by the controller.

This standard can be implemented differently
across robotic platforms.

\paragraph*{An alignment experiment with independent tests.}

Programmable wheeled or legged robots provide
direct control over local alignment rules.
A useful experiment would implement a
specified alignment model and independently
measure the realized relationship between
neighbor orientation and turning response.

The experiment would then vary noise,
interaction range, and population size
while measuring polarization,
velocity correlations, density
fluctuations, and relaxation
after perturbations.

The critical point is that the collective
observables should be predicted using
the characterized microscopic rule.
They should not each receive a
separately fitted parameterization.

Existing robotic flocking experiments
establish the feasibility of distributed
alignment and controlled collective
motion
\cite{Turgut2008selforganizedflocka,
Tarcai2011patternstransition,
Vsrhelyi2018optimizedflockingo,
Zhou2024anticipationpromote,
Verdoucq2025flockingphasetrans}.
The proposed experiment would extend
this capability toward a more demanding
test of ordering theory.

A particularly useful comparison would
preserve the effective alignment law
while changing how it is implemented:
for example, local visual perception
versus explicit communication.
If the measured collective statistics
remain unchanged, that would support
the relevance of the effective rule
over implementation details.

If they change, the discrepancy could
identify physical variables omitted
from the simplified theory, including
latency, perception anisotropy,
or actuator constraints.

\paragraph*{A clustering experiment that separates attraction from persistence.}

The combined clustering/MIPS literature
contains multiple physical mechanisms.
A decisive experiment should distinguish
aggregation caused by explicit attraction
from density-dependent slowing of
persistent active particles.

Vibration-driven robots are promising
because steric interactions can produce
collective organization without direct
neighbor-to-neighbor communication
\cite{Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,
Welch2019activereorientation,
Deblais2019howtoforceanarmy,
Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,
Luo2025flockingphasesepar}.

The experimental program would begin
with isolated-particle trajectories
and controlled pair collisions.
It would measure propulsion speed,
orientation persistence, and
contact-dependent motion.

Subsequently, density and persistence
would be varied independently where
the hardware permits. The resulting
cluster-size distributions,
coexisting densities, and
spatial correlations could be
compared with predictions derived
from the measured microscopic
dynamics.

Programmable robots could provide
a complementary control experiment.
One could explicitly introduce
attraction, remove it, and test
whether clustering persists
through activity and crowding
alone.

Such comparisons would help distinguish
motility-induced phase separation
from other routes to aggregation.
They would also expose conditions
under which a simplified active
Brownian particle description fails.

\paragraph*{A nonreciprocity experiment with controlled symmetry.}

Nonreciprocal interactions are another
especially promising target.
Programmable robots and feedback-controlled
colloids can generate asymmetric
responses that are difficult to
realize through conventional
passive interactions
\cite{Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,
Buerle2020formationofstable,
Chen2022collectiveresponse,
Chen2024persistentandrespo}.

A decisive experiment would vary
interaction reciprocity while keeping
other relevant properties approximately
constant. It would then measure
collective order, circulation,
relaxation, and response to
localized perturbations.

The most useful predictions would
concern features not encoded directly
in the interaction rule, such as
the onset of oscillations,
collective-state selection,
or changes in fluctuation spectra.

The same effective model could be
tested on mechanically different
platforms. Agreement across
implementations would provide
stronger evidence than repeated
realizations using the same
feedback apparatus.

\paragraph*{A mechanical-response experiment for active solids.}

Robotic metamaterials provide direct
access to response functions.
They offer a route toward
quantitative tests of
active elasticity and
nonreciprocal mechanics
\cite{Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,
Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,
Binysh2026moreislessinunpe,
Coulais2025activemetaterials}.

The proposed experiment would measure
a mechanical response matrix under
independently imposed perturbations.
It would characterize the relation
between actuation, displacement,
force, and frequency-dependent
response.

The resulting coefficients could
then be used to predict wave
propagation or deformation
under new loading conditions.

Repeating the comparison across
different network sizes,
geometries, and boundaries
would test whether the measured
response is a property of
the effective active material
rather than one particular
mechanical assembly.

A particularly informative
observable would be the work
associated with a controlled
cycle of deformation.
This could help distinguish
active nonconservative response
from passive dissipation
and controller-specific
motion.

\subsection{Under-tested platforms and phenomena}
\label{sec:outlook-gaps}

The corrected October 17, 2026 evidence base
contains 491 included studies, but the
distribution of experimental approaches
is uneven. The largest platform class
is wheeled or legged swarm robots.
By contrast, feedback-controlled
active colloids, smarticles,
and active mechanical solids
have substantially smaller
experimental literatures.

Study frequency should not be mistaken
for scientific priority. A small
literature may contain particularly
informative quantitative experiments.
Nevertheless, uneven coverage reveals
where independent reproduction and
cross-platform comparison remain limited.

\paragraph*{From large swarms to large-system physics.}

Robotic swarms can be scaled to
substantial agent numbers, but many
included experiments use comparatively
small populations. Future studies
should distinguish the engineering
achievement of controlling more agents
from the physics of collective
behavior at increasing system size.

The relevant question is whether
order parameters, correlations,
transport coefficients, or
collective response obey
size-dependent predictions.

An experiment that repeats the same
measurement across several population
sizes, while accounting for changes
in density and boundary conditions,
would contribute more directly to
finite-size theory than a
large demonstration reporting
only its maximum agent count.

This priority follows from the
weaknesses identified in the
D3 finite-size and boundary
risk-of-bias domain.

\paragraph*{Mechanically interacting collectives.}

Vibration-driven robots and smarticles
offer opportunities to study physical
contact, friction, mechanical
constraints, and geometry-dependent
activity without prescribing every
collective response through software.

Relevant experiments include
collective transport, mechanically
mediated ordering, and
configuration-dependent locomotion
\cite{Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,
Li2020programmingactiveg,
Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,
Bhamla2021collectivetransport,
Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork}.

Future work should report
single-agent dynamics,
pair-collision responses,
contact statistics,
and collective behavior
under the same driving conditions.

Such measurements would permit
more stringent tests of
stochastic-particle and
mechanical-interaction models.

\paragraph*{Microscopic and feedback-mediated systems.}

Robotic micro- and nano-agents can
access hydrodynamic, magnetic,
and field-mediated interactions.
Feedback-controlled colloids
can additionally modify
state-dependent effective
coupling
\cite{Sun2021swarmingmicrodrople,
Pauer2021programmabledesign,
Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,
Guo2026afeedbackcontrolle}.

The major opportunity is to
separate common external forcing
from genuinely interaction-mediated
collective behavior.

Experiments should measure the
isolated-particle response,
pair interactions, and
collective observables
under matched conditions.

This would help determine when
a global driving field merely
coordinates independent particles
and when the observed state
depends on interparticle coupling.

\paragraph*{Temporal and mechanical observables.}

Synchronization, jamming,
collective vortices,
and active elasticity
have smaller or more
specialized evidence bases
than broad categories such as
transport and self-assembly.

These phenomena would benefit
from standardized observables.
Synchronization experiments
should report phase differences,
frequency distributions,
and relaxation after perturbation.

Jamming studies should distinguish
dynamical arrest,
structural order,
and congestion through
measurements of mobility,
packing, and correlations.

Vortex experiments should separate
intrinsic particle rotation
from collective angular motion.
Active-solid experiments should
report mechanical response
rather than only the motion
of an actuated structure.

Such distinctions would improve
comparability without erasing
the diversity of the platforms.

\subsection{Reporting standards for robotic active matter}
\label{sec:outlook-reporting}

The synthesis identifies recurring limitations in
replication reporting, finite-size characterization,
data and code availability, and quantitative
theory comparison. These limitations suggest
a practical reporting standard for the field.

A useful minimum is a complete description
of the physical implementation,
the independent experimental realizations,
the measured collective observables,
and the relationship between theory
parameters and experimental data.

\paragraph*{Hardware and interaction characterization.}

Experimental reports should identify the
number of physical agents,
their relevant dimensions and
propulsion properties,
and the distribution of
important agent-to-agent
variations.

They should describe the interaction
mechanism rather than relying
only on a collective-behavior label.

For programmed robots, this means
documenting the local update rule,
sensing range, communication topology,
control frequency, and relevant delay.

For field-driven microscopic agents,
it means characterizing the external
field and, where possible,
the isolated-particle and
pairwise response.

For mechanically driven robots,
it means reporting actuation,
contact geometry, frictional
conditions, and relevant
single-agent fluctuations.

For active solids, it means
describing connectivity,
actuator response,
loading conditions,
and the measured
mechanical degrees of freedom.

These details are necessary
to determine what physical
model the apparatus
actually realizes.

\paragraph*{Replications and uncertainty.}

Reports should state the number
of independent experimental runs
for every principal collective
measurement.

They should distinguish independent
realizations from repeated samples
obtained within one run.
Statistical summaries should reflect
the corresponding dependence
structure.

Where practicable, authors should
report uncertainty intervals,
run-to-run variability,
and sensitivity to
initial conditions.

A striking trajectory
can establish the existence
of an experimental event.
It cannot by itself determine
the reproducibility of a
collective state.

\paragraph*{Geometry, boundaries, and system size.}

The physical arena or domain
should be documented alongside
its boundary conditions.

When a study makes claims
about collective phases,
continuum behavior,
or long-range order,
experiments at multiple
system sizes should be
considered.

If such tests are not feasible,
the limitation should be
stated explicitly.
A finite-system observation
remains scientifically useful
when its domain of
applicability is clear.

\paragraph*{Theory comparison and parameter provenance.}

A quantitative comparison should
identify the observables compared,
the uncertainty of those
observables, and the parameters
used by the model.

Most importantly, it should
distinguish parameters obtained
from independent measurements,
parameters fitted to the
reported experiment,
and parameters selected
through prior optimization.

Predictions evaluated outside
the fitting conditions
should be identified separately.

This distinction would allow
future systematic reviews
to assess predictive validity
more directly than the
present qualitative versus
quantitative agreement labels.

\paragraph*{Code, data, and reproducible analysis.}

Where possible, studies should
provide machine-readable
trajectory or response data,
the analysis code used to
calculate collective observables,
and the controller or simulation
implementation.

Relevant calibration procedures
and software versions should
also be recorded.

These materials need not
include proprietary hardware
designs when sharing is
not possible, but sufficient
methodological information
should remain available
to evaluate the physical claim.

The appropriate general
principle is that a reader
should be able to distinguish
experimental observations
from processing choices,
model assumptions,
and fitted parameters.

\subsection{The living review as a community resource}
\label{sec:outlook-living}

A static review becomes incomplete as new
experiments appear. This is particularly
important for robotic active matter,
where platform designs, interaction
mechanisms, and theoretical comparisons
continue to change.

The living-review approach addresses
this problem through scheduled evidence
updates and transparent revision
of the synthesis
\cite{Elliott2014living,Elliott2017living}.

Our infrastructure combines monthly
literature searches, a searchable
public evidence website,
and community-submitted candidate
studies. The goal is not merely
to append new publications.
It is to maintain a traceable
connection between the studies,
their extracted characteristics,
and the scientific conclusions.

\paragraph*{Monthly searches and versioned snapshots.}

Database searches will be rerun monthly
using the documented search strategy.
Newly identified records will undergo
the same eligibility assessment
specified by the review protocol.

The process will preserve
deduplication decisions,
screening outcomes,
extraction records,
and adjudication history.

Each released snapshot will
carry a version and date.
The manuscript and figures
will identify the snapshot
on which their numerical
claims depend.

The correction from 492 to
491 included studies
illustrates why this
versioning is essential.
A merged duplicate changes
descriptive counts, even when
the underlying scientific
literature has not changed.

A living synthesis must
therefore distinguish
changes in evidence
from corrections to
the evidence-processing
workflow.

\paragraph*{A reconfigurable public taxonomy.}

The public website provides a searchable
interface to the included studies.
Its taxonomy is not restricted
to one fixed hierarchy.

Readers can choose which extracted
attributes form the levels of
the taxonomy tree and reorder
those levels to explore
different relationships.

For example, one reader may
begin with platform class,
then subdivide studies
by interaction modality
and collective phenomenon.

Another may begin with
theoretical framework,
then organize the evidence
by agreement type,
control architecture,
or platform.

This follows the general
living-review concept of
an explorable evidence
resource, rather than
a static catalogue
\cite{Matl2017vascular}.

The reconfigurable tree
is particularly useful
because no single hierarchy
captures the scientific
structure of robotic
active matter.

A classification by
hardware emphasizes
experimental implementation.
A classification by
interaction mechanism
emphasizes physical coupling.
A classification by
phenomenon reveals
cross-platform similarities.
A classification by
theoretical framework
exposes where quantitative
tests remain sparse.

Readers can search the
studies and follow
different routes
through the same
underlying evidence
without changing
their inclusion status
or extracted data.

This flexibility
should not be confused
with unrestricted
reinterpretation.
The website must
distinguish source-derived
fields, missing values,
and editorial
certainty judgments.

A change in the order
of taxonomy attributes
is a change in
visual organization,
not a change
in the evidence.

\paragraph*{Community contributions through GitHub.}

Database coverage can be supplemented
by contributions from researchers
and readers.

Anyone may propose a missing paper
through a GitHub pull request
containing one small YAML file
with the candidate study's
bibliographic information.

An automated check will confirm
the DOI against Crossref
and flag candidate papers
that already appear
in the review.

This check is intended
to improve identifier
validity and identify
possible duplicates.
It is not an
eligibility decision.
A valid DOI does
not establish that
a paper contains
qualifying robotic
active-matter evidence.

After automated checking,
maintainers review
the proposed entry
and merge eligible
submissions into
the candidate-record
queue.

The next scheduled
update screens
the candidate using
the same panel,
eligibility criteria,
and adjudication
procedure applied
to database records.

This preserves
consistency between
community contributions
and records found
through monthly searches.

Under PRISMA 2020,
these contributions
are accounted for
as records identified
via other methods,
rather than silently
added to the
database-search totals
\cite{Page2021prisma}.

The distinction is
important for
reproducibility.
Readers should
be able to determine
how a study entered
the screening process
and whether it was
assessed under
the same criteria
as other records.

Community submission
does not guarantee
inclusion. Nor should
maintainer approval
of a bibliographic
entry replace
independent
eligibility assessment.

The pull-request
history provides
a public record
of proposed additions,
automated checks,
and maintainer
decisions. It can
also expose possible
duplicates and
correct bibliographic
metadata before
screening.

In this way,
the contribution
mechanism broadens
discovery while
retaining a
structured and
auditable review
process.

\subsection{What future updates will evaluate}
\label{sec:outlook-tracking}

The living design will track not only
the number of included studies,
but also changes in their scientific
content and methodological quality.

At the platform level,
updates will monitor the appearance
of new physical implementations
and changes in the residual
``other'' category.

A growing residual class
would indicate that
the taxonomy requires
documented refinement.

At the phenomenon level,
updates will track
new evidence for
collective ordering,
clustering, synchronization,
active mechanical response,
and other extracted outcomes.

However, newly reported
phenomena will not
automatically be treated
as independently validated
physical states.

At the theory level,
the most important
changes will concern
quantitative comparisons,
independently determined
parameters, and tests
outside fitting conditions.

A new demonstration
of programmed flocking
may expand platform
coverage without
substantially changing
certainty about
ordering theory.
An independently
replicated scaling
measurement could
have a much
larger effect
on that conclusion.

At the methodological
level, updates will
monitor independent-run
reporting, experimental
size and boundaries,
measurement validity,
and data and
code availability.

We will also track
whether newly accessible
full texts resolve
abstract-only
assessment limitations,
and whether preprints
can be reconciled
with published versions.

Changes to automated
screening or extraction
procedures will require
documentation and,
when material,
additional validation.
The living process
cannot assume that
a future model
has the same
performance as
the workflow
evaluated in
the present snapshot.

Finally, each update
will reconsider
the certainty
of the principal
conclusions.
Certainty may
increase when
new studies
provide independent,
low-risk
quantitative tests.
It may decrease
when contradictory
evidence or
previously unrecognized
limitations emerge.

\subsection{Toward predictive robotic active matter}
\label{sec:outlook-conclusion}

The future of robotic active matter
depends on bringing two experimental
traditions into closer contact.

Robotics contributes programmable
agents, controlled sensing,
feedback, and the ability
to alter interaction rules.
Active-matter physics contributes
theoretical descriptions of
collective organization,
fluctuations, transport,
and nonequilibrium response.

The strongest experiments
will use both.
They will not merely
implement a model
or reproduce
a recognizable
collective pattern.

Instead, they will
characterize microscopic
dynamics, derive or
specify predictions,
and test those
predictions through
controlled interventions.

They will also
seek circumstances
in which a model
fails, because
such failures
identify missing
physical mechanisms.

The living review
supports this
transition by
making evidence
discoverable,
classifications
reconfigurable,
and changes
auditable.

Monthly searches
will maintain
coverage.
The public
taxonomy will
permit alternative
views of the
same evidence.
Community contributions
will help identify
missing studies
without bypassing
the established
screening process.

Yet, no review
infrastructure
can substitute
for decisive
experiments.
The essential
advance must
occur in the
laboratory:
independent
parameter measurements,
replicated collective
observables,
systematic variation,
and direct tests
of competing
physical theories.

Robotic active
matter will
make its
most distinctive
contribution
when programmability
is used not
to prescribe
collective behavior,
but to determine
why that behavior
emerges and
under which
conditions it
ceases to exist.
