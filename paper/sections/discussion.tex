\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Robotic active matter connects programmable interactions,
collective motion, and nonequilibrium statistical physics.
Its distinctive achievement is the ability to modify
microscopic interactions and measure their consequences
in physical hardware.

The evidence nevertheless reveals an asymmetry:
demonstrations of collective behavior are widespread,
while independently replicated quantitative tests of
general physical theories remain less common.

For objective O1, robotic active matter encompasses
programmed mobile swarms, mechanically driven particles,
microscopic agents, feedback-controlled colloids,
modular assemblies, and active mechanical structures.
These platforms offer complementary experimental
capabilities without necessarily sharing one
theoretical description.

For O2, the evidence establishes collective motion,
clustering, transport, assembly, rotation, and
synchronization across several platforms.
More specialized phenomena include active elasticity
and odd mechanical response. Similar patterns,
however, can arise from different mechanisms.

For O3, quantitative comparisons span several
theoretical frameworks, although agent-based
simulation remains the dominant comparator.
Reproducing an implemented controller must
be distinguished from independently predicting
collective observables.

For O4, sensing, communication, and feedback
make interactions adjustable. The controller,
however, is part of the physical dynamics
and may impose the behavior to be explained.

Finally, O5 identifies weaknesses in replication
documentation, finite-size characterization,
and quantitative theory validation. These
limitations do not invalidate observations,
but restrict their generality.

We assess these limitations and distinguish
well-supported conclusions from those
that remain conditional.

\subsection{Risk of bias across the evidence base}
\label{sec:discussion-risk}

Robotic active matter lacks a validated quality
instrument spanning its heterogeneous experimental
designs. Microscopic colloids, aerial swarms,
and active metamaterials require different
measurements, yet share conditions necessary
for reliable physical inference.

We therefore assessed six prespecified domains:
replication (D1), agent heterogeneity (D2),
finite-size and boundary effects (D3),
measurement validity (D4), data and code
availability (D5), and quantitative theory
comparison (D6).

These ratings describe limitations of reported
evidence, not experimental correctness.
Missing documentation does not establish
that the corresponding practice was absent.

The October 17, 2026 evidence base contains
489 studies. The domain counts below retain
the extracted ratings pending reconciliation
of the final record-level quality table.
One record in that extraction lacks all
six domain ratings.

\paragraph*{Replication and independent runs.}

For D1, 125 studies are rated low risk,
346 have some concerns, and 17 are high risk.
Thus, among the 488 extracted records
with a D1 rating, 25.6\% received
a low-risk assessment.

A numerical independent-run count is recorded
for 194 studies, representing 39.7\% of
the 489-study evidence base.

Stochastic trajectories and fluctuating collective
states require repeated realizations to establish
measurement uncertainty. Experiments on aggregation,
colloidal organization, and mechanical response
illustrate the relevance of reproducibility
\cite{Garnier2005aggregationbehaviou,Buerle2020formationofstable,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork}.

Run counts alone do not establish statistical
independence: trajectory samples may be correlated,
and repeated experiments may share calibration
errors. The extraction does not standardize
between-run variation or measurement uncertainty.

D1 therefore supports moderate confidence
in experimental reproducibility, with
considerable uncertainty where independent
repetitions are incompletely reported.

\paragraph*{Agent heterogeneity.}

For D2, 389 studies are rated low risk,
98 have some concerns, and four are high risk.
This is the most favorable domain.

Relevant heterogeneity depends on platform:
motors and sensors in mobile robots;
propulsion, geometry, and friction in
vibration-driven systems; and size or
field response in microscopic agents.
Such variation can affect collective
states despite identical nominal rules
\cite{Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Deblais2019howtoforceanarmy,Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic,Pauer2021programmabledesign,Li2019particleroboticsba,Vsrhelyi2018optimizedflockingo,Zion2023morphologicalcomput}.

The favorable rating indicates better
documentation, not physical irrelevance.
Distributions of agent properties would
permit stronger tests of heterogeneity-dependent
ordering, transport, and stability.

\paragraph*{Finite-size and boundary effects.}

D3 is a major limitation. Only 39 studies
receive low-risk ratings, 445 have some
concerns, and seven are high risk.
Just 7.9\% of rated extraction records
are assessed as low risk.

Robotic collectives often occupy small,
bounded arenas. Boundaries can influence
a substantial fraction of agents and alter
the states predicted for extended systems
\cite{Deblais2018boundariescontrolc,Boudet2025effectsofboundarie,Casiulis2025ageometricconditio,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Zhang2026emergentnonhermiti,Coulais2025activemetamaterials,Wagner2026geometryinducedtra}.

A boundary-accumulation measurement does
not establish how confinement affects
a bulk transition. Similarly, persistent
polarization in a finite group does not
demonstrate thermodynamic long-range order;
a dense aggregate does not necessarily
establish phase coexistence; and localized
mechanical response need not represent
a size-independent material property.

D3 therefore limits broad theoretical
claims more strongly than individual
experimental observations.

\paragraph*{Measurement validity.}

For D4, 380 studies are rated low risk,
106 have some concerns, and five are
high risk. Measurement methods are
generally better documented than
finite-size analysis or theory validation.

Robotic platforms permit direct tracking,
microscopic trajectory measurements,
and mechanical-response characterization
\cite{Tarcai2011patternstransition,Buerle2020formationofstable,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Hanson2025electrokineticpropu,Engbring2023anonlinearfluctuat}.

Validity is nevertheless observable-dependent.
Tracking sufficient for center-of-mass motion
may not resolve short-range correlations;
visualized geometry may not constrain
forces or fluctuations. Future extraction
should characterize resolution,
uncertainty, tracking failure,
and independent calibration.

\paragraph*{Data and code availability.}

D5 shows the clearest reporting deficit:
38 studies receive low-risk ratings,
57 have some concerns, and 396
are high risk.

Explicit code availability is recorded
for 71 studies (14.5\% of the
489-study evidence base), and data
availability for 54 (11.0\%).

Availability was coded positively only
when a repository or archive was named.
Missing availability therefore does not
establish unwillingness to share.

Controller code defines programmed
interactions; feedback implementations
determine state-dependent coupling;
calibration and trajectory data constrain
stochastic models; and mechanical-response
data support estimates of constitutive
coefficients. Without these materials,
independent verification may remain
difficult even when the qualitative
observation is clear.

Sharing trajectories, calibrations,
controllers, and analysis code
could improve reproducibility without
requiring new experimental hardware.

\paragraph*{Quantitative theory comparison.}

For D6, 83 studies are rated low risk,
146 have some concerns, and 262
are high risk. Only 16.9\% of
the rated extraction records
receive a low-risk assessment.

The agreement field provides a distinct measure:
126 studies report quantitative agreement,
238 qualitative agreement, 124 no comparison,
and three have missing values.

Reported quantitative agreement can coexist
with methodological concerns, including
parameters fitted to the validation data
or inadequate uncertainty estimates.
Qualitative comparisons remain valuable
for identifying mechanisms but support
different claims.

Experiments connecting microscopic interactions
with collective observables provide some
of the strongest theoretical evidence
\cite{Buerle2020formationofstable,Xiao2023perceptionofmotion,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Zion2023morphologicalcomput,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe}.

Nevertheless, the extraction does not
systematically distinguish fitted descriptions
from independently constrained predictions.
The conclusion is not that theoretical
models generally fail, but that
discriminating tests remain limited.

\paragraph*{Joint limitations.}

The six domains interact. Quantitative agreement
is less convincing without documented repetitions;
continuum comparisons may be constrained by
finite size even when measurements are accurate.

Only 30 studies in the extracted quality
assessment receive low-risk ratings in
both D1 and D6, equivalent to 6.1\%
of the 489-study evidence base
\cite{Garnier2005aggregationbehaviou,Buerle2020formationofstable,Yang2022emergentmicroroboti,Xiao2023perceptionofmotion,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,Casiulis2025ageometricconditio,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Zion2023morphologicalcomput,Li2020programmingactiveg}.

This subset is useful for sensitivity analysis,
but favorable ratings in two domains do not
establish low risk in the others or
independent replication across research groups.

We therefore assess certainty by scientific
claim, rather than treating the entire
literature as uniformly reliable
or unreliable.

\subsection{Reporting bias and publication status}
\label{sec:discussion-reporting}

Publication status provides one possible indicator
of reporting differences, but it is not a
direct measure of scientific quality.
Preprints may contain complete experimental
methods and quantitative comparisons,
while peer-reviewed articles may omit
important details.

The review protocol specifies a comparison
between preprint-only and peer-reviewed
findings. However, the extraction contains
venue labels rather than a reconciled
publication-status variable. A record
listed under arXiv or bioRxiv may have
a corresponding peer-reviewed version
outside the included table. We can
therefore compare venue labels
descriptively, but cannot reliably
distinguish preprint-only from
peer-reviewed studies.

In the previously tabulated venue
extraction, 47 records have venues
explicitly containing arXiv or bioRxiv;
445 have other or missing venue labels.
These are historical classification
counts, not a reconciled breakdown
of the current 489-study evidence base.

Among the 47 preprint-labelled records,
18 report quantitative theoretical
agreement, 21 qualitative agreement,
and eight no comparison. Their
corresponding proportions are
38.3\%, 44.7\%, and 17.0\%.

Among the other 445 records,
109 report quantitative agreement,
217 qualitative agreement,
116 no comparison, and three
have missing values.
Quantitative agreement is recorded
for 24.5\% of this group.

Thus, preprint-labelled records
contain a higher proportion of
quantitative comparisons, but
this cannot be interpreted
as a publication-bias effect.
The groups may differ in
discipline, year, and platform,
and do not reliably distinguish
publication histories.

For D6, ten preprint-labelled
studies are rated low risk,
20 have some concerns,
and 17 are high risk.
Among the other records,
73 are rated low risk,
126 have some concerns,
245 are high risk,
and one lacks a rating.

For replication, nine
preprint-labelled studies
are low risk,
37 have some concerns,
and one is high risk.
Among the remaining records,
117 are low risk,
311 have some concerns,
16 are high risk,
and one lacks a rating.

These differences do not suggest
that the preprint-labelled subset
is uniformly weaker under the
extracted quality criteria.
Nevertheless, the comparison
cannot determine whether positive
collective phenomena are more
likely to be published.

The evidence base contains
reported studies, not a
representative sample of
unsuccessful experiments,
unpublished null results,
or abandoned comparisons.
Selective reporting may
also occur within studies
when only observables
showing clear effects
are published.

Without protocols, complete
datasets, and publication-history
reconciliation, we cannot estimate
the magnitude of these biases
or apply a reliable numerical
correction.

The evidence therefore supports
a qualitative concern about
selective reporting rather than
a quantitative estimate.
Future living-review updates
should reconcile preprints
with published counterparts
and, where possible, document
null-condition runs and
unsuccessful experiments.

\subsection{Certainty of the main conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion-certainty}

We translate the evidence into high, moderate,
and low certainty judgments for the principal
scientific conclusions. Following the prespecified
framework, we consider experimental breadth,
independent support, replication, and the
D1 and D6 assessments.

These ratings apply to particular claims,
not to entire platforms or phenomenon families.

\paragraph*{Robots can realize collective active behavior: high certainty.}

Physical robotic collectives exhibiting coordinated
motion, spatial organization, transport, and
reconfiguration are documented across
programmed mobile robots, mechanically driven
particles, microscopic assemblies, and
modular systems
\cite{Rubenstein2014programmableselfas,Vsrhelyi2018optimizedflockingo,Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Li2019particleroboticsba,Yigit2019programmablecollect,Buerle2020formationofstable,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic,Yang2022emergentmicroroboti,Xi2024emergentintelligenc,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork}.

These observations do not depend on one
theoretical interpretation. We therefore
assign high certainty to the existence
of measurable collective active behavior
in robotic systems, without extending
that rating automatically to the
mechanisms proposed to explain it.

\paragraph*{Collective mechanisms generalize across platforms:
moderate certainty.}

Related collective phenomena occur under
different propulsion and interaction
mechanisms, including ordering,
mechanically mediated transport,
clustering, and rotation
\cite{Tarcai2011patternstransition,Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove,Buerle2020formationofstable,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Luo2025flockingphasesepar,Casiulis2025ageometricconditio}.

This supports moderate certainty that
related collective mechanisms can be
realized across multiple platforms.
However, similar patterns can arise
from different microscopic interactions,
and normalized information about
independent experimental groups is lacking.
We therefore do not infer a common
universality class.

\paragraph*{Specific universal phase behavior:
low certainty.}

Universal flocking transitions,
motility-induced phase separation,
and thermodynamic scaling require
more than observations of ordered
or clustered states.

Although individual studies provide
quantitative evidence
\cite{Tarcai2011patternstransition,Buerle2020formationofstable,Xiao2023perceptionofmotion,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Verdoucq2025flockingphasetrans},
few studies receive low-risk
finite-size and boundary ratings,
and qualitative comparisons
remain prevalent.

The extraction does not establish
consistent finite-size scaling
or independent validation across
the literature. We therefore
assign low certainty to broad
claims of universal phase behavior,
without excluding stronger
conclusions under particular
experimental conditions.

\paragraph*{Quantitative theory--experiment agreement:
moderate certainty.}

Quantitative comparisons encompass
Vicsek-type models, active Brownian
particles, kinetic and hydrodynamic
theories, active elasticity, and
other statistical-mechanical
descriptions.

Several experiments connect
microscopic dynamics to measured
collective observables
\cite{Buerle2020formationofstable,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Xiao2023perceptionofmotion,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,Hanson2025electrokineticpropu,Zion2023morphologicalcomput}.

We assign moderate certainty
that quantitative active-matter
models successfully describe
selected robotic experiments.

However, the agreement labels
do not systematically distinguish
fitted descriptions from
independent predictions.
General predictive validity
across platforms therefore
receives low certainty.

\paragraph*{Programmability provides causal experimental control:
high certainty.}

Robotic systems permit controlled
changes in interaction rules,
feedback, sensing, and actuation.
This capability is demonstrated
in mobile swarms, feedback-controlled
colloids, and active structures
\cite{Ferrante2013aselfadaptivecomm,Li2019particleroboticsba,Buerle2020formationofstable,Chen2022collectiveresponse,Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Coulais2025activemetamaterials}.

We assign high certainty to
the experimental accessibility
of interaction parameters.

The stronger conclusion that
programmability has already
enabled decisive tests of
general active-matter theories
receives moderate certainty.
Low-risk quantitative comparisons
remain limited, and controllers
may directly impose the observed
collective behavior.

\paragraph*{Experimental reproducibility is adequately documented:
low certainty.}

Reporting indicators show
substantial missingness in
independent-run counts,
data availability, and
code availability.

Only a minority of studies
receive low-risk ratings
in both replication and
quantitative theory comparison.

We therefore assign low certainty
to the claim that the literature
provides documentation sufficient
for broad independent reproduction
of its quantitative conclusions.

This assessment concerns the
available reporting, not a
claim that the experiments
themselves are generally
irreproducible.

\subsection{Limitations of the evidence}
\label{sec:discussion-evidence-limitations}

The certainty judgments above must be interpreted in light
of limitations extending beyond individual risk-of-bias
domains. These concern the composition of the evidence
base, comparability of experimental platforms, the
distinction between phenomena and mechanisms,
and selective reporting.

The October 17, 2026 evidence base comprises
489 studies. Its breadth is a strength, but
the included literature is not a representative
sample of every attempt to realize collective
active behavior. Publication, indexing, retrieval,
and eligibility assessment all affect the
available evidence.

\paragraph*{Heterogeneity of physical implementations.}

The included platforms differ in propulsion,
sensing, interactions, size, and control
architecture. These differences are scientifically
useful but limit direct pooling of results.

Collective ordering in communicating robots
\cite{Turgut2008selforganizedflocka,Vsrhelyi2018optimizedflockingo,Zhou2024anticipationpromote}
need not arise from the same effective interactions
as ordering in mechanically driven systems
\cite{Welch2019activereorientation,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Luo2025flockingphasesepar}
or feedback-controlled colloids
\cite{Buerle2020formationofstable,Chen2024synchronousandfull,Chen2024persistentandrespo}.

Order parameters may be similar while
interaction range, conservation laws,
noise, and information transfer differ.
Phenomenological replication must therefore
be distinguished from mechanistic replication:
the reproduction of a collective pattern
does not necessarily confirm the same theory.

Our synthesis consequently emphasizes
measured observables and interaction mechanisms
rather than treating every occurrence
of a phenomenon as equivalent evidence.

\paragraph*{Small systems and finite experimental arenas.}

Many studies use small collectives because
agents require tracking, calibration,
or restricted experimental arenas.
Such systems can reveal pairwise coupling
and local response, but are less decisive
for theories involving large-system scaling.

Stable polarization in a small flock
does not establish long-range order.
A confined aggregate does not necessarily
demonstrate macroscopic phase coexistence.
Likewise, finite mechanical assemblies
can exhibit nonreciprocal response
without establishing a size-independent
constitutive law
\cite{Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe,Coulais2025activemetamaterials}.

The unfavorable D3 assessment therefore
limits generalization from finite experiments
to universal active-matter behavior.
More publications using similar system sizes
cannot by themselves resolve this problem.

\paragraph*{Outcome definitions and theoretical specificity.}

The phenomenon taxonomy accommodates terminology
from different communities, but some categories
combine theoretically distinct outcomes.

Clustering or MIPS encompasses programmed
attraction, field-mediated assembly,
contact-driven aggregation, and other
forms of spatial organization. Only some
of these directly test motility-induced
phase separation.

Likewise, chirality or rotation,
swarming or vortices, and self-assembly
or shape formation include several
mechanisms. Programmed assemblies
\cite{Rubenstein2014programmableselfas,Li2019particleroboticsba,Yi2025reconfigurablerobot}
and microscopic field-driven collectives
\cite{Pauer2021programmabledesign,Sun2024multiplemagnetoopt,Cheng2025selfassemblyandco}
can share a phenomenon label while
addressing different physical questions.

A common label does not justify pooling
order parameters, effect sizes, or
agreement statistics. We therefore
avoided a pooled effect estimate.
This reflects genuine differences
in mechanisms, experimental conditions,
and quantities being estimated,
not merely missing numerical data.

\paragraph*{Independence of experiments and research groups.}

Publication counts are not equivalent to
independent experimental confirmations.
Several studies may share apparatus,
controllers, datasets, or research programs.

The extraction contains bibliographic metadata
and experimental descriptors, but no fully
reconciled mapping of shared apparatus,
experimental lineages, or independent groups.
We therefore cannot reliably count
independent replications of specific
theoretical claims.

Cross-platform evidence partly addresses
this concern, but platform diversity
does not guarantee independent groups
or equivalent theoretical tests.

\paragraph*{Null results and selective outcome reporting.}

The literature may disproportionately represent
successful collective demonstrations, but
the magnitude of this bias cannot be
estimated from included publications.

The evidence base contains no registry
of attempted experiments, unsuccessful
implementations, or unreported parameter
sweeps. Selective reporting may also
occur within studies that measure several
observables but emphasize the clearest effects.

These concerns are particularly relevant
when models have adjustable parameters
or controllers are optimized to produce
specific patterns. Missing datasets
and analysis implementations further
limit retrospective assessment.

We therefore do not interpret the
frequency of positive agreement labels
as an unbiased estimate of a
theory's probability of being correct.

\paragraph*{Concentration of theoretical evidence.}

Theoretical comparisons are distributed
unevenly across frameworks and platforms.
Agent-based simulation is widespread,
whereas run-and-tumble models and
active elasticity have smaller
experimental literatures.

More comparisons do not necessarily
mean stronger theoretical support.
Independent parameter constraints,
measurement uncertainty, and
predictions outside fitting conditions
matter more than comparison counts.

Although the extraction records
agreement type, it does not
systematically distinguish fitted
from independently predicted
quantities for every study.
We therefore cannot assign
a numerical predictive-validity
score across theoretical frameworks.

\subsection{Limitations of this review}
\label{sec:discussion-review-limitations}

The review also has limitations arising from its
search strategy, eligibility rules, automated
assistance, extraction process, and treatment
of incomplete reports. These must be distinguished
from shortcomings in the underlying experiments.

We used a prespecified protocol,
PRISMA-based selection accounting,
a controlled extraction vocabulary,
and documented validation and adjudication.
These procedures improve transparency
without eliminating uncertainty.

\paragraph*{Database coverage and retrieval.}

The search identified 15\,034 database records.
After removing 6\,332 duplicates,
8\,702 records were screened.
The final evidence base contains 489 studies.

Database searching cannot guarantee complete
coverage of robotic active matter. The field
crosses physics, robotics, materials science,
and engineering, whose terminology differs.
Experiments investigating active mechanical
response may not use the phrase ``active matter'';
studies of distributed robotic control may
report collective fluctuations without identifying
them as active-matter phenomena.

Broader search strings may improve coverage,
but also increase irrelevant retrieval.
The eligibility criteria likewise define
the scope: we required interacting physical
artificial agents and at least one quantitative
collective or interaction-level observable.

Simulation-only studies without the specified
hardware connection were excluded, as were
engineering studies reporting solely task
performance without a qualifying physical
observable. These exclusions serve the
review objectives; they do not imply
that the excluded research is unimportant.

\paragraph*{Incomplete retrieval and abstract-only assessment.}

The selection flow distinguishes
full-text from abstract-only assessment.
Of 976 reports assessed on full text,
459 were included,
513 excluded, and four awaited
a decision.

Another 179 reports were assessed
on abstracts because full text
was unavailable: 30 were included
and 149 excluded. The 662
eligibility exclusions therefore
comprise 513 full-text and
149 abstract-only exclusions.

Following record reconciliation,
the final 489-study evidence base
contains 30 abstract-only inclusions.
The remaining 459 studies were
assessed on full text. The four
reports awaiting a decision remain
outside the included evidence base.

Abstract-only assessment creates
specific uncertainties. An abstract
may establish that an experiment
involved interacting physical agents
and a collective observable without
documenting independent repetitions,
measurement validity, controller
implementation, or quantitative
theory comparison.

Such records are consequently more
susceptible to missing extraction
fields and ambiguous classification.
We retained them under the specified
eligibility procedure but avoided
unsupported detailed conclusions.

A sensitivity analysis excluding
abstract-only inclusions would
establish whether the synthesis
depends materially on these records.
It would not recover their
missing methodological information.

\paragraph*{LLM-assisted screening and extraction.}

Large language models assisted screening,
extraction, and parts of adjudication.
This enabled structured assessment
of a substantial literature but
introduced additional potential errors.

A model may misidentify physical
hardware, interaction between agents,
or whether an observable is genuinely
collective. It may also assign
a platform or phenomenon label
from terminology that does not
adequately describe the experiment.

Such errors can be systematic,
particularly when familiar categories
are favored or collective task
performance is confused with
collective active dynamics.
Controlled vocabularies improve
consistency but may force
classifications when evidence
is incomplete.

Automated extraction is therefore
an evidence-processing procedure
requiring validation, not an
independent source of
scientific authority.

\paragraph*{Blind validation versus final-label validation.}

The validation exercise must be interpreted
according to the reference labels used.

The first author labels were assigned
blindly on a 200-record sample.
Against those initial judgments,
panel sensitivity was 0.290,
specificity 0.917, and
Cohen's $\kappa=0.232$.

Against the final adjudicated labels,
sensitivity was 1.000,
specificity 0.983, and
$\kappa=0.922$.

These estimates are not interchangeable.
The first measures agreement with
initial blind judgments; the second
measures agreement with labels
established after review and
adjudication.

The improvement characterizes the
final classification process, but
does not constitute a fresh blind
validation against an untouched
external gold standard. Reference
labels changed during adjudication,
and shared errors remain possible.

We therefore report the initial
blind comparison alongside the
final-label analysis instead of
treating the latter as an
independent estimate of screening
accuracy.

\paragraph*{The Opus judge and adjudication dependence.}

Claude Opus served as an adjudicator
for conflicting screening decisions
and separately as a blind screener
on the validation sample.

Across 423 full-text screening
disagreements, the judge agreed
with the panel in 362 cases,
reversed 59 decisions, and
left two undecided. These
decisions materially affected
the resulting classifications.

The agreement rate is not an
independent accuracy estimate.
Some judgments involved direct
consultation of full text;
others relied on evidence
available through the
adjudication workflow.
Different disagreement types
may therefore have received
different levels of verification.

Although the decisions are
documented for audit,
the judge may share systematic
interpretive tendencies with
the models it adjudicated.

Evaluated separately as a
blind screener against final
author labels, Opus achieved
sensitivity 0.550,
specificity 1.000, and
Cohen's $\kappa=0.687$.
These figures cannot be
combined with adjudication
agreement because the tasks
and information conditions differ.

Extraction adjudication identified
167 conflicting fields, of which
18 were ultimately assigned null.
These missing values reduce the
precision of some comparisons,
but are preferable to unsupported
numerical or categorical assignments.

\paragraph*{The residual platform category.}

The platform taxonomy contains
a substantial residual ``other''
class: 87 of the 489 studies,
or approximately 17.8\%.

These records encompass physically
different systems and cannot be
interpreted as one coherent
experimental mechanism.

The category limits the resolution
of platform comparisons and indicates
a need for future taxonomic refinement.
Nevertheless, retaining ``other''
is preferable to forcing systems
into inaccurate hardware classes.

Four additional records have no
assigned platform class. We preserve
that missingness rather than inferring
an assignment without supporting evidence.

Future versions may classify systems
more effectively through interaction
mechanisms, control architecture,
and physical constraints rather
than hardware appearance alone.

\paragraph*{Limitations of the extracted variables.}

The extraction table supports
descriptive and critical synthesis,
but cannot resolve every question
relevant to active-matter physics.

Agreement labels do not establish
whether theoretical parameters
were measured independently,
fitted to experimental data,
or adjusted after observing
the collective response.

Study-level risk-of-bias fields
likewise cannot replace detailed,
claim-specific evaluation of
every reported observable.

The maximum hardware agent count
is an incomplete measure of
experimental scale. Microscopic
populations may contain many
particles without resolving
individual trajectories; connected
active solids may contain many
actuators but comparatively few
independent collective modes.

Theoretical categories are also
multi-label. A study comparing
agent-based simulation with an
active Brownian model can appear
in both groups. Framework totals
therefore cannot be interpreted
as independent study counts.

These limitations motivate
the narrative, mechanism-oriented
synthesis adopted here rather
than a pooled numerical estimate.

\subsection{What the living design can and cannot fix}
\label{sec:discussion-living}

The living design keeps the evidence base current
as robotic active-matter experiments appear.
Monthly searches, versioned screening decisions,
extraction records, and updated syntheses permit
corrections and incorporation of new evidence.
They do not automatically improve the reliability
of the underlying experiments.

\paragraph*{What updating can correct.}

Monthly searches can identify newly indexed studies,
revised preprints, and previously inaccessible
publications. When full texts become available,
abstract-only inclusions can be reassessed,
improving eligibility decisions and extraction
of experimental methods.

The workflow can also reconcile duplicate records,
publication versions, and missing classifications.
Such corrections may change descriptive counts
without changing the underlying scientific
literature. Every numerical claim must therefore
refer to a dated evidence snapshot, with revisions
recorded in the change log.

Future updates can refine platform assignments,
reconcile preprints with peer-reviewed publications,
and incorporate additional methodological details.
A newly accessible supplement, for example, may
document independent repetitions or provide data
and code, allowing a risk-of-bias rating to change
without any new experiment.

\paragraph*{Updating methodological validation.}

Screening models, prompts, adjudication rules,
and extraction procedures may change over time.
These changes can affect study inclusion and
classification and must therefore be versioned.

The October 17, 2026 validation describes
the workflow evaluated at that time, not
the performance of subsequent models or
procedures. Substantial methodological changes
should trigger renewed validation against
independently assessed records.

Future reports must continue to distinguish
initial blind labels from final adjudicated
reference labels. Otherwise, apparent
improvements in agreement may reflect
changes in the reference standard rather
than screening performance.

Likewise, the Opus judge's adjudication
decisions must remain distinct from
its evaluation as a blind screener.
These activities address different
questions and cannot be combined
into one accuracy estimate.

\paragraph*{Updating certainty assessments.}

A living synthesis should assess how new
studies affect specific scientific claims,
not merely increase publication counts.

Another flocking demonstration may broaden
the inventory of experimental platforms
without strengthening evidence for a
universal ordering transition.
By contrast, one well-designed finite-size
study could materially improve certainty
about a particular theoretical prediction.

Similarly, independent measurements of
nonreciprocal elasticity may provide
stronger evidence for active-solid theory
than several additional demonstrations
of programmable mechanical motion.

Certainty should therefore change when
new evidence alters quantitative agreement,
risk of bias, or independent cross-platform
support. Contradictory results may warrant
downgrading a conclusion, just as strong
replication may justify upgrading it.

\paragraph*{What updating cannot repair.}

A living review cannot supply independent
experimental repetitions that were never
performed or documented. It cannot establish
thermodynamic-limit behavior from small
experiments without relevant size-dependent
measurements.

Nor will more frequent searching eliminate
publication bias when unsuccessful experiments
remain unpublished. Revalidation can reveal
errors in automated screening and extraction,
but cannot guarantee the absence of systematic
bias shared by models and adjudicators.

Most importantly, additional qualitative
agreement cannot substitute for independent
predictive validation. That requires experiments
designed to distinguish competing explanations.

\paragraph*{A stable protocol with revisable judgments.}

Eligibility criteria must remain sufficiently
stable for meaningful comparisons across
snapshots. Changes in definitions or
procedures should be documented as protocol
amendments rather than introduced silently.

Taxonomic classifications, however, should
remain revisable when the evidence warrants it.
The residual platform category may eventually
be subdivided according to distinct physical
mechanisms. Any such revision should include
consistent reclassification of earlier
records wherever possible.

Future extraction could also distinguish
fitted from independently predicted parameters,
spontaneous self-assembly from prescribed
formation, and independent experimental groups
from publications sharing apparatus or data.

These refinements would improve scientific
interpretation, provided that classification
changes remain distinguishable from genuine
changes in the underlying evidence.

\paragraph*{Implications for interpretation.}

The principal contribution of the living design
is epistemic rather than merely administrative.
It preserves an auditable connection between
evidence, classification, certainty, and
scientific conclusions.

New studies can challenge existing interpretations;
additional full texts can resolve uncertain records;
and renewed validation can expose limitations
in the review workflow.

The governing standard nevertheless remains
unchanged: the strength of a conclusion depends
on the observations and tests supporting it,
not on how frequently the review is updated.

\subsection{Overall interpretation}
\label{sec:discussion-overall}

Taken together, the evidence establishes
robotic active matter as a versatile
experimental approach to collective
nonequilibrium physics.

Physical robotic collectives realize
a broad range of organized behaviors
through programmed, mechanical,
and field-mediated interactions.
Programmability also provides a means
of changing the microscopic rules
thought to determine collective behavior.

However, theoretical interpretation
requires greater care than the visual
similarity of collective states suggests.
The evidence is less decisive for
universal phase behavior, large-system
scaling, and the independent predictive
validity of particular models.

The risk-of-bias assessment identifies
three major constraints: incomplete
replication reporting, limited
finite-size characterization, and
insufficiently documented quantitative
theory tests. Limited data and code
availability further restricts
independent verification.

These limitations occur across
experimental platforms, although
their importance depends on the
scientific question.

The living design can improve
coverage, resolve incomplete records,
correct classifications, and update
certainty assessments. It cannot
replace experiments that distinguish
physical mechanisms.

We therefore regard the next stage
of robotic active-matter research
as a transition from realizing
collective behavior to testing
it predictively.

The most informative experiments
will characterize microscopic
interactions, measure collective
observables with independent
replication, vary relevant
parameters systematically,
and test predictions outside
the conditions used for fitting.

Cross-platform replication can
then establish which principles
are general and which depend
on specific actuation, sensing,
or mechanical implementations.

The value of robotic active matter
lies not only in constructing
controllable nonequilibrium systems,
but in using that control to make
physical theories vulnerable
to precise experimental tests.
