\section{Collective phenomena in robotic active matter}
\label{sec:phenomena}

The platform taxonomy describes how activity is implemented.
We now turn to what these systems actually do. This distinction
is central to active-matter physics. Two robotic collectives may
exhibit similar motion despite using different propulsion and
interaction mechanisms. Conversely, changing a single control
parameter may produce qualitatively different collective states
on the same hardware.

We organize the evidence by ten families of collective phenomena.
The categories follow the reconciled extraction vocabulary and
are not mutually exclusive. A rotating cluster, for example, may
contribute to clustering, swarming, and chirality. We therefore
interpret each family through its reported observables and
theoretical comparisons, rather than treating the number of
classified studies as independent confirmations of a physical law.

The evidence base contains 489 studies in the corrected snapshot
of October 17, 2026. Within this corpus, our central question
is not whether a phenomenon has been demonstrated. It is which
mechanism the experiment establishes, which predictions have
been tested, and whether the finding survives changes in
platform, scale, and experimental conditions.



\subsection{Flocking and polar order}
\label{sec:phenomena-flocking}

Flocking is among the most recognizable signatures of collective
active matter. Individual agents move in different directions,
yet their interactions may produce a common orientation or
direction of travel. At the microscopic level, a common
description involves local alignment and fluctuations.
At the collective level, we seek a nonzero polarization
and correlations that persist over relevant spatial
and temporal scales.

A useful starting point is the polarization
\begin{equation}
P(t)=\left|
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\mathbf v}_i(t)
\right|,
\label{eq:phenomena-polarization}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf v}_i$ denotes the unit velocity
direction of agent $i$. This definition separates
directional coherence from absolute speed. For
agents with well-defined headings, an analogous
orientational order parameter may be used.

Note that a nonzero polarization in a finite
system is not sufficient evidence for a
thermodynamic ordered phase. A small group can
remain aligned through direct control, geometric
constraints, or transient correlations. Claims
about collective ordering therefore require
attention to system size, fluctuations, and
the dependence on interaction parameters
\cite{Vicsek1995novel}
\cite{Toner1995longrange}.

Flocking or polar order is recorded in 155
studies. Wheeled or legged swarm robots account
for 93, while 52 belong to the heterogeneous
``other'' category. Vibration-driven robots
contribute five studies, feedback-controlled
colloids four, and smarticles or granular
robots one. Thus, approximately nine tenths
of the classified flocking literature lies
in conventional mobile swarms and residual
robotic implementations.



\paragraph*{Programmed alignment and local information.}

Early robotic flocking experiments established
that relatively simple interaction rules could
produce coordinated movement in physical
collectives
\cite{Hayes2003selforganizedflock,Kelly2004ascalableonboard,Regmi2005experimentalimpleme,Sugawara2006collectivemotionan,Turgut2008modelingphasetrans,Turgut2008selforganizedflocka,Navarro2008anapproachtoflock,Turgut2008selforganizedflockb,Wang2008alocalsensorbased,Gu2009leaderfollowerfloc,Wang2009leaderfollowerfloc,Gke2010theprosandconsof,Miki2010asimplerulebased,Ercan2011swarmrobotflocking,Tarcai2011patternstransition,Fine2011flockingdontneed,Hauert2011reynoldsflockingin}.
These systems demonstrate that local rules can
generate coordination despite real actuator,
sensor, and environmental constraints.

The strongest physical interpretation is obtained
when the experiment measures more than a successful
group trajectory. Polarization, orientation
distributions, and velocity correlations can
establish the degree of collective alignment.
Their dependence on local density, interaction
range, or noise can then distinguish different
mechanisms.

Studies with experimentally reported flocking
observables include investigations of alignment,
collective trajectories, and order-related
statistics
\cite{Tarcai2011patternstransition,Virgh2014flockingalgorithmf,Vsrhelyi2014outdoorflockingand,Zoss2018distributedsystemo,Vsrhelyi2018optimizedflockingo,Berlinger2021selforganizedevasi,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Zhou2024anticipationpromote,Castro2025visualcollectivebe,Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc}.
These measurements provide a more direct connection
to active-matter models than endpoint performance
alone.



Examples include the stability of an ordered
state under disturbances, the onset of
collective coherence, spatial fluctuations,
and the response to interaction asymmetry.
These properties can distinguish a merely
functional flocking algorithm from an
experimental model of active-matter physics.

More recent systems extend the range of
information and control mechanisms.
The included literature covers predictive
coordination, visual interaction,
learning-based approaches, and
decentralized collective response
\cite{Schilling2019learningvisionbase,Brandsttter2022multiagentspatial,Lee2022distributedcohesive,Gao2023robustdistributedc,Baumann2024hybridflockforma,Karagzel2024selforganizedflock,Horyna2024fastswarmingofuav,Zheng2024emergenceofcollect,Liu2024optimizationbasedf,Wu2024adversarialimitatio,Li2025llmflockdecentral,Tan2025goflockgoalorien,Zhang2025learningefficientf,Zheng2025shapingcollectiveb,Choi2026communicationfreec,Jond2026positionbasedflock}.
Their diversity reveals that common collective
patterns can be obtained using substantially
different information-processing architectures.



\paragraph*{Order, response, and the meaning of a transition.}



Changing communication radius may alter
both the number of neighbors and the
frequency of control updates. Increasing
robot speed may change collision frequency,
turning accuracy, and the influence
of boundaries. Consequently, a measured
change in polarization cannot always
be attributed to one theoretical parameter.

Experiments involving collective ordering,
gradient response, and spatial organization
provide several approaches to this problem
\cite{Ferrante2013aselfadaptivecomm,Karagzel2022collectivegradient,Lee2022distributeddeformab,PliegoJimnez2023flockingandformati,Chen2024synchronousandfull,Zheng2024bodyorientationcha,Verdoucq2025flockingphasetrans,Zhou2025activesearchpromot,PitaRomero2026flockingbehaviorfo,Rezeck2026decentralizedemerge,Yan2026flockingbehaviorfo}.
However, the extraction fields do not establish
that every such study measured a phase
transition or performed finite-size scaling.
The strength of each physical conclusion
must be determined from its specific
observables and comparisons.

The distinction between polar order and
coordinated formation is particularly
important. Robots may maintain a prescribed
formation while moving in a common direction.
This produces collective coherence, but
the formation is maintained by feedback
designed for that purpose.

Such experiments are informative about
the physical implementation of
distributed interactions. They are
less decisive as tests of spontaneous
symmetry breaking unless the collective
state emerges without directly prescribed
global organization.

Studies involving leader--follower
coordination, formation response,
and distributed flocking illustrate
the range of this distinction
\cite{Lei2006asequencableflocki,La2011decentralizedflocki,Min2011designandanalysis,Wang2012flockingbaseddistr,Jia2014experimentalimpleme,Mao2014flockingofmultiro,Lee2014distributedcohesive,Savkin2015distributedformatio,Jia2015leaderfollowerfloc,Sakai2016flockingformultiro,Shirazi2017astrategyforself,Khaledyan2019flockingandtarget,Zheng2020experimentalimpleme,Ibuki2020optimizationbasedd,Zheng2021optimizingparameter,Zhang2022collectivefissionb,MendozaRobles2026distributedformatio}.
The broader literature also examines how
adaptive control, environmental information,
and collective objectives influence
group motion
\cite{Fine2012examiningtheinform,elikkanat2010steeringselforgani,Toshiyuki2016adaptiveroleassign,Yasuda2018generatingandanaly,OrdazRivas2018collectivetasksfor,Horyna2022decentralizedswarms,nr2022mindthegappredic,nr2024predictivesearchmo,CetinaDenis2025flockingcontrolfor,Bahaidarah2024swarmflockingusing,Soria2021predictivecontrolo,Huang2024atiassembletopolo,Petrek2020bioinspiredcompact,Novk2021fastcollectiveevas,Ahmad2022pacnavacollective,Balzs2020adaptiveleadership,Liu2020computationalandro}.
These studies should be interpreted through
their actual collective observables rather
than through the common term ``flocking''
alone.

\paragraph*{Nonreciprocity and physical realization.}

Flocking is especially interesting when
the effective interaction is not reciprocal.
One agent may respond to another more
strongly than the reverse, or its response
may depend on relative orientation and
perception. Such asymmetry can change
the stability and dynamics of collective
states.

The included literature contains experiments
addressing nonreciprocal or asymmetric
collective responses
\cite{Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,Buerle2020formationofstable,Chen2022collectiveresponse,Lffler2023collectiveforaging,Jianchao2024emergentchiralitya,Luo2025flockingphasesepar,Casiulis2025ageometricconditio}.
These studies are particularly relevant
because interaction asymmetry provides
a possible distinction from simplified
reciprocal alignment models.



Mechanically driven systems offer
a complementary perspective.
Experiments on vibration-driven
collectives investigate motion
and ordering arising through
physical coupling rather than
explicit communication
\cite{Welch2019activereorientation,Deblais2018boundariescontrolc,Escobar2025effectofparticlec,Boudet2021fromcollectionsof}.
Although the number of such
studies is small relative to
programmed robot swarms,
they broaden the range of
physical mechanisms associated
with flocking.

Likewise, controlled colloidal
systems offer access to
particle-scale orientation
and collective order
\cite{Chen2024synchronousandfull,Buerle2020formationofstable,Lffler2023collectiveforaging,Chen2022collectiveresponse}.
Their importance lies in
providing collective observables
under different driving
and interaction conditions,
rather than simply increasing
the number of reported
flocking demonstrations.

Additional included experiments
investigate group motion and
coordination in aerial,
distributed, and other
robotic implementations
\cite{Zhou2021synchronizedswimmin,Beaver2022aconstraintdriven,Amorim2024aminimalistic3dse,Rezeck2021flockingsegregative,Albani2022distributedthreedi,Fernando2021onlineflockingcont,Brandsttter2023multiagentspatial,Brandstatter2024flockformationcont,Yu2024grfbasedpredictive,Amorim2021selforganizeduavf,Cioarg2010evaluationoffishs,Fine2011flockingdontneed,Hauert2011reynoldsflockingin,nr2023onboardpredictivef,Iacone2024decentralizedaccele,Wang2022optiswarmopticals,Abpeikar2025adualtaskdeeprei,Zhang2026noiseresilientimpl,Wang2026t2rswarmtransferr}.
These examples reinforce the breadth
of robotic alignment experiments,
but they do not remove the need
for platform-independent tests.

\paragraph*{Strength and limitations of the evidence.}

The 155 studies classified under flocking
or polar order contain 18 quantitative
theory comparisons, 87 qualitative
comparisons, and 50 without a recorded
comparison. Thus, fewer than one in
eight provide an extracted quantitative
comparison.

The risk-of-bias assessment gives a
similarly cautious picture.
For D6, 13 studies are rated low risk,
35 have some concerns, and 107
are rated high risk. Consequently,
the prevalence of experimental
flocking demonstrations greatly
exceeds the number of studies
providing strong quantitative
tests of theoretical predictions.

Replication evidence is more
substantial but still incomplete.
For D1, 39 studies are rated low
risk, 110 have some concerns,
and six are high risk.
Independent-run counts are
reported in 67 studies.
Explicit code availability
is recorded in 21,
and data availability in nine.

All 155 studies carrying the
flocking label were assessed
on a full-text basis in the
supplied extraction table.
This avoids the additional
uncertainty of abstract-only
assessment for this
particular phenomenon family.
Nevertheless, missing
replication and validation
information remains important.

The breadth of experimental
implementations provides
moderate support for the
feasibility and robustness
of coordinated collective
motion in robotic systems.
The evidence for a common
physical ordering mechanism
is more limited. Most
experiments use programmed
or informational interactions,
and comparatively few
report low-risk quantitative
theory comparisons.

We therefore distinguish
the established experimental
capability to realize flocks
from the stronger physical
claim that a particular
universality class has
been demonstrated.

The most informative future
experiments will measure
polarization, fluctuations,
and correlation functions
across controlled parameter
ranges and several system
sizes. Comparisons between
programmed, mechanical,
and feedback-mediated
alignment would be
especially valuable.

Such studies could establish
which aspects of polar
collective motion depend
on interaction details
and which survive changes
in robotic implementation.
This would turn the
experimental diversity
of flocking systems into
direct evidence about
active-matter theory.

\subsection{Clustering and motility-induced phase separation}
\label{sec:phenomena-clustering}

Clustering is a common collective outcome in active systems,
but its physical interpretation depends on how the agents
interact. Aggregation can arise from explicit attraction,
steric interactions, feedback, confinement, or reduced
motility at high density. These mechanisms may produce
similar cluster geometries while representing different
nonequilibrium processes.

Motility-induced phase separation (MIPS) is a more specific
theoretical phenomenon. In its simplest form, persistent
self-propelled particles accumulate because collisions
slow their motion, producing a positive feedback between
local density and reduced motility
\todocite{Cates and Tailleur, motility-induced phase separation}
\todocite{Fily and Marchetti, athermal phase separation of
self-propelled particles}. A dense cluster alone is
therefore insufficient evidence for MIPS. A stronger
test must connect the measured density organization
to the underlying persistence and interaction dynamics.

In the extracted taxonomy, clustering and MIPS are
combined into one phenomenon label. This choice
supports broad evidence mapping, but it requires
careful interpretation. We use the label to identify
experiments reporting collective aggregation or
density organization, not to imply that all of
them realize motility-induced phase separation.

The corrected evidence base contains 489 studies.
Clustering or MIPS is recorded in 109 studies.
Wheeled or legged swarm robots contribute 50,
vibration-driven robots 24, microscopic
robotic agents 21, feedback-controlled
colloids three, smarticles or granular robots
two, modular or self-reconfigurable robots
two, and other platforms seven.
Thus, clustering is represented across
substantially more physical interaction
mechanisms than the flocking category.

\paragraph*{Clustering through physical interactions.}

Mechanically driven robots provide an important
experimental route to clustering because their
collective organization need not be specified
by a software rule. Contact, friction,
propulsion persistence, and geometric constraints
can generate density inhomogeneities.

The included studies investigate aggregation,
collective motion, and configuration-dependent
behavior in such systems
\cite{Fersula2026aggregatingswarmst,Standish2016roboticswarmingwit,Wang2021fielddriverobotsw,Arvin2014comparisonofdiffer,Cates2015mips,Alaviani2024visionbasedlearnin,Porvatov2021optimizingselfrota,Hao2022controllingcollisio,Mayya2019nonuniformrobotde,Zhu2025fromspikestoswarm,Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Welch2019activereorientation,Deblais2019howtoforceanarmy,Arbel2025amechanicalroutef,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Boudet2025nonmarkoviandynami,Casiulis2025ageometricconditio,Luo2025flockingphasesepar,Deblais2018boundariescontrolc,Boudet2022effectivetemperatur,Musacchio2026fluidizationinduced,Zion2023morphologicalcomput}.
Their contribution is particularly relevant
to active Brownian particle descriptions,
which connect persistent motility and
steric interactions to collective density
organization.

However, vibration-driven agents may differ
from ideal active Brownian particles.
Their orientation dynamics can depend
on collisions, substrate properties,
and internal vibration. Consequently,
a model fitted to isolated-agent motion
may not describe their behavior at
high density without additional terms.

The strongest comparisons therefore combine
single-agent motility measurements with
collective observables such as cluster-size
distributions, pair correlations,
density profiles, or temporal relaxation.
They can test whether aggregation follows
from independently characterized motion,
rather than merely reproducing a
clustered steady state.

Smarticles and granular robots provide
a related but distinct physical regime.
Their collective organization can depend
strongly on shape, internal actuation,
mechanical constraints, and contact networks
\cite{Li2018supersmarticlealo,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Li2020programmingactiveg,Li2019particleroboticsba,Kaeser2025individualandcolle}.
In such systems, a cluster may be stabilized
by geometric or mechanical coupling rather
than by the density-dependent slowing
central to simple MIPS models.

This distinction matters because the
observed aggregate can look similar
in both cases. Mechanical measurements,
configuration statistics, and changes
in collective response are needed
to identify the relevant mechanism.

\paragraph*{Programmable aggregation.}

Wheeled or legged swarm robots form
the largest platform group in the
clustering category. These systems
often use sensing or communication
to generate attraction-like rules,
local recruitment, or adaptive
collective organization.

Early experiments established the
feasibility of distributed aggregation
and interaction-mediated group formation
\cite{Garnier2005aggregationbehaviou,Hamann2008spatialmacroscopic,Arvin2011imitationofhoneybe,Giardina2022collectivephototact,Mijalkov2016engineeringsensoria,Cazenille2016automatedcalibratio,Valentini2015collectivedecision,Guzzi2014bioinspiredobstacle}.
Such systems can reproduce collective
spatial organization using local
information rather than direct
central control.

Later experiments extend these ideas
toward programmable responses,
collective searching, and
configuration-dependent motion
\cite{Vega2023simulatelessexpec,Vega2023indirectswarmcontr,Vega2024agentbasedemulatio,Yang2023collectivechaseem,Sankar2026trendformationwith,Zhou2025activesearchpromot,Liu2026noiseenabledgoala,Dizenhaus2026anonymousleadership,Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc,Zhang2026emergentnonhermiti,Zheng2026nestedinteractionn,Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,Barone2024experimentalandnum,Zhou2024anticipationpromote,Rezeck2021flockingsegregative,Rezeck2026decentralizedemerge}.
These experiments demonstrate how
aggregation can be altered by
interaction rules or environmental
conditions.

Yet, programmed aggregation should
not be interpreted automatically
as an experimental realization
of motility-induced phase separation.
If robots explicitly move toward
neighboring agents, the microscopic
mechanism includes an attraction
rule absent from the simplest
MIPS description.

The distinction is scientifically useful.
Programmability permits direct comparisons
between different clustering mechanisms.
For example, one can implement explicit
attraction, density-dependent slowing,
or changes in persistence on the
same physical platform.

Such experiments could determine
whether similar density patterns
arise from different microscopic
dynamics and identify observables
capable of distinguishing them.

\paragraph*{Microscopic clustering and feedback.}

Microscopic robotic collectives provide
another route to aggregation through
magnetic, optical, acoustic, and
hydrodynamic interactions.

The included studies investigate
collective assembly, density organization,
and configuration-dependent behavior
\cite{Calabr2026interactiondrivena,Rivas2023spatialpatterningo,Yang2022emergentmicroroboti,Aubret2018targetedassemblyan,Yan2024programmingstructur,Yigit2020cohesiveselforgani,Hanson2025electrokineticpropu,Ceron2023programmableselfor}.
These systems can provide particle-scale
measurements of collective structure,
but their interactions may differ
substantially from steric-only
active Brownian models.

Magnetic coupling can produce
effective attraction or alignment.
Fluid-mediated interactions can
change relative motion.
Common external fields may impose
spatial organization. A density
inhomogeneity therefore requires
a mechanism-specific interpretation.

Feedback-controlled colloids offer
an especially useful comparison
because the effective interaction
can be modified without changing
the particle material.

Relevant experiments include
collective assembly and
configuration-dependent response
\cite{Yan2014colloidalsuperstruc,Buerle2020formationofstable,Knippenberg2022roleofcohesionin,Lffler2021behaviordependentc,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Guo2026afeedbackcontrolle}.
Their potential contribution is the
ability to vary attraction-like
coupling, persistence, or response
rules while measuring the resulting
collective state.

However, the feedback loop itself
can introduce effective interactions
and memory. These features should
be included when comparing the
experiment with stochastic-particle
or continuum descriptions.

\paragraph*{Observables and theoretical discrimination.}

Several quantities can distinguish
clustering mechanisms. Cluster-size
distributions characterize the
organization of aggregates.
Pair-correlation functions describe
spatial structure. Density profiles
can reveal coexistence between
dense and dilute regions.
Time-dependent measurements
can distinguish transient
aggregation from persistent
collective states.

For an experiment claiming MIPS,
the most informative test is
whether the collective density
organization can be predicted
from independently measured
motility and interaction
parameters.

A model that reproduces
one cluster-size distribution
after fitting may be useful,
but it does not establish
a general phase-separation
mechanism.

By contrast, a model that
predicts how clustering
changes when persistence,
density, or confinement
is varied provides
a stronger test.

Finite-size effects are
particularly important.
A dense cluster in a
small arena may be
maintained by boundaries
or geometric constraints.
The distinction between
a finite aggregate and
macroscopic phase coexistence
requires appropriate
system-size analysis.

\paragraph*{Strength and limitations of the evidence.}

Among the 62 clustering/MIPS studies,
27 report quantitative theory--experiment
agreement, 24 qualitative agreement,
and eleven no comparison.

For D6, seventeen studies are
rated low risk, 21 have
some concerns, and 24
are high risk.
This indicates a larger
quantitative testing component
than in the flocking category,
but substantial uncertainty
remains.

Replication reporting is
also incomplete. For D1,
nineteen studies are rated
low risk, 42 have some
concerns, and one is
high risk. Independent-run
counts are recorded in
25 studies.

Explicit code availability
is recorded for eight
studies, and data
availability for seven.
All 62 clustering/MIPS
studies were assessed
on a full-text basis.

These results support
moderate confidence in
the existence of
interaction-mediated
clustering across
robotic platforms.
They provide weaker
evidence for a
common phase-separation
mechanism.

The principal limitation
is conceptual as well
as methodological.
Different physical
processes can produce
similar aggregates,
and the extracted
phenomenon label does
not distinguish
them automatically.

We therefore conclude
that robotic systems
have established
multiple routes
to collective
clustering, but
general claims
about motility-induced
phase separation
require more
specific evidence.

Future experiments
should compare
clustering mechanisms
under controlled
conditions, measure
single-agent and
pairwise dynamics,
and test predictions
across density,
persistence, and
system size.

Such comparisons
would turn the
diversity of
robotic clustering
experiments into
a direct opportunity
to discriminate
between competing
theories.

\subsection{Swarming and vortices}
\label{sec:phenomena-swarming}

Swarming and vortex formation describe collective motion in which
agents remain spatially associated while exhibiting coordinated
translation, circulation, or persistent changes in direction.
These states differ from simple polar flocking. A flock may have
a common direction of motion, whereas a vortex can possess
substantial angular momentum even when its net polarization
is small. Consequently, the same visual impression of
collective coherence may correspond to different order
parameters and physical mechanisms.

A useful distinction is between translational and rotational
collective motion. For an assembly with center of mass
$\mathbf R$ and particle positions $\mathbf r_i$, we may
define an instantaneous measure of collective rotation as
\begin{equation}
L_z =
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[
(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf R)
\times
(\mathbf v_i-\mathbf V)
\right]_z ,
\label{eq:phenomena-angular-momentum}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf V$ is the center-of-mass velocity.
This quantity measures circulation about the moving
collective center. It must not be confused with
intrinsic rotation of an individual agent.

The extracted swarming or vortex category contains
11 studies. Six involve robotic micro- and nano-agents,
four involve wheeled or legged robots, and one involves
feedback-controlled active colloids. The category is
therefore smaller than the broader classes of flocking,
clustering, or collective transport.

The microscopic studies include field-driven and
reconfigurable assemblies in which collective
configuration and motion are coupled
\cite{Tural2025dropletbasedmicrof,Chen2023nanorobotmediateds,Hanson2025electrokineticpropu,Liu2026magneticcompartment,Wang2022reconfigurablemagne,Yu2019reconfigurablecollo}.
The reported observables include translational and
angular velocities, collective dimensions, particle
separations, and geometrical descriptors of the
resulting assemblies.

These measurements establish different aspects
of collective motion. Angular velocity describes
rotation, while cluster dimensions and relative
particle positions characterize the organization
of the moving group. Their simultaneous
measurement is useful because an externally
driven assembly can rotate without exhibiting
a self-organized vortex in the sense of
a collective instability.

In microscopic systems, common external forcing
requires particular attention. Magnetic or
electric fields may produce correlated motion
because the agents experience similar driving
conditions. Interparticle interactions may
then determine whether the resulting motion
remains coherent or reorganizes the assembly.



Macroscopic robotic experiments provide
a complementary approach
\cite{Vega2023simulatelessexpec,Zhou2025gazingpreferencein,Zhu2025amillingswarmofg,Abpeikar2023iterativetransferl}.
These studies include implementations
of collective milling, configuration-dependent
motion, and controlled swarm behavior.
Their observables cover quantities such as
turning rate, group geometry, relative motion,
and measures of circular organization.



Feedback-controlled active colloids permit
a more direct connection between collective
rotation and response measurements
\cite{Lffler2021behaviordependentc}.
The extracted observables include a rotational
order parameter, autocorrelation, relaxation
time, susceptibility, and hysteresis.
These quantities are especially informative
because they characterize not only the
existence of collective circulation but
also its response and temporal stability.

For example, a susceptibility peak may
identify a regime of enhanced response.
A hysteresis loop can reveal history
dependence under parameter variation.
Neither automatically establishes
a particular phase transition, but
both constrain possible theoretical
descriptions more strongly than
a trajectory plot alone.

Across the 11 studies, four report
quantitative theoretical agreement,
three qualitative agreement, and
four no comparison. The D6 ratings
are low risk for two studies,
some concerns for two, and
high risk for seven.

Thus, the evidence is sufficient
to establish that several robotic
implementations can realize
coordinated circulation or
swarming-like collective motion.
It is substantially weaker
for identifying a general
mechanism of vortex formation
across those implementations.

Replication documentation is also
limited. Two studies receive
low-risk D1 ratings, eight
have some concerns, and one
is high risk. Independent-run
counts are reported in three
studies. None has explicit
code or data availability
recorded in the extraction.
All 11 were assessed on
a full-text basis.

We therefore regard the existence
of experimentally realized
swarming and milling states
as established within the
reported platforms, but their
cross-platform theoretical
equivalence remains uncertain.

\subsection{Jamming and crystallization}
\label{sec:phenomena-jamming}

Jamming and crystallization concern the relationship
between microscopic constraints and collective
organization. In a jammed system, local interactions
can suppress motion or prevent rearrangements.
In a crystallized system, particle positions
develop spatial order. These properties may
coexist, but neither necessarily implies
the other.

For active matter, the distinction becomes
particularly interesting because energy
continues to enter the system. A driven
assembly may become mechanically constrained,
while individual constituents remain active.
Alternatively, persistent motion may promote
rearrangement and allow an ordered structure
to emerge.

Consequently, we must distinguish dynamical
arrest, geometric packing, and structural
order. A collective that stops moving
has not necessarily crystallized.
Likewise, an ordered configuration
need not be mechanically jammed.

The extracted category contains ten studies.
Five use wheeled or legged swarm robots,
three use vibration-driven robots,
one involves a robotic metamaterial
or active solid, and one belongs
to the residual platform category.

The relevant observables include
packing fraction, collision frequency,
mean-squared displacement,
pair correlations, structural
order parameters, transport
throughput, and measures
of jam formation or escape.
These measurements address
different aspects of the
relationship between activity
and constrained motion.

A useful example of spatial order
is the sixfold bond-orientational
parameter,
\begin{equation}
\psi_6 =
\left|
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\frac{1}{n_i}
\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}
e^{6\mathrm{i}\theta_{ij}}
\right|,
\label{eq:phenomena-hexatic}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal N_i$ denotes the
neighbors of particle $i$ and
$\theta_{ij}$ their bond orientation.
This quantity is relevant to
local sixfold ordering, but
a nonzero value alone
does not establish
long-range crystalline order.

Likewise, dynamical arrest
can be investigated through
mean-squared displacement
and time-dependent correlations.
The appropriate conclusion
depends on the temporal
and spatial scales accessible
to the experiment.

\paragraph*{Physical constraints and active rearrangement.}

Vibration-driven systems provide
a direct experimental route
to active jamming because
particle motion is generated
mechanically while contacts
constrain rearrangements.
The included studies address
diffusive motion, collective
ordering, and jam formation
\cite{Rozenblit2020diffusivedynamicsa,Dmitriev2021statisticalcorrelat,Rozenblit2025trafficjamformatio}.

Their extracted observables include
mean-squared displacement,
diffusion exponents, spatial
ordering, pair correlations,
and measures of jamming
efficiency and mobility.
Together, these quantities
connect transport to
collective structure.

The important physical question
is whether activity changes
the onset or stability
of a constrained state.
For example, increasing
driving may enhance
particle rearrangements
in one regime but
increase collisions
and effective crowding
in another.



Robotic assemblies with
programmable interactions
provide different tests.
The included studies cover
large-scale programmable
aggregation, mechanically
constrained collective

\cite{Linevich2016robophysicalstudyo,Xiao2023perceptionofmotion,Liu2026noiseenabledgoala,Guzzi2013humanfriendlyrobot,Rubenstein2014programmableselfas}.

Some of the extracted observables
measure collective arrangement,
while others characterize
collision rates, escape times,
or task-related consequences
of restricted motion.
Their interpretation depends
on whether the measured
quantity directly establishes
a physical jammed state.



The residual platform category
includes an experiment involving
flow-mediated interaction and
collective constraints
\cite{Newbolt2024flowinteractionsle}.
Such systems illustrate that
congestion or restricted
motion can arise through
environment-mediated
coupling as well as
through direct contact.

\paragraph*{Active structures and entanglement.}

An additional realization occurs
in programmable active structures
\cite{Rezanejad2025programmableentangl}.
The extracted measurements include
packing fraction, entanglement-related
quantities, and escape or
transport statistics.

This broadens the meaning of
jamming beyond the loss of
mobility in a crowd of
independently translating
agents. Mechanical connectivity,
entanglement, and geometric
constraints may themselves
become collective variables.



We therefore treat the
combined extraction category
as evidence of constrained
collective dynamics and
spatial ordering, rather
than as evidence that
every included study
demonstrates both
jamming and crystallization.

\paragraph*{Strength and limitations of the evidence.}

Of the ten studies, four report
quantitative agreement,
two qualitative agreement,
and four no comparison.
For D6, three receive
low-risk ratings,
three have some concerns,
and four are high risk.

The relatively high proportion
of quantitative comparisons
is encouraging, although
the total evidence base
is small. More importantly,
the experiments examine
different quantities:
structural order,
collective congestion,
mechanical trapping,
and restricted transport.

These outcomes should not
be combined into one
claim about the existence
of a common active
jamming transition.

Replication indicators
are comparatively favorable.
Seven studies are rated
low risk for D1,
and three have
some concerns.
Independent-run counts
are reported in seven
studies. Explicit
code and data
availability are each
recorded for one study.
All ten records were
assessed on full text.



A decisive comparison
would measure structural
order, transport,
relaxation, and
mechanical response
within the same
controlled experiment.
Such measurements
could establish whether
changes in mobility
coincide with
structural organization
or arise through
different mechanisms.

Finite-size variation
would be particularly
valuable. Robotic
platforms can sometimes
change the number
of agents while
preserving their
interaction rules.
This offers a direct
way to distinguish
small-system congestion
from collective
behavior approaching
a macroscopic limit.

\subsection{Chirality and rotation}
\label{sec:phenomena-chirality}

Chirality introduces a preferred handedness into
active motion or interaction. Individual agents
may rotate persistently, interact asymmetrically,
or generate collective states with a preferred
direction of circulation. Such systems provide
a useful test of how microscopic symmetry
properties influence macroscopic organization.

We must distinguish intrinsic particle rotation
from collective rotation. A population of
identically rotating particles need not
form a circulating collective. Conversely,
a group may develop collective angular
motion even when the individual agents
have no prescribed intrinsic chirality.

Similarly, a clockwise or counterclockwise
collective trajectory does not by itself
demonstrate broken chiral symmetry.
The direction may be imposed by
a controller, an external field,
or the experimental geometry.

The stronger physical questions concern
how rotational dynamics couple to
translation, spatial structure,
and collective response. Relevant
observables include angular velocity,
rotational order, orientation
distributions, angular correlations,
and response under changes
in driving or interaction asymmetry.

The extracted chirality or rotation
category contains 55 studies.
Robotic micro- and nano-agents
contribute 14, wheeled or legged
robots 15, vibration-driven
robots nine, feedback-controlled
colloids seven, residual
platforms six, and modular
robots three. One additional
study has no assigned
platform class.

This distribution is relatively
broad. It includes microscopic
field-driven systems, mechanically
driven active particles,
programmable collective motion,
and feedback-controlled
colloidal assemblies.

The range of implementations
is valuable because rotational
dynamics can arise through
different microscopic mechanisms.
Yet, this same diversity
prevents a single interpretation
of the combined phenomenon label.

\paragraph*{Rotating microscopic assemblies.}

Micro- and nano-agent systems provide
several ways to control rotational
motion and chiral collective states.
The included studies investigate
collective reconfiguration,
magnetic or optical actuation,
and coupling between translation
and rotation
\cite{Sun2021swarmingmicrodrople,MayorgaMartinez2023multimodaldrivenma,McGovern2024multifunctionalchir,Xu2023multiplecilialike,Gong2025collectivereconfigu,Chen2022programmablespatio,Aubret2021metamachinesofplur,Gao2025softmagneticmicror,Aubret2018targetedassemblyan,Raghavendra2026programmablepersist,Boudehane2026physicsbasedreduce,Wang2021precisecontrolofm,Dong2020controllingtwodime,Li2022vectorcontrolledwh}.

Their observables include
rotational frequency,
angular displacement,
collective geometry,
orientation, and
transport-related
measurements. These
quantities can characterize
the relationship between
individual actuation
and collective motion.

For example, a field-driven
assembly may rotate because
the external drive
directly supplies a torque.
Interparticle interactions
may then stabilize a
particular spatial
configuration or modify
the rotational response.

A different mechanism
would arise if the
collective spontaneously
selected a rotation
direction despite
symmetric external
conditions. These
possibilities are
physically distinct,
even when the resulting
trajectories look similar.

The extracted observables
therefore need to be
interpreted relative to
the actuation protocol.
Measurements of isolated
particles, pairs, and
larger assemblies can
help determine which
aspects of rotation
depend on interactions.

\paragraph*{Feedback-mediated chirality.}

Feedback-controlled colloids
provide a particularly useful
route toward quantitative
tests of collective rotational
dynamics

These studies include
measurements of rotational
order, angular velocity,
orientation distributions,
collective configuration,
and response to controlled
changes in interaction
or driving.

The important experimental
advantage is that the
programmed interaction
can, in principle,
be varied without
changing the particles
themselves. This
permits more direct
tests of how rotational
behavior depends on
interaction symmetry.

Yet, a feedback controller
may explicitly impose
a preferred rotation
direction. Demonstrating
such a state establishes
programmable chirality,
not necessarily
spontaneous chiral
symmetry breaking.

To distinguish these
cases, we need
measurements of state
selection and response
under controlled
reversal or removal
of the imposed bias.
Temporal correlations
and switching statistics
are especially useful
when several rotating
states are accessible.

\paragraph*{Mechanical and programmed rotation.}

Vibration-driven systems
provide examples of rotational
motion generated through
mechanical actuation and
contact-mediated dynamics
\cite{Xi2024beamsbotsandbey,Li2022chiralseparationof,Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove,Ma2025simplerobotswarmw,Noirhomme2024brainbotsassmarta,Callegari2024activematterexperi,Li2013asymmetricgearrect,Bashkova2026differentialequatio,He2022dynamicbehaviorof}.

Here, geometry, substrate
interaction, and driving
can influence the
relationship between
individual rotation
and collective movement.

The scientific opportunity
is to investigate
whether chiral motion
produces robust collective
states through physical
interactions alone.
The limitation is
that changes in
driving may also
alter translational
speed, collision
statistics, and
effective fluctuations.

For this reason,
experiments should
characterize both
linear and angular
single-agent dynamics.
Otherwise, differences
between collective
states may be
incorrectly attributed
to interaction
chirality.

Wheeled or legged
systems approach
rotational collective
behavior through
programmable interactions
and local response rules
\cite{Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,Paramanick2024emergenceofrunand,Vega2023indirectswarmcontr,Zhou2024anticipationpromote,Zheng2024bodyorientationcha,Castro2025visualcollectivebe,Jianchao2024emergentchiralitya,Dizenhaus2026anonymousleadership,Beattie2025realizingemergentc,Zheng2024emergenceofcollect,Raoufi2023individualityinswa,Zheng2020experimentalcapabil,Makino2004collectivebehavior,Fruchart2020phasetransitionsin,Li2020selfpropulsiononsa}.

These experiments make
information asymmetry,
turning rules, and
collective orientation
accessible to controlled
variation. They are
particularly useful
for testing how
nonreciprocal interactions
modify collective motion.

However, the presence
of programmed asymmetry
does not establish
the quantitative
validity of a
nonreciprocal
active-matter theory.
The decisive comparison
concerns predicted
collective observables,
their stability,
and their response
across parameter regimes.

Other implementations
investigate rotational
and chiral behavior
through specialized
mechanical or driven
systems
\cite{Li2024memoryinducedspont,Hu2026phototunablenonreci,Deng2022lightfuelednonreci,Zhou2025activesearchpromot,Ren2025chlamydomonasinspir,Leech2026spincoupledhydrody,Smith2025loopymovementseme,Wang2026oscillatingeelrobo,ICHIKAWA2004emergentbehaviorso,Fruchart2021nonreciprocalphase}.
The unclassified record
in this group is retained
without inferring a

\paragraph*{Strength and limitations of the evidence.}

Among the 55 studies,
20 report quantitative
theoretical agreement,
26 qualitative agreement,
and nine no comparison.
Thus, quantitative
comparisons account
for a substantial
minority of the

The D6 assessment
rates 16 studies
low risk, 24 as

and 15 high risk.
This profile is

and experimental

Replication documentation
remains less complete.
Nine studies are
rated low risk for D1,

and three are high risk.
Independent-run counts
are reported in

and data availability
in five.



The cross-platform

the experimental
realizability of
chiral and rotational
collective motion.
However, the variety
of driving mechanisms
makes it difficult
to identify a common

from the phenomenon

Particularly useful
would be experiments
that reverse microscopic

preserving other

One could then ask
whether collective

Such tests would
help distinguish

They would also

collective states.

\subsection{Active solids and odd elasticity}
\label{sec:phenomena-active-solids}

Active solids extend collective active-matter
physics from transport and spatial organization
to mechanical response. In these systems,
internally generated forces or programmed
actuation can change how a material
deforms, propagates disturbances,
or responds to externally imposed
loads.

A particularly important possibility
is nonreciprocal elastic response.
In passive equilibrium elasticity,
the constitutive relation between
stress and strain is constrained
by the existence of an elastic
energy under the usual assumptions.
Active systems can violate these
constraints because their forces
need not derive from a conservative
potential
\cite{Scheibner2020odd}.

For a linearized system, we may write
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}u_{kl},
\label{eq:phenomena-active-elasticity}
\end{equation}
where $u_{kl}$ is strain and
$C_{ijkl}$ the effective
response tensor. In an
active solid, the
constitutive coefficients
may contain components
that cannot be derived
from an equilibrium
elastic energy.

The experimentally decisive
question is whether such
components can be measured,
rather than simply whether
the structure undergoes
active motion.

The extracted active-solid
or odd-elasticity category
contains 14 studies.
Seven use robotic metamaterials
or active solids, four use
vibration-driven robots,
one uses a modular system,
one a wheeled or legged
robotic platform, and
one belongs to the
residual category.

The reported observables
include stiffness matrices,
elastic moduli,
deformation fields,
vibrational spectra,
mechanical susceptibility,
strain correlations,
and collective
structural organization.

These measurements differ
fundamentally from
trajectory-based order
parameters. They permit
the experimental system
to be characterized
through its response
to perturbations.
Consequently, they
provide a direct route
to quantitative tests
of active mechanical
theory.

\paragraph*{Nonreciprocal response in robotic structures.}

Robotic metamaterials
provide controlled
realizations of
mechanically coupled
active degrees of freedom
\cite{Baconnier2022tensioncontrolledc,Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Du2026metamaterialsthatl,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe,Coulais2025activemetamaterials,Chen2020selfsensingmetamat}.

These studies investigate
different aspects of
active mechanical
response, including
nonreciprocal coupling,
oscillatory dynamics,
programmable response,
and collective deformation.

The principal strength
of such platforms is
that response can be
measured directly.
Rather than inferring
an interaction law
from collective
trajectories alone,
the experimenter
can impose a
perturbation and
measure the resulting
mechanical response.

This makes it possible
to test predicted
relationships between
actuation, deformation,
and propagation.
For example, a
frequency-dependent
transmission measurement
can reveal modes
that are enhanced
or suppressed by
active coupling.

Similarly, a stiffness
matrix can be examined
for asymmetry, while
the eigenvalue spectrum
can characterize the
dynamics of a coupled
active structure.

Yet, mechanical
nonreciprocity can
also result from
explicitly programmed
controllers. Such
an implementation
provides a physical
realization of
nonreciprocal response,
but a stronger
theoretical test
requires predictions
for observables
that were not
directly imposed.

The distinction
is especially
important when
the same model
determines the
control architecture
and describes the
experimental results.
Agreement may then
reflect successful
implementation,
rather than
independent validation
of a general
constitutive law.

\paragraph*{Active ordering and mechanically driven media.}

Vibration-driven
systems provide another
route toward active
solid-like behavior
\cite{Antonov2025selfsustainedfrict,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Wagner2026geometryinducedtra}.

Their extracted observables
include spatial
correlations,
bond-orientational order,
collective motion,
and measures of
mechanical response
or structural
organization.

Such systems are
particularly interesting
because local propulsion
and interactions
can create organized
collective states
without requiring
every constituent
to participate
in an explicit
elastic controller.

However, structural
order and elasticity
must be distinguished.
An assembly may
exhibit strong
bond-orientational
correlations without
having a solid-like
mechanical response
over the relevant
time scales.

Conversely, a connected
active structure
may support
mechanical response
without displaying
conventional crystalline
order. The two
possibilities should
not be merged
into one category
of physical behavior
merely because both
involve constrained
collective motion.

Additional experiments
extend the phenomenon
toward programmed
modular structures,
field-driven robots,
and specialized
active systems
\cite{Zhang2026anisotropicshapesh,Wang2021emergentfielddrive,Bo2026threephasesofodd}.

These systems illustrate
the need for comparisons
based on mechanical
observables rather
than platform
appearance alone.

\paragraph*{Strength and limitations of the evidence.}

Seven studies report
quantitative theoretical
agreement, five
qualitative agreement,
and one no comparison.
The agreement field
is missing for
one additional record.

For D6, six studies
are rated low risk,
six have some concerns,
and two are high risk.
This is a comparatively
favorable distribution
for a small and
specialized phenomenon
family.

Nevertheless, the
replication evidence
is substantially weaker.
Only one study receives
a low-risk D1 rating,
while 13 have
some concerns.
Independent-run counts
are reported for
two studies.

Explicit code availability
is identified in four
studies, and data
availability in six.
Two studies were
assessed from abstracts
alone, limiting
the detail available
for evaluating their
experimental response.

These indicators suggest
a promising but still
developing evidence base.
The available experiments
demonstrate that robotic
structures can realize
active mechanical
responses and permit
quantitative comparisons.
They do not yet establish
that a broad range
of active-solid
predictions has been
independently reproduced
across multiple
experimental platforms.

The central next step
is to standardize
mechanical characterization.
A useful experimental
program would report
response matrices,
frequency dependence,
boundary conditions,
and repeated measurements
under controlled
actuation.

Comparisons across
different structures
would then establish
which properties
depend on microscopic
implementation and
which follow from
a common active
constitutive description.

In particular, direct
measurements of
nonreciprocal response
under cyclic loading
could distinguish
genuinely active
mechanical work
from ordinary
dissipation or
controller-specific
motion.

We therefore regard
robotic active solids
as an emerging area
with substantial
theoretical relevance
and encouraging
quantitative evidence.
Its present limitation
is not the absence
of interesting
mechanical phenomena,
but the limited
number of independently
replicated tests
of general
active-solid laws.

\subsection{Transport and locomotion}
\label{sec:phenomena-transport}

Transport is a particularly broad outcome of active-matter
experiments. A collection of driven agents may carry objects,
move coherently through its environment, or generate a net
displacement that cannot be achieved by its constituents
individually. Such behavior connects the physics of
nonequilibrium motion to practical robotic functionality.
Yet, successful transport is not necessarily evidence
for a collective physical mechanism.

We therefore distinguish collective transport from
individual propulsion and coordinated navigation.
The relevant physical question is how interactions
modify displacement, mobility, spreading, or
mechanical performance. A group of independently
moving robots can achieve a transportation task
without exhibiting an emergent collective state.
Conversely, a mechanically coupled assembly may
locomote even though none of its constituent
components possesses an independently controllable
translation mechanism.

A useful observable is the center-of-mass displacement,
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf R(t)=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[\mathbf r_i(t)-\mathbf r_i(0)\right].
\label{eq:phenomena-transport}
\end{equation}
The corresponding mean-squared displacement is
\begin{equation}
M(t)=
\left\langle
\left|\mathbf R(t)-\mathbf R(0)\right|^2
\right\rangle .
\label{eq:phenomena-transport-msd}
\end{equation}
These quantities characterize collective translation
and spreading. However, they do not by themselves
establish that the motion arises from interactions.
For this purpose, we also require comparisons with
isolated agents, changes in group composition, or
measurements of the forces and correlations
responsible for collective displacement.

Transport or locomotion is among the most frequently
reported phenomena. In the corrected extraction,
198 records carry this label. Robotic micro- and
nano-agents contribute the largest group,
followed by wheeled or legged robots,
vibration-driven systems, other implementations,
and modular or self-reconfigurable assemblies.
Smarticles, feedback-controlled colloids,
and active solids also contribute.
The reported transport total is calculated
directly from the 489-study evidence base.

\subsection{Synchronization}
\label{sec:phenomena-synchronization}

Synchronization occurs when interacting agents
develop correlated temporal dynamics.
The collective may coordinate oscillations,
orientation changes, periodic actuation,
or repeated mechanical motion.
Unlike flocking, which primarily concerns
spatial orientation and translation,
synchronization concerns the relationship
between dynamical phases or temporal responses.

For agents with a well-defined phase
$\phi_i(t)$, a conventional measure
of synchronization is
\begin{equation}
Z(t)=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_i(t)},
\qquad
R(t)=|Z(t)|.
\label{eq:phenomena-sync}
\end{equation}
Here, $R=1$ corresponds to complete
phase alignment, while lower values
indicate less coherent phases.
The interpretation assumes that a
meaningful phase can be assigned
to each constituent
\cite{Acebron2005kuramoto}.

This assumption is important.
Coordinated motion does not
automatically establish
oscillator synchronization.
Similarly, externally imposed
simultaneous commands can
make agents move together
without demonstrating
interaction-mediated
phase locking.

The extraction contains
66 synchronization-labelled
studies. Wheeled or legged
robots contribute 23,
other platforms 16,
micro- and nano-agents 12,
modular robots seven,
and vibration-driven
robots five. Active solids,
feedback-controlled colloids,
and unclassified platforms
contribute one each.

The breadth of implementations
shows that temporal coordination
is relevant to several
experimental regimes.
Nevertheless, the underlying
interactions differ substantially,
and the extracted category
does not guarantee that
every study investigates
a system of coupled
autonomous oscillators.

\subsection{Self-assembly and shape formation}
\label{sec:phenomena-self-assembly}

Self-assembly and shape formation concern the
emergence or controlled modification of collective
geometry. Interacting agents may form compact
clusters, extended structures, chains, lattices,
or configurations with prescribed boundaries.
In active systems, these structures need not
minimize a passive interaction energy.
They may instead be sustained by continuous
driving, feedback, or nonequilibrium
mechanical interactions.

The phenomenon is particularly important
for robotic active matter because
programmability can be used to modify
both assembly pathways and stable
collective configurations. Yet,
the ability to construct a desired
shape does not automatically
demonstrate self-organization
in the physical sense.

We distinguish spontaneous assembly
from externally prescribed formation.
In the former, local interactions
generate collective structure
without direct specification
of every agent's final position.
In the latter, a controller
may explicitly guide agents
toward a predefined geometry.

Both are experimentally relevant.
However, their implications
for active-matter theory differ.
An assembly algorithm can
demonstrate robust distributed
construction, whereas a
spontaneous structural transition
can provide evidence for
collective organization arising
from microscopic interactions.

\subsection{Boundary accumulation}
\label{sec:phenomena-boundaries}

Boundaries are not passive details of an active-matter
experiment. They can modify particle motion,
generate persistent accumulation, and change
the stability of collective states.
In contrast to an equilibrium gas,
an active population may develop
strong near-wall density variations
because persistent propulsion
continually drives particles
toward confining surfaces
\cite{Bechinger2016active}.



The physical interpretation
therefore requires comparison
between boundary geometry,
single-agent motion,
and the resulting
spatial distribution.
Useful observables include
density profiles,
residence times,
wall-contact statistics,
orientation distributions,
and transport
near boundaries.

The extracted boundary-accumulation

Vibration-driven robots

Wheeled or legged
robots contribute 17,
micro- and nano-agents 14,
other systems four,
and feedback-controlled
colloids and active
metamaterials one each.

This distribution is
particularly informative
because the experiments
span contact-mediated,
programmed, and
field-driven interactions.
Yet, their common
boundary-accumulation label
does not establish
a common mechanism.

\paragraph*{Mechanically driven accumulation.}

Vibration-driven robots

platform-specific
characterization.

\paragraph*{Evidence and open questions.}

Among the 65 studies,
29 report quantitative
theory comparison,
28 qualitative comparison,
and eight no comparison.
The D6 ratings are
low risk for 17 studies,
some concerns for 27,
and high risk for 21.

This is a comparatively
substantial quantitative
evidence base for a
phenomenon whose
mechanisms can often
be characterized
through spatial
measurements.

Nevertheless, replication
documentation is incomplete.
Fifteen studies receive
low-risk D1 ratings,
48 have some concerns,
and two are high risk.
Independent-run counts
are recorded in 20 studies.
Explicit code availability
occurs in ten,
and data availability
in 13.







Such experiments
could distinguish
persistent-motion
accumulation from
mechanical trapping,
programmed wall
responses, and
field-mediated
effects.

\subsection{Cross-phenomenon synthesis}
\label{sec:phenomena-cross}

The phenomenon taxonomy provides a broader view
than the platform taxonomy alone.
It reveals that related collective patterns
can be realized through substantially different
microscopic interactions. Yet, this observation
does not imply that those systems belong
to the same physical universality class.

The distinction between a phenomenon and
its mechanism is therefore central
to interpreting the evidence.

Flocking, clustering, collective transport,
synchronization, and self-assembly
have all been realized through
programmable robotic interactions.
This establishes an important
experimental capability:
we can construct physical
systems in which selected
microscopic rules generate
measurable collective behavior.



For flocking, these
include fluctuations,
spatial correlations,
and the dependence
of order on system
size and noise.
For clustering,
they include density
distributions,
coexistence,
and the dependence
of collective motion
on local crowding.
For synchronization,
they include phase
relationships,
frequency locking,
and relaxation
under perturbation.

Similarly, vortex
formation should
be distinguished
from prescribed
circular movement.
Chiral collective
response should
be distinguished
from externally
imposed rotation.
And shape formation
should be distinguished
from a controller
that assigns every
agent a target
position.

These differences
are not merely
terminological.
They determine
what a robotic
experiment can
establish about
active-matter
physics.

\paragraph*{What is reproduced across platforms?}

The evidence is strongest for the experimental
existence of collective motion, transport,
spatial organization, and coordinated
shape formation. These behaviors appear
across multiple physical realizations
with different propulsion and
interaction mechanisms.

However, the extracted platform
labels do not identify
independent research groups
in a normalized form.
First-author names,
venues, and study
counts cannot reliably
establish independence.
We therefore do not
equate cross-platform
coverage with a
numerical count of
independent replications.

A further distinction
concerns theoretical
specificity. Observing
clustering in several
systems supports
the general conclusion
that active collectives
can aggregate.
It does not
independently confirm
motility-induced
phase separation
in every system.

Likewise, collective
rotation in magnetic
microrobots,
vibration-driven particles,
and programmed
robot swarms
does not establish
a common mechanism
of spontaneous
chiral symmetry
breaking.

The physical
interpretation becomes
stronger when comparable
observables can be
measured across
platforms and
related to a common
theoretical prediction.

\paragraph*{The role of quantitative observables.}

The review's most important methodological
finding is that the presence of a collective
pattern and the quality of its theoretical
explanation must be assessed separately.

For example, polarization provides
a common observable for
collective directional order,
while mean-squared displacement
can characterize transport
and relaxation.
Pair correlations,
cluster statistics,
and density profiles
provide information
about spatial
organization.
Response matrices
and vibrational
spectra characterize
mechanical behavior.





We therefore place
greater weight on
experiments that
report quantitative
comparisons,
independent repetitions,
and explicit
characterization
of boundaries
and finite-size
effects.



\paragraph*{Experimental robustness and reporting.}

Across the phenomenon families,
risk-of-bias assessments show
that low-risk quantitative
theory tests remain
a subset of the
available experimental
literature.

The evidence is
particularly heterogeneous
for broad families
such as flocking,
transport, and
shape formation.
These categories
contain many studies
with qualitative
comparisons or
no recorded
theory comparison.

More specialized
phenomena, including
active elasticity
and certain forms
of chiral response,
contain comparatively
high proportions
of quantitative
comparisons.
However, their
smaller evidence
bases and limited
replication reporting
restrict the generality
of conclusions.

A separate limitation
arises from missing
experimental-run counts.
Where repetition
information is absent,
we cannot establish
how precisely an
observable was measured
or how robustly
a reported state
was reproduced.
Missing reporting
is not proof
of absent replication,
but it limits
independent assessment.

The same applies
to code and data
availability.
A complete controller
description,
experimental trajectory
dataset, and
analysis implementation
would permit stronger
verification of
the reported physical
conclusions.

For feedback-mediated
systems, these materials
are particularly important
because the controller
forms part of the
effective interaction.
For mechanically driven
systems, calibration
and particle-level
measurements are
similarly important.

\paragraph*{From demonstration to discriminating experiment.}

The next stage of robotic active-matter
research should use programmability
to distinguish physical mechanisms
rather than merely realize
new collective patterns.

We see three particularly useful
forms of comparison.

The first is the controlled
comparison between isolated
agents and interacting
collectives. It establishes
which changes in dynamics
can be attributed to
interactions rather
than the propulsion
of individual agents.

The second is systematic
variation of microscopic
interaction parameters.
Such experiments can
distinguish competing
predictions and reveal
whether apparent
collective transitions
depend on a specific
control implementation.

The third is cross-platform
replication with comparable
observables. If a predicted
relationship survives
changes in propulsion,
sensing, or mechanical
coupling, confidence
in its generality
increases.

These approaches
also clarify the
relationship between
robotics and
active-matter theory.
Robotics provides
control and
experimental access.
Active-matter theory
provides predictions
about collective
states, fluctuations,
response, and
nonequilibrium
organization.

Their combination
is most productive
when each constrains
the other.

In particular,
a theoretical model
should not be regarded
as validated merely
because its
interaction rule
can be implemented.
Nor should an
unexpected collective
pattern be interpreted
as a new physical
phase without
the necessary
quantitative evidence.



The following
theory section
examines this
distinction directly:
which models have
been confronted
quantitatively
with robotic
experiments,
which observables
were compared,
and where the
available evidence
supports or
limits the
proposed explanation.
