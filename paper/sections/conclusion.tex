\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

The evidence snapshot dated 2026-10-14 contains 530 studies, of which 358 appeared from 2020 onward. Robotic active matter is therefore no longer a collection of isolated demonstrations. It is a rapidly growing experimental field that connects statistical physics, robotics, microrobotics, and active materials. Yet, its evidence is heterogeneous. Platforms, interaction mechanisms, observables, and standards of theory comparison differ substantially. The main result of this review is consequently not one universal law. It is an account of which conclusions survive these differences.

For objective O1, the evidence establishes robotic active matter as a family of experimental platforms rather than a single apparatus. Wheeled or legged swarms form the largest class with 220 studies. Robotic micro- and nano-agents contribute 85 studies, while vibration-driven robots contribute 58. Smaller classes comprise modular systems, feedback-controlled colloids, smarticles or granular robots, and robotic metamaterials or active solids. Each class exposes different physics. Wheeled robots provide direct control of sensing and communication. Vibration-driven systems reveal contact-dominated dynamics. Micro- and nano-agents expose fluid-mediated and field-mediated coupling. Controlled colloids permit programmable particle interactions. Modular systems and active structures connect collective dynamics to topology and material response. Hence, platform diversity is both the field's experimental strength and its main obstacle to direct comparison.

For objective O2, the evidence shows that collective phenomena recur across robotic embodiments. Self-assembly or shape formation appears in 259 studies, transport or locomotion in 220, flocking or polar order in 156, clustering or motility-induced phase separation in 111, and chirality or rotation in 95. Swarming, synchronization, boundary accumulation, jamming, and active-solid response extend this repertoire. Still, recurrence of a label does not establish recurrence of a mechanism. A cluster can result from attraction, motility reduction, confinement, common forcing, or jamming. A rotating state can be imposed or spontaneously selected. The strongest conclusion is therefore that robotic hardware realizes a broad range of collective states. Claims of universal mechanism require matched observables and controlled comparison across platforms.

For objective O3, theory is widely present but quantitative confrontation remains a minority practice. Quantitative theory--experiment comparison is coded in 134 studies, compared with 244 qualitative comparisons and 129 studies without a comparison. Agent-based simulation provides the broadest bridge between controller, embodiment, and observed dynamics. Kinetic, hydrodynamic, active-Brownian, statistical-mechanical, Vicsek-type, run-and-tumble, and active-elasticity descriptions have smaller experimental bases. What works best is a model tied to a measured interaction and tested against a collective response. Agreement obtained after flexible fitting to one apparatus supports consistency. Independent parameter calibration, competing models, transient prediction, and transfer across regimes support explanation. Hence, future theory tests should be designed around conditions under which plausible models disagree.

For objective O4, programmability is most informative when used as an experimental intervention. On-board rules occur in 300 studies, external feedback in 88, passive or uncontrolled rules in 65, central control in 17, and learned policies in 13; the architecture is unresolved or unreported in 47. No architecture is universally superior. Passive systems expose mechanical sufficiency. On-board rules implement local interactions. External feedback permits precise perturbation. Central control can realize designed couplings, while learning can search a large policy space. The decisive distinction is between producing a state and testing its cause. A controller becomes a physical instrument when it changes one sensing, communication, delay, noise, or coupling parameter and measures the response.

For objective O5, reproducibility remains the principal limitation. Only 188 studies state a number of independent runs. Public code is named in 78 studies and public data in 56. These counts do not show that all remaining experiments are irreproducible. They show that replication, reanalysis, and reconstruction are usually impossible from the reported artifacts alone. Finite size, boundaries, controller timing, parameter provenance, and calibration are also incompletely treated. Furthermore, 34 studies were assessed from abstracts only, and 140 rows contain at least one unresolved field. Conclusions depending on those records remain less secure until full texts or adjudications become available.

Taken together, the five objectives yield one central conclusion. Robotic active matter contributes most when programmability makes a mechanism vulnerable to experiment. A collective pattern demonstrates feasibility. A calibrated perturbation that distinguishes explanations provides physical knowledge. The next advance therefore requires a common experimental logic: characterize isolated agents, measure interactions, vary one mechanism at a time, test beyond the calibration regime, change size and boundaries, and release the path from apparatus to analysis. The living review will track whether these practices spread and whether new evidence changes the present certainty.
