\section{Overview of the evidence base}
\label{sec:results_overview}

The evidence base spans several research traditions that share a physical question: how do interactions among actively driven artificial agents produce collective behavior? Yet, the systems differ substantially in scale, actuation, control, and experimental purpose. We therefore begin with the selection process and the composition of the literature before interpreting its scientific contributions. Throughout this section, counts refer to the evidence snapshot of October 17, 2026.

\subsection{Study selection}
\label{sec:results-selection}

The database searches identified 15\,034 records. OpenAlex contributed 7\,119 records, followed by NASA ADS with 4\,772, PubMed with 983, IEEE Xplore with 901, arXiv with 695, Crossref with 373, HAL with 147, and the Europe PMC bioRxiv index with 44. These are source-level retrieval counts, not independent studies. A publication may occur in several databases.

After removal of 6\,332 duplicates, 8\,702 records entered title and abstract screening. Of these, 7\,423 were excluded, leaving 1\,279 reports sought for retrieval. Full texts could not be obtained for 300 reports. The remaining 979 underwent full-text assessment. The retrieval failures included 179 reports assessed on their abstracts under the protocol amendment permitting abstract-only eligibility decisions.

The recorded full-text exclusions numbered 662. The most frequent reason was the absence of experimental comparison with robotic hardware (E2, 261 reports). Another 224 reports were excluded because they described engineering performance without a qualifying physical collective observable (E3). Non-primary publications accounted for 127 exclusions (E5), single-agent investigations for 32 (E1), synthetic colloids without programmable or feedback control for 16 (E6), and biological-only systems for two (E4).

The substantial contributions of E2 and E3 illustrate the practical importance of the review boundary. Robotic active matter lies between the physics of interacting driven systems and the engineering of coordinated robots. Yet, neither a numerical simulation of collective behavior nor successful coordination of multiple machines establishes an experimental measurement of collective physics. Our eligibility criteria require this additional evidence.

The final included-studies table contains 492 records, corresponding to 492 reported studies. Of these, 30 were included on an abstract-only basis. The selection pathway is displayed in Fig.~\ref{fig:prisma}.

The selection totals reconcile when the two assessment bases are distinguished. Of the 979 reports assessed on full text, 462 were included, 513 were excluded, and four were awaiting a decision. Among the 179 reports assessed on their abstracts because full text could not be obtained, 30 were included and 149 were excluded. Hence, the 662 eligibility exclusions comprise 513 full-text exclusions and 149 abstract-only exclusions, while the final evidence base of 492 studies comprises 462 full-text-based and 30 abstract-only inclusions. The four reports awaiting a decision remain outside the included evidence base. This separation is important because abstract-only assessments carry greater uncertainty about experimental methods and quantitative results.

\subsection{Composition of the evidence base}
\label{sec:results-composition}

The evidence encompasses physical platforms ranging from individually controlled mobile robots to assemblies of microscopic active agents. Each study was assigned one primary platform class. We retain this classification for descriptive statistics while recognizing that it cannot capture every hybrid system.

Wheeled or legged swarm robots form the largest category, with 204 studies (41.5\%). This class includes experiments with programmable mobile agents, local sensing, and distributed or centralized coordination. It provides a direct connection to conventional swarm robotics, but the included reports also address collective observables rather than task performance alone. The class contains studies of rule-based collective motion, interaction-mediated responses, and collective dynamics under programmed control \cite{Miki2010asimplerulebased,Yamada2011circularmotiongene,Chen2013aflockingalgorithm,Wang2021fielddriverobotsw,Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,Yang2023collectivechaseem,Paramanick2024emergenceofrunand,Kernbach2011threecasesofconne,Giardina2022collectivephototact,Vega2023simulatelessexpec,Vega2023indirectswarmcontr}.

A second substantial category comprises robotic micro- and nano-agents, with 77 studies (15.7\%). These experiments extend robotic programmability toward length scales at which magnetic, optical, acoustic, and other actuation mechanisms become important. Their inclusion also emphasizes that a robotic realization need not resemble a conventional autonomous mobile robot. The relevant criterion is experimentally controlled activity combined with a measured interaction or collective response \cite{Sun2021swarmingmicrodrople,Calabr2026interactiondrivena,Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic,Pauer2021programmabledesign,MayorgaMartinez2023multimodaldrivenma,Yigit2019programmablecollect,Das2023optoelectronictraje,Rivas2023spatialpatterningo,McGovern2024multifunctionalchir,Feng2023weakionexchangeba,Sun2024multiplemagnetoopt,Cheng2025selfassemblyandco}.

Vibration-driven robots contribute 63 studies (12.8\%). These platforms offer a different experimental regime because propulsion and fluctuations are closely coupled to the mechanical properties of the agents and substrate. Their extracted reports cover collective responses in mechanically driven robotic systems \cite{Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Welch2019activereorientation,Deblais2019howtoforceanarmy,Xi2024beamsbotsandbey,Balda2022playingwithactive,Xi2024emergentintelligenc,Mohanty2025multimodalmotionan,GuzmnLastra2026emergenceofbeating,Edwards2026robusttopologically,Musacchio2026activeembracemente,Hoek2026pistonlikeinformat,Smith2026fromswarmtoindivi}.

Modular or self-reconfigurable robots account for 23 studies (4.7\%). Smarticles and granular robots contribute another ten (2.0\%). Both categories are relevant to the relationship between individual actuation and collective mechanical response. The modular category includes experiments on reconfiguration, assembly, and coordinated motion \cite{Yang2024selfamputatingand,Hu2026diatominspired1di,Ma2024smarticle20desig,Yi2025reconfigurablerobot,Rogowski2026coordinatedselfass,Zhao2024snailinspiredrobot,Zhang2026anisotropicshapesh,Li2019particleroboticsba,Bhattacharjee2026collectiveassembly,Oliveri2021continuouslearning,Drew2021acousticcommunicati}. The smarticle and granular category includes systems that combine active internal degrees of freedom with collective mechanical interactions \cite{Li2018supersmarticlealo,Bhamla2021collectivetransport,Vardhan2022glidersinshapecha,Savoie2018phototacticsupersma,OzkanAydin2020collectiveprotectio,Luo2025flockingphasesepar,Warkentin2018locomotingrobotsco,Chvykov2020lowrattlingapred,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,Li2020programmingactiveg}.

Feedback-controlled active colloids account for 13 studies (2.6\%). Robotic metamaterials or active solids contribute ten (2.0\%). Although these categories are numerically smaller, they are conceptually important because programmable actuation offers ways to modify interactions and mechanical response. Their presence expands the experimental domain beyond mobile swarms \cite{Martinet2022rotationcontrolin,Yan2014colloidalsuperstruc,Chen2024synchronousandfull,Buerle2020formationofstable,Aubret2021metamachinesofplur,Heuthe2025tunablecolloidalsw,Knippenberg2022roleofcohesionin,Lffler2023collectiveforaging,Han2020programmablechiral,Chen2022collectiveresponse,Guo2026afeedbackcontrolle,Chen2024persistentandrespo,Lffler2021behaviordependentc,Baconnier2022tensioncontrolledc,Zhang2026noiseenhancedtempo,Rezanejad2025programmableentangl,Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Du2026metamaterialsthatl,Brandenbourger2021limitcyclesturnac,Binysh2026moreislessinunpe,Coulais2025activemetamaterials,Chen2020selfsensingmetamat}.

The remaining classification comprises 88 studies (17.9\%) assigned to ``other'' and four studies (0.8\%) without a recorded platform class. This residual category is too large to interpret as a coherent physical platform. It instead indicates a limitation of any finite taxonomy applied to a field with diverse implementations. We preserve these classifications rather than reallocating studies without additional extraction evidence \cite{Zhou2021synchronizedswimmin,Gravish2023coordinatedcollecti,Li2024memoryinducedspont,Lin2024decodingfrequencies,Zion2024amechanicalorigin,Schilling2019learningvisionbase,Brandsttter2022multiagentspatial,Beaver2022aconstraintdriven,Li2025llmflockdecentral,Tan2025goflockgoalorien,Verdoucq2025flockingphasetrans,Lin2025whyorbweavingspid,Choi2026communicationfreec}.

Hence, the platform distribution measures the representation of experimental approaches in the included literature. It does not establish which approach provides stronger evidence for any particular collective mechanism. That requires comparison of observables, theoretical predictions, and experimental quality, as developed in the following sections.

\subsection{Collective size and experimental scale}
\label{sec:results-scale}

The number of interacting agents is an important experimental parameter. It determines which collective observables can be measured and which finite-size effects may arise. Yet, the largest demonstrated collective is not necessarily the most informative experimental system. Small ensembles can isolate interactions, whereas larger assemblies permit the investigation of collective fluctuations, spatial organization, and scaling.

The maximum number of hardware agents was reported for 409 studies (83.1\%). Among all 492 included studies, 216 (43.9\%) had a recorded maximum between two and nine agents, while 156 (31.7\%) reached between ten and 99 agents. Another 24 studies (4.9\%) reached between 100 and 999 agents, and ten (2.0\%) reported maxima of at least 1\,000. Three records (0.6\%) reported a maximum of one agent despite the collective eligibility criterion. These require verification against the original reports. Agent maxima were missing for 83 studies (16.9\%).

The distribution is therefore strongly weighted toward relatively small experimental collectives. Among studies with a reported maximum, the median was eight agents, while the upper quartile was 25. The distribution is also highly skewed: the largest recorded system contained 500\,000 agents, far beyond the typical robotic assembly. This extreme value belongs to a microscopic platform and should not be interpreted as directly comparable to the agent counts of independently operating macroscopic robots \cite{Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic}.

Several studies demonstrate substantially larger collectives, including programmable robot assemblies and microscopic systems \cite{Rubenstein2014programmableselfas,Ferrante2012explicitandimplici,Gauci2017errorcascadesinco,Yang2019statisticsbasedaut,Baconnier2022selectiveandcollec,Fu2023entropybyneighbor,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork,Zhang2026emergentnonhermiti,Ceron2026fluidictorqueenabl}. However, the extracted maximum number of agents describes experimental scale, not necessarily the number of agents contributing to every reported measurement.

Note that finite-size scaling requires more than a large collective. A convincing test must examine how an observable changes with system size while controlling relevant experimental conditions. The available agent-count distribution alone cannot establish which studies meet that requirement. We therefore distinguish collective size from evidence for thermodynamic or continuum behavior in the subsequent synthesis.

\subsection{Publication history and growth}
\label{sec:results-growth}

The included literature extends from 2003 to 2026. The earliest portion of the dataset is dominated by macroscopic robotic platforms. Studies from this period already addressed self-organized motion, physical interactions, and experimental coordination \cite{Hayes2003selforganizedflock,Mizuguchi2004collectivedynamics,Spears2004distributedphysics,Kelly2004ascalableonboard,Belta2004controlofateamof,ICHIKAWA2004smallintelligentro,Turgut2008modelingphasetrans,Navarro2008anapproachtoflock,Christensen2008swarmorphscripta,Turgut2008selforganizedflocka,Hamann2008spatialmacroscopic}.

We observe a marked increase in the number of included studies over the subsequent decades. The dataset contains 40 studies published through 2009, followed by 52 during 2010--2014 and 68 during 2015--2019. By comparison, 213 studies were published during 2020--2024, and another 119 have publication years of 2025 or 2026.

Thus, 332 studies (67.5\%) have publication years from 2020 onward. Annual counts increased from 27 in 2020 to 39 in 2021 and 51 in 2022. The series subsequently records 41 studies in 2023, 55 in 2024, 55 in 2025, and 64 in 2026. The 2026 count must be interpreted as a partial-year snapshot rather than a completed calendar year.

Figure~\ref{fig:growth} shows the annual distribution and its platform composition. The development is not a uniform expansion of a single experimental approach. Instead, the dataset records increasing representation of microscopic systems, vibration-driven platforms, and other specialized realizations alongside conventional mobile robots \cite{Welch2018aggregatesuppressio,Scholz2018rotatingrobotsmove,Brandenbourger2019nonreciprocalrobot,Yigit2019programmablecollect,Xie2020bioinspiredsoftmic,Li2020programmingactiveg,Buerle2020formationofstable,Fruchart2022nonreciprocalphase,Gardi2022microrobotcollectiv,Li2024memoryinducedspont,Xi2024emergentintelligenc,Luo2025flockingphasesepar,Du2026metamaterialsthatl}.

The platform-specific histories reinforce this interpretation. Of the 204 wheeled or legged robot studies, 76 appeared through 2014. In contrast, none of the 77 studies classified as robotic micro- or nano-agents has a publication year before 2015. For vibration-driven robots, only four of 63 studies appeared through 2014, while 25 have publication years of 2025 or 2026.

These changes suggest diversification of experimental realizations within the eligible literature. Yet, they do not by themselves demonstrate an increase in the scientific quality of the evidence. Publication counts are also influenced by database coverage, terminology, preprint indexing, and the review's eligibility criteria. We therefore interpret the growth curve as a description of the assembled evidence, not as a calibrated measure of the entire field's research activity.

\subsection{Publication venues and assessment basis}
\label{sec:results-venues}

The literature is distributed across physics, robotics, materials science, and interdisciplinary venues. The extracted venue field contains 203 distinct nonmissing strings. Some entries represent naming variants or different forms of conference proceedings, so this number should not be interpreted as a normalized count of independent publication venues.

Among the largest exact venue categories are arXiv (Cornell University), with 37 records, Nature Communications and IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, with 20 each, and the recorded IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation proceedings category, with 17. The IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems contributes 15, while APS March Meeting Abstracts contributes 13. Twelve records have no venue recorded.

The coexistence of these publication venues reflects the interdisciplinary character of robotic active matter. Relevant experimental observations appear in robotics research, where control and coordination are central, and in physics and materials research, where collective states, interactions, and mechanical properties are the primary objects of investigation \cite{Vsrhelyi2018optimizedflockingo,Savoie2019arobotmadeofrobo,Boudet2021fromcollectionsof,Berlinger2021implicitcoordinatio,Zion2023morphologicalcomput,Taghavi2024coordinatedbehavior,Ma2026crosslinkcollectiv,Paramanick2025spontaneousemergenc,Deblais2018boundariescontrolc,Jianchao2024emergentchiralitya,Xia2024biomimeticsynchroni,Wang2025hyperuniformnetwork}.

However, venue alone is not evidence of either theoretical rigor or reproducibility. Conference papers may contain detailed hardware validation, while journal articles may emphasize a striking observation without fully characterizing finite-size effects. Consequently, our assessment relies on extracted experimental information and risk-of-bias domains rather than venue prestige.

Finally, 462 studies (93.9\%) were assessed on a full-text basis, and 30 (6.1\%) were assessed from abstracts alone. The abstract-only studies are not distributed uniformly across platform classes. Nine belong to the residual ``other'' category, seven to wheeled or legged robots, five to vibration-driven robots, two each to micro- and nano-agents, smarticles or granular robots, and active solids, and one to modular robots. Two further abstract-only records lack a platform classification. None of the 13 feedback-controlled active-colloid records was assessed on an abstract-only basis.

This distinction is particularly important for claims about theoretical validation and reproducibility. An abstract may establish that a physical experiment was performed and that a collective observable was measured. It may not report enough information to determine independent repetition counts, measurement uncertainty, boundary effects, or the precise quality of agreement with theory. Hence, absence of these details in an abstract cannot be interpreted in the same way as absence from an accessible full report.

\subsection{Implications for the synthesis}
\label{sec:results-implications}

The evidence overview establishes three constraints on the physical interpretation that follows.

First, the literature is heterogeneous in a scientifically meaningful sense. The same collective phenomenon may be realized through sensing and communication, mechanical contact, imposed fields, or programmable interactions. We should therefore ask which physical mechanisms generalize across platforms, rather than assume that similar visual patterns imply identical dynamics.

Second, experimental scale is uneven. Small collectives dominate the records with reported agent counts, while large-scale assemblies occur in a comparatively small subset. We must accordingly distinguish demonstrations of interaction rules from evidence for collective scaling laws. A result observed in a few agents may establish a valid microscopic mechanism without establishing the corresponding continuum limit.

Third, the growth of the literature has been accompanied by diversification of experimental platforms and publication venues. This creates opportunities for independent tests of common theoretical predictions. Yet, diversity alone does not establish reproducibility. For that, we require comparable observables, controlled perturbations, documented repetitions, and quantitative agreement across experimental realizations.

The following sections therefore organize the evidence by platform, collective phenomenon, theoretical comparison, and control mechanism. Our objective is not to rank research communities by publication volume. It is to determine where programmable robotic systems have provided convincing experiments on active matter, where the available evidence remains conditional, and which physical questions can now be addressed more decisively.
