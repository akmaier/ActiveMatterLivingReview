\textbf{Background and objectives.} Robotic active matter makes interaction laws experimentally programmable, but evidence is dispersed across physics, robotics, microrobotics, and active materials. We systematically mapped platforms and collective phenomena, assessed quantitative theory--experiment comparisons, characterized control and programmability, and evaluated reproducibility.

\textbf{Sources, eligibility, and methods.} We searched OpenAlex, Crossref, arXiv, bioRxiv, PubMed, IEEE Xplore, HAL, and NASA ADS, supplemented by Google Scholar, citation chasing, and journal hand-searching. Eligible studies examined at least two interacting, actively driven artificial agents in hardware and reported a quantitative collective or interaction observable. Controlled colloids qualified only with programmable or feedback control; simulation-only and task-performance-only studies were excluded. A three-model panel screened records; two models extracted structured data and a third adjudicated disputes. We synthesized the extracted taxonomy without meta-analysis and assessed six risk-of-bias domains.

\textbf{Results and limitations.} The snapshot dated 2026-10-14 contains 530 studies from 15,034 identified records; 358 studies appeared from 2020 onward. Wheeled or legged swarms were the largest platform class (220 studies). Self-assembly or shape formation (259) and transport or locomotion (220) were the most frequent phenomena. Quantitative theory--experiment comparison occurred in 134 studies, while on-board rules were the most common control architecture (300). Only 188 studies stated independent-run counts; 78 named public code and 56 public data. Thirty-four studies were assessed from abstracts, and 140 records retained at least one unresolved field.

\textbf{Conclusions.} Robotic platforms establish broad collective phenomenology, but transferable physical explanations remain narrower. Programmability contributes most when it isolates and perturbs a mechanism rather than merely producing a state. Living updates will track quantitative tests, cross-platform reproduction, and reproducibility.
